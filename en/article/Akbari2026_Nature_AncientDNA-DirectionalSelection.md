# Ancient DNA reveals pervasive directional selection across West Eurasia

## Citation (NLM)
Akbari A, Perry A, Barton AR, Kariminejad M, Gazal S, Li Z, et al. Ancient DNA reveals pervasive directional selection across West Eurasia. Nature. 2026;654(8109):419-429. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10358-1

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10358-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10358-1)

---

## Summary

Ancient DNA has transformed our understanding of **population history** — migrations, admixture, and demography. Its potential to reveal as much about **human evolutionary biology** has gone largely unrealized, for two reasons. Sample sizes were too small, and, more fundamentally, it is hard to distinguish a **sustained rise in allele frequency caused by increasing fitness** — directional selection — from frequency shifts caused by **migration, population structure, or non-adaptive purifying and stabilizing selection**. An allele can rise simply because the people carrying it migrated in, which is a demographic fact, not an adaptive one.

The authors present a method for detecting **directional selection in ancient DNA time-series data** that tests for **consistent** allele frequency change over time, in a way that is robust to the confounders above. The logic is that migration and structure produce frequency changes tied to specific demographic events and ancestry components, whereas selection produces sustained, directional change within ancestry strata — so conditioning appropriately on ancestry separates the two.

Applied at scale across **West Eurasia**, where ancient DNA sampling is densest and the major demographic transitions of the last ten thousand years are well characterized, the analysis finds that directional selection was **pervasive**. This is a substantive claim about human evolution: rather than being confined to a handful of textbook examples such as lactase persistence and skin pigmentation, adaptive change has been widespread across the genome during the Holocene — the period spanning agriculture, sedentism, new pathogens, new diets, and dramatic population growth.

The methodological point is as important as the biological one. Time-series data are qualitatively better than modern data for detecting selection, because selection is a process in time. Modern-only methods infer past selection from the footprints it leaves in present-day variation, which requires assumptions about demography; ancient DNA lets allele frequencies be **observed at successive dates**, converting inference into measurement.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Demonstrates **natural selection observed directly in humans over time**, rather than inferred from present-day patterns
- Explains the central confounder in selection inference — that **migration and drift mimic selection** — and why controlling for ancestry is essential
- Places lactase persistence and pigmentation in context as examples of a much broader phenomenon, correcting the impression that human adaptation is rare
- Connects population genetics theory to the archaeological and historical record of the Holocene
- A strong methodological example of why **temporal sampling** transforms an inference problem

## Future Research Directions

- Identify the phenotypes under selection for the strongest signals, and determine what environmental change drove each
- Extend the approach to other world regions as ancient DNA sampling densities increase, since West Eurasia is currently unrepresentative
- Test whether selection targeted variants that today contribute to complex disease risk, linking Holocene adaptation to modern health
- Determine the relative contribution of standing variation versus new mutation to the detected selective responses
- Combine directional selection detection with polygenic trait analysis to ask whether whole trait distributions shifted adaptively
