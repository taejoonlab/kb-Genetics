# Intrinsically accelerated cellular degradation is amplified by TDP-43 loss in ALS-vulnerable motor neurons in a zebrafish model

## Citation (NLM)
Asakawa K, Tomita T, Shioya S, Handa H, Saeki Y, Kawakami K. Intrinsically accelerated cellular degradation is amplified by TDP-43 loss in ALS-vulnerable motor neurons in a zebrafish model. Nat Commun. 2025;16:9821. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-65097-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-65097-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-65097-0)

---

## Summary

**Selective neuronal vulnerability** is the defining and least understood feature of neurodegenerative disease. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), motor neurons degenerate while most other neurons are spared, and among motor neurons some classes are far more susceptible than others — large spinal motor neurons are lost early, whereas ocular motor neurons are famously resistant. The question is why a mutation present in every cell kills only a subset.

The authors approached this in zebrafish, exploiting a property the animal has and mammals do not: a **translucent spinal cord** that allows autophagy to be monitored at **single-cell resolution in the living animal**. Rather than comparing diseased to healthy tissue, they measured a basal cellular property across neuron classes in the normal animal, then asked how disease perturbs it.

The baseline result reframes the problem. Motor neurons are **the cell population with the highest autophagic flux** in the spinal cord — before any disease insult. Within motor neurons the gradient tracks vulnerability precisely: **large spinal motor neurons (SMNs), the most ALS-susceptible class, show higher flux than smaller SMNs and than ALS-resistant ocular motor neurons.** Vulnerability is therefore not conferred by disease; it corresponds to a pre-existing cell-intrinsic state.

Large SMNs accelerate **both autophagy and proteasome-mediated degradation** — the two major protein-clearance systems — and both are **further augmented by loss of TDP-43**, the RNA-binding protein whose mislocalization and loss of nuclear function is the defining pathology of most ALS. The authors also observe acceleration of **multiple unfolded protein response pathways**, indicating that these neurons have an innate tendency toward high protein-handling load.

The model that emerges is a ceiling effect. Large motor neurons already run their degradation machinery near capacity as a normal condition of being large, highly active cells with enormous axons. TDP-43 loss adds demand to a system with no remaining headroom, so the same insult that other neurons absorb pushes these past their limit.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Offers a mechanistic answer to **selective vulnerability**: the same mutation kills only some cells because those cells start closer to a limit
- Demonstrates why the **zebrafish** model earns its place — optical transparency permits live single-cell measurement of a dynamic process in an intact spinal cord
- Introduces **autophagy, proteasomal degradation, and the unfolded protein response** as quantitative cellular phenotypes rather than pathway diagrams
- A good example of measuring the **normal** state first: the key insight came from wild-type animals, not from the disease model
- Supports the disease-modelling lecture and complements the *Drosophila* MPS IIIB model in this vault, which also links protein/vesicle handling to neuronal dysfunction

## Future Research Directions

- Test the ceiling hypothesis directly by increasing degradative capacity in large SMNs and asking whether TDP-43 loss becomes tolerable
- Determine what drives the intrinsically high flux — cell size, axon length, firing rate, or metabolic demand — by dissociating these correlated variables
- Ask whether ocular motor neuron resistance can be transferred: does conferring their expression profile on spinal motor neurons protect them?
- Extend to genetic ALS models beyond TDP-43 (*SOD1*, *C9orf72*) to test whether the ceiling model generalizes across causes
- Evaluate whether the same flux gradient exists in human motor neurons, using patient-derived or postmortem material
