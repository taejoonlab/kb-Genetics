# Genomic history of early dogs in Europe

## Citation (NLM)
Bergström A, Furtwängler A, Johnston S, Rosengren E, Breidenstein A, Booth T, et al. Genomic history of early dogs in Europe. Nature. 2026;651(8097):986-996. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10112-7

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10112-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10112-7)

---

## Summary

The dog was the **first domesticated animal**, and the only one domesticated before agriculture — a hunter-gatherer domestication. Because dogs accompanied people, dog population history is a proxy for human population history, and the two can be compared to ask whether dogs moved with the people who kept them. Modern dog genomes cannot resolve this: intensive recent breed formation, with severe bottlenecks and extensive crossing, has overwritten most of the earlier population structure. Recovering the history requires **ancient DNA** from dogs that lived before that overwriting.

This study assembles ancient dog genomes from across Europe, spanning from the Palaeolithic through later prehistory into historical periods, with a large international consortium supplying archaeological material — the author list reflects how many excavations must be combined to obtain adequate temporal and geographic coverage.

The analysis addresses several linked questions. **What was the population structure of early European dogs**, and how did it change over time? **Did dog ancestry turn over when human populations did** — the major demographic events of European prehistory being the Neolithic farming expansion from Anatolia and the Bronze Age steppe migrations? If dog and human ancestry shifted together, dogs were moving as part of human migrations rather than being adopted locally. And **when did the ancestry of modern European dogs become established**?

Ancient DNA is the appropriate tool because it provides **direct temporal sampling**. Rather than inferring past events from present-day variation — which requires demographic assumptions and is confounded by later admixture and bottlenecks — ancient genomes measure ancestry at known dates, so turnover and continuity can be observed rather than modelled.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Extends the dog model-organism lecture backwards: before breeds, before agriculture, to the first domestication event
- Demonstrates **ancient DNA** as a method and explains why direct temporal sampling beats inference from modern variation
- Illustrates **population turnover versus continuity**, a core concept in population genetics, with an unusually legible test case
- Connects animal domestication to **human prehistory**, since dog and human population histories can be compared directly
- Complements the dog *de novo* mutation study and the Kooiker polymyositis paper in this vault, which deal with modern breed genetics

## Future Research Directions

- Extend sampling to regions and periods still poorly represented, particularly eastern Europe and the earliest Palaeolithic
- Test directly whether dog ancestry turnover is quantitatively synchronous with human turnover at the same sites
- Identify when and where phenotypic traits under selection — body size, coat, skull shape — arose in the ancient record
- Recover ancient wolf genomes from the same regions to distinguish dog–wolf admixture from population replacement
- Use ancient dog genomes to date functional variants known from modern breeds, establishing whether they are recent or ancient
