# Gene ancestries reveal diverse microbial associations during eukaryogenesis

## Citation (NLM)
Bernabeu M, Manzano-Morales S, Marcet-Houben M, Gabaldón T. Gene ancestries reveal diverse microbial associations during eukaryogenesis. Nature. 2026;656(8093):399-407. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10639-9

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10639-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10639-9)

---

## Summary

The origin of eukaryotes remains a central problem in biology. Continuing debates agree on the pivotal role of a **symbiosis between an alphaproteobacterium and an Asgard archaeon**, but disagree about everything else: the nature, timing and contributions of **other potential bacterial partners**, and the role of **interactions with viruses**.

The authors approached this with advanced phylogenomic methods applied to comprehensive datasets spanning the known diversity of **cellular life and viruses** — the inclusion of viruses being an important design choice, since viral gene transfer into the eukaryotic lineage is often excluded from such analyses by construction.

They produce a **revised reconstruction of the LECA proteome**, tracing the phylogenetic origin of each protein family. Their conclusion is that the two-partner picture is too simple. They find **compelling evidence for multiple waves of horizontal gene transfer from diverse bacteria** during eukaryogenesis, meaning the emerging eukaryotic lineage acquired genes from a succession of bacterial sources, not only from the mitochondrial ancestor. The pre-eukaryotic lineage evidently existed in a **microbially complex environment**, exchanging genes with many partners over an extended period, with only one of those partners eventually becoming an organelle.

Reading this against the Tobiasson paper in this vault is instructive, because the two reach different emphases from overlapping evidence. Tobiasson and colleagues, applying constrained-tree hypothesis testing, conclude that Asgard archaea dominate the LECA gene complement with limited alphaproteobacterial input. Bernabeu and colleagues find diverse bacterial contributions in multiple waves. The disagreement is genuine and instructive: it turns on how ancient gene trees are modelled, how conservatively origins are assigned, and whether an ambiguous gene tree is scored as archaeal, bacterial, or unresolved. Both papers appeared within months of each other in the same journal.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Presents eukaryogenesis as a **prolonged, multi-partner process** rather than a single symbiotic merger — a substantially different picture from the textbook account
- Demonstrates **horizontal gene transfer** as a major force shaping a eukaryotic genome, not only a prokaryotic phenomenon
- Includes **viruses** in the analysis, raising the underexplored question of viral contributions to eukaryotic origins
- Read with Tobiasson: an excellent case study in **how two rigorous analyses of the same question disagree**, and how to evaluate which methodological choices drive the difference
- Directly supports the tree-of-life and orthology/paralogy lecture

## Future Research Directions

- Establish which methodological choices — tree model, taxon sampling, origin-assignment threshold — drive the disagreement with Asgard-dominant analyses
- Determine whether the multiple HGT waves correspond to identifiable donor lineages and, if so, what ecological association each implies
- Test whether viral-derived gene families in LECA are robust to alternative phylogenetic treatments, since virus phylogenies are especially difficult
- Integrate gene-origin analysis with duplication dating to determine when in the timeline each HGT wave occurred
- Expand sampling of environmental prokaryotic and viral diversity, since unsampled lineages can systematically bias origin assignment
