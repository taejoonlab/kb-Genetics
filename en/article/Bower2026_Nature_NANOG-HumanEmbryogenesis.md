# Base editing reveals an essential role for NANOG in human embryogenesis

## Citation (NLM)
Bower OJ, Orsi AER, McMahon R, Staneva D, Blagrove JR, Singh K, et al. Base editing reveals an essential role for NANOG in human embryogenesis. Nature. 2026. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10792-1

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10792-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10792-1)

---

## Summary

How the first cell lineages of human development are specified and maintained is fundamental biology with direct clinical implications for regenerative medicine, infertility, and pregnancy loss. Mouse models have identified the transcription factors regulating early development, but **translating those findings to human embryos has been limited by ethical, technical and practical constraints** — human embryos are scarce, donated under strict regulation, and available only in small numbers, so every experiment must be maximally informative per embryo.

The technical contribution is the use of **base editing** rather than nuclease-mediated knockout. Conventional CRISPR knockout in embryos is problematic: a double-strand break produces heterogeneous repair outcomes, so different cells within one embryo acquire different alleles, creating **mosaicism** that confounds interpretation of a phenotype in an embryo that cannot be cloned or bred. Base editing installs a **defined single-base change** without a double-strand break — here used to introduce a premature stop codon — giving a more uniform, predictable genotype in a limited number of precious embryos.

The biological target is ***NANOG***, a core pluripotency transcription factor. In mouse, *Nanog* has a well-defined role in the pluripotent epiblast, but mouse and human preimplantation development differ substantially in timing, morphology, and the regulatory networks controlling lineage specification, so mouse function does not establish human function. The authors show that **NANOG is essential for human embryogenesis**, testing the requirement directly in the relevant species rather than inferring it.

The result matters beyond the single gene. It demonstrates that base editing makes systematic loss-of-function genetics feasible in human embryos, converting early human development from a descriptive science, dependent on transcriptomic atlases and inferred models, into one where necessity can be tested.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Establishes that **mouse function does not guarantee human function**, tested rather than asserted — a central caution for the model-organism material
- Explains why **mosaicism** is the key obstacle to embryo genetics and how base editing circumvents it
- Introduces **pluripotency** and lineage specification with a genuine human loss-of-function experiment
- Raises the **ethical and regulatory framework** for human embryo research, which belongs in the course alongside the science
- Connects to the base-editing thalassaemia trial in this vault: the same technology used for therapy and for basic developmental genetics

## Future Research Directions

- Extend base editing to systematically test other candidate lineage regulators in human embryos, building a human-specific requirement map
- Determine what NANOG regulates in human embryos transcriptionally, by combining editing with single-cell RNA sequencing
- Establish where human and mouse regulatory networks diverge, and whether human-specific requirements cluster in particular processes
- Assess whether stem-cell-based embryo models faithfully reproduce the requirements identified in real embryos, which would reduce reliance on donated material
- Determine whether the findings inform causes of early pregnancy loss, where lineage specification failure is a plausible mechanism
