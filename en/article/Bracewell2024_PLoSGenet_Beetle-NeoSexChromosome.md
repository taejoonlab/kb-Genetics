# Sex and neo-sex chromosome evolution in beetles

## Citation (NLM)
Bracewell R, Tran A, Chatla K, Bachtrog D. Sex and neo-sex chromosome evolution in beetles. PLoS Genet. 2024;20(11):e1011477. doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1011477

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011477](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011477)

---

## Summary

Differentiated sex chromosomes have evolved from ordinary autosomes many times independently in animals and plants. What we know about how that happens comes almost entirely from a handful of model clades — mammals, birds, *Drosophila*, *Caenorhabditis* — which have revealed both general principles and lineage-specific quirks. Beetles (Coleoptera) are the most species-rich group of animals, with over 400,000 described species and famously variable karyotypes: chromosome numbers vary more than in most insect groups, implying high rates of fusion and fission. Cytogenetics established long ago that chromosomal sex determination is conserved across beetles (most species XY, some X0), but the **molecular identity and conservation of beetle sex chromosomes had never been examined systematically**.

The authors generated a **whole-chromosome assembly of *Tribolium confusum***, a flour beetle that carries a **neo-sex chromosome** — a sex chromosome formed recently by fusion of an autosome to an existing sex chromosome. They combined this with **eleven additional beetle genomes** to reconstruct karyotype evolution across Coleoptera.

Two results structure the paper. First, they identify **ancestral linkage groups, which they name "Stevens elements,"** that carry a conserved set of genes across beetles. This is the beetle equivalent of *Drosophila*'s Muller elements: chromosome numbers change by fusion and fission, but the underlying gene content of the ancestral units is recognizable across the order. The name honours **Nettie Stevens**, who identified the chromosomal basis of sex determination working on *Tenebrio* beetles. Second, they show that the **ancestral X chromosome is maintained across beetles**, but that **autosomes have been added independently, multiple times**, to the ancestral sex chromosomes — repeated, independent neo-sex chromosome formation within one order.

Critically, these neo-sex chromosomes then **evolve the stereotypical properties of sex chromosomes**. The authors document the evolution of **dosage compensation** — the mechanism that equalizes expression of X-linked genes between the sexes after the Y-linked copy degenerates — and a **non-random distribution of genes with sex-biased expression** on the neo-sex chromosomes. In other words, the same evolutionary trajectory that shaped the ancient X and Y is replayed on a young chromosome that was an autosome until recently. Because beetles have many such independent replicates at different ages, they offer a natural experiment for dissecting the general versus lineage-specific forces in sex chromosome evolution.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly supports the chromosomes and sex-determination lecture by showing that sex chromosomes are **not fixed features of a lineage** but repeatedly created, degraded, and replaced
- Introduces the concept of a **neo-sex chromosome** and, with it, the entire logic of sex chromosome evolution: recombination suppression → Y degeneration → dosage compensation
- **Stevens elements** provide a memorable link to the history of the field — Nettie Stevens discovered sex chromosomes in beetles, and the conserved linkage groups now bear her name
- Shows how **comparative genomics** answers evolutionary questions: one new assembly plus eleven existing genomes reconstructs 400,000 species' worth of karyotype history
- **Independent replication** is the key methodological virtue here — repeated origins let you distinguish general rules from historical accidents

## Future Research Directions

- Use neo-sex chromosomes of different ages across beetles as a time series to date the steps of Y degeneration and dosage compensation
- Determine the mechanism of beetle dosage compensation — is it upregulation of the single X as in *Drosophila*, X inactivation as in mammals, or something new?
- Test what triggers autosome–sex chromosome fusion: is it random, or biased by the gene content of the fusing autosome?
- Examine whether sexually antagonistic genes accumulate on neo-sex chromosomes soon after fusion, as theory predicts
- Expand sampling across Coleoptera to estimate the rate of sex chromosome turnover and ask whether it correlates with speciation rate in this hyperdiverse order
