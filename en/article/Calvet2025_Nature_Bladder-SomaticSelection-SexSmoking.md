# Sex and smoking bias in the selection of somatic mutations in human bladder

## Citation (NLM)
Calvet F, Blanco Martinez-Illescas R, Muiños F, Tretiakova M, Latorre-Esteves ES, Fredrickson J, et al. Sex and smoking bias in the selection of somatic mutations in human bladder. Nature. 2025;647(8084):436-444. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09521-x

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09521-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09521-x)

---

## Summary

Men are at higher risk of several cancer types than women, and for **bladder cancer the risk is roughly four times higher** — a difference that has never been satisfactorily explained. Smoking is also a principal risk factor. Since tumorigenesis is driven by somatic mutations, the authors asked whether the landscape of mutant clones in **normal** bladder differs by sex and by smoking history, long before any tumour appears.

They used **ultradeep duplex DNA sequencing at approximately 5,000× coverage** on normal bladder tissue. Duplex sequencing reads both strands of each original molecule and calls a variant only when both agree, which is what makes it possible to detect mutations present in a tiny fraction of cells. At this depth the clonal composition of histologically normal epithelium can be reconstructed: which driver mutations are present, in how many clones, and how large those clones have grown.

The framing distinguishes two processes that are easily conflated. **Mutation** supplies variants; **selection** determines which mutant clones expand. A sex or smoking difference in cancer risk could arise from either — more mutations, or stronger selection on the mutations that occur. By measuring clone sizes and driver mutation frequencies in normal tissue, the design separates them.

The finding of **sex and smoking bias in selection** places the difference at least partly in the second process. It is not only that men or smokers accumulate more mutations; the somatic environment of the bladder favours the expansion of driver-mutant clones differently depending on sex and smoking status. The tissue context, not just the mutational input, shapes which clones grow — and clone expansion in normal tissue is the substrate from which cancer eventually emerges.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Separates **mutation from selection** experimentally, a distinction central to cancer genetics and to evolution generally
- Shows that **normal tissue is already an evolving cell population**, years or decades before cancer — reframing carcinogenesis as a long selective process
- Offers a mechanistic handle on a major epidemiological puzzle: the **male bias in bladder cancer**
- Demonstrates **ultradeep duplex sequencing** as the technology that made normal-tissue somatic evolution measurable
- Complements the NF1 normal-tissue study and the Lawson population-scale somatic mutation paper in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Determine the mechanism of sex-biased selection — hormonal signalling, loss of Y, immune surveillance differences, or carcinogen exposure in urine
- Test whether smoking cessation shifts the selective landscape back, which would explain why risk declines after quitting
- Establish whether clone size distribution in normal bladder predicts future cancer risk, which would make it a biomarker
- Extend the analysis to other tissues with strong sex disparities to test whether the mechanism is bladder-specific
- Investigate whether selection differences reflect the tissue microenvironment or cell-intrinsic differences, using transplantation or organoid systems
