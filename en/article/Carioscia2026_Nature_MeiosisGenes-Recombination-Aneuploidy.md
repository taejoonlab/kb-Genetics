# Common variation in meiosis genes shapes human recombination and aneuploidy

## Citation (NLM)
Carioscia SA, Biddanda A, Starostik MR, Tang X, Hoffmann ER, Demko ZP, McCoy RC. Common variation in meiosis genes shapes human recombination and aneuploidy. Nature. 2026;651(8095):146-155. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09964-2

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09964-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09964-2)

---

## Summary

The leading cause of human pregnancy loss is **aneuploidy**, most often tracing to errors in chromosome segregation during **female meiosis**. Abnormal **crossover recombination** is a known risk factor: chiasmata physically tether homologues until anaphase I, so chromosomes with too few crossovers, or crossovers positioned too near the telomere or centromere, are prone to mis-segregate. What has been missing is data large enough to determine whether recombination and aneuploidy share a **genetic basis** — whether the same inherited variants that shape a woman's recombination landscape also shape her risk of producing aneuploid eggs.

The authors obtained that scale from an unexpected source: **retrospective analysis of pre-implantation genetic testing (PGT) data from 139,416 in vitro fertilized embryos from 22,850 sets of biological parents**. PGT genotypes embryos for clinical aneuploidy screening, and each embryo is genotyped alongside both parents — effectively a vast trio dataset covering the earliest observable stage of development, before selection against aneuploidy has removed affected conceptions. By tracing **haplotype transmission**, the authors identified **3,809,412 crossovers** and **92,485 aneuploid chromosomes**, jointly measuring both phenotypes in the same individuals.

The direct relationship confirms the mechanistic expectation: **crossover counts were lower in aneuploid than in euploid embryos**, consistent with the role of chiasmata in holding homologues together. With both phenotypes measured at this scale, the authors then map **common variation in meiosis genes** influencing recombination rate and placement, and test whether the same variants affect aneuploidy risk.

The implication is that susceptibility to aneuploidy is not purely a function of maternal age but has a component of **inherited common variation** — meaning women differ genetically in their baseline risk of producing aneuploid eggs, over and above the well-known age effect.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Connects **recombination, meiosis, and aneuploidy** in one dataset, linking three topics usually taught separately
- Provides a mechanistic and quantitative basis for the chiasma requirement: fewer crossovers, more mis-segregation, measured across millions of events
- Demonstrates a striking example of **repurposing clinical data** for basic research at a scale no research study could fund
- Introduces the idea that **aneuploidy risk is partly heritable**, which is not part of the standard maternal-age account
- Complements the Palsson recombination maps and the Li MeiHMM non-disjunction study in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Identify the causal variants and genes underlying the mapped associations and determine their molecular roles in meiosis
- Test whether a polygenic score for aneuploidy risk adds predictive value beyond maternal age in IVF outcomes
- Determine whether the same variants affect male meiosis, where aneuploidy rates are far lower
- Investigate gene–age interaction: do genetic effects on aneuploidy risk grow, shrink, or stay constant with maternal age?
- Extend the analysis to ancestrally diverse IVF cohorts, since PGT datasets are demographically skewed
