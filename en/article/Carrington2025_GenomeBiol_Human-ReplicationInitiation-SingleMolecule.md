# Most human DNA replication initiation is dispersed throughout the genome with only a minority within previously identified initiation zones

## Citation (NLM)
Carrington JT, Wilson RHC, de La Vega E, Thiyagarajan S, Barker T, Catchpole L, et al. Most human DNA replication initiation is dispersed throughout the genome with only a minority within previously identified initiation zones. Genome Biol. 2025;26:122. doi:10.1186/s13059-025-03591-w

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-025-03591-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-025-03591-w)

---

## Summary

Where does human DNA replication start? In budding yeast the answer is precise: defined origin sequences bound by the origin recognition complex. In mammals no such sequence consensus exists, and locating initiation sites has been a long-standing technical problem. Population-level methods — averaging replication signals over millions of cells — identify broad **initiation zones**, and a picture emerged in which replication starts within a limited number of such zones, correlated with transcription and particular epigenetic marks.

This study argues that picture is an artifact of averaging. The authors developed an **unbiased single-molecule assay**: cells incorporate **BrdU**, and individual DNA molecules are read by **nanopore sequencing**, which detects BrdU directly in the sequence signal. Because BrdU marks newly synthesized DNA, the pattern of incorporation along a single read reveals the replication history of that specific molecule. Increasing BrdU incorporation allowed them to measure replication dynamics and to identify, **on individual reads**, where a fork **initiated**, which **direction** each fork travelled, and where forks **terminated** — at high resolution, throughout S phase, genome-wide, and at very high coverage at specific loci using targeted enrichment.

The result reframes the field. Initiation events *are* significantly enriched within the broad initiation zones identified by population studies — so those zones are real. But **those focused sites account for only about 20% of all identified initiation events**. The remaining **~80% of initiation is dispersed throughout the genome**. These dispersed sites are invisible to population methods for a simple statistical reason: **each is used only rarely**. Averaged over millions of cells, a site used in one cell in a thousand contributes almost nothing to the signal, while a site used in a tenth of cells stands out as a "zone." Population averaging does not just blur the map; it systematically deletes the majority of it.

Consistent with being a distinct class rather than weak versions of the same thing, the dispersed initiation sites **do not show a strong relationship to transcription or to any particular epigenetic signature** — precisely the correlations that had been used to explain what specifies mammalian origins. The conclusion is that most of the human genome is replicated from initiation events that are individually rare, genomically dispersed, and not specified by the features previously thought to define origins.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly extends the DNA replication portion of the central dogma lecture beyond the textbook bacterial/yeast origin model to what actually happens in human cells
- A superb methodological lesson: **population averages can create phenomena that do not exist at the individual level**, and can erase phenomena that do. Applicable far beyond replication
- Introduces **single-molecule sequencing** (nanopore) as a way to read modified bases directly, not just sequence
- Shows how a well-established consensus ("replication initiates in defined zones associated with transcription") can be substantially revised by a change of measurement scale rather than a new hypothesis
- Connects replication to chromatin and transcription — and then shows that the connection is weaker than assumed for most initiation events

## Future Research Directions

- Determine what, if anything, specifies dispersed initiation sites — if not sequence, transcription, or chromatin marks, is initiation partly stochastic licensing?
- Test whether the ratio of focused to dispersed initiation changes with cell type, replication stress, or oncogene activation
- Ask whether dispersed initiation is a backup system: are these sites used more when zone-based initiation is inhibited?
- Relate dispersed initiation to genome instability — common fragile sites and under-replicated regions may reflect insufficient dispersed initiation
- Apply the single-molecule approach across species to test whether the focused/dispersed split is a general feature of large genomes
