# New perspectives on Drosophila melanogaster de novo gene origination revealed by investigation of ancient African genetic variation

## Citation (NLM)
Cridland JM, Polston ES, Begun DJ. New perspectives on Drosophila melanogaster de novo gene origination revealed by investigation of ancient African genetic variation. Genetics. 2025;230(1):iyaf044. doi:10.1093/genetics/iyaf044

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf044](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf044)

---

## Summary

Where do new genes come from? For decades the answer was **gene duplication** followed by divergence. More recently a second route has been proposed: ***de novo* gene origination**, in which ancestral intergenic or intronic sequence is recruited into producing a mature transcript that has no homolog in the ancestor. Candidates have been reported in *Drosophila*, mouse, yeast, human, rice, and nematodes, but the field lacks consensus on almost everything that matters — how to define and detect such genes, how abundant they are, how long they persist, what population-genetic forces spread or remove them, and whether they do anything.

Two detection strategies have been used in *D. melanogaster*. The first compares the reference annotation across species. The second, more sensitive one uses **raw transcriptome data** to find unannotated transcribed sequences with no evidence of expression in relatives. Critically, nearly all of the second-approach work has used flies from **recently established cosmopolitan populations** (Raleigh, North Carolina; Europe). This is a sampling problem, because *D. melanogaster* originated in Africa and most of the species' genetic variation is found in **African populations**. If you want to understand where genetic novelty comes from, you should look where the variation is.

The authors therefore sequenced transcriptomes from **testis and accessory glands** of flies from **Zambia** and compared the *de novo* gene candidates found there with those from North American populations. They report a **large number of previously undiscovered candidates**, most of which are **expressed polymorphically** — present in some genotypes and absent in others within a population, which is exactly what you expect of genes at the moment of origin rather than established genes. Many are predicted to encode **secreted proteins**, consistent with the long-standing observation that novel genes are enriched in male reproductive tissues.

Two results complicate simple stories. First, despite the far higher genomic variation in Zambian flies, Zambian and North American populations express **similar numbers** of candidate *de novo* genes — so candidate abundance is not a simple function of standing nucleotide diversity. Second, using the Raleigh inbred lines, the authors show that a fraction of the **rarely expressed** candidates in that population represents **deleterious transcription promoted by inbreeding depression** — spurious transcription unmasked by homozygosity, not genuine gene birth. That is a sobering methodological finding: some of what the field calls *de novo* genes is transcriptional noise revealed by inbreeding. Finally, many candidates are expressed in **multiple tissues and both sexes**, which complicates the assumption that these genes originate under male-limited selection. The authors are careful to conclude that the relative importance of positive and negative selection in this process remains unresolved.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly addresses the question "**where do new genes come from?**" — the natural counterpart to duplication/paralogy in the tree-of-life and orthology lectures
- Demonstrates that **sampling matters**: conclusions drawn from a recently derived population may not describe the species, a lesson that applies equally to human genomics
- A rare and honest example of a paper identifying an artifact in its own field's method — inbreeding-driven spurious transcription masquerading as gene birth
- Introduces **expression polymorphism** as a phenotype and shows why segregating, not fixed, variation is where origination is visible
- Connects population genetics (nucleotide diversity, inbreeding depression, selection) with molecular evolution (transcriptome annotation, ORF prediction) in one study

## Future Research Directions

- Distinguish genuine *de novo* genes from inbreeding-unmasked spurious transcription systematically — develop a criterion that can be applied before candidates enter the literature
- Test whether the polymorphically expressed candidates are under selection: measure allele frequencies of expression-conferring haplotypes and look for signatures of positive or purifying selection
- Determine whether any of the predicted secreted proteins are actually translated and have measurable phenotypic effects, using ribosome profiling and knockouts
- Extend sampling to additional African populations and to other *Drosophila* species to estimate origination and loss rates directly
- Ask why candidate counts are similar in Zambia and North America despite very different diversity — is candidate number limited by transcription machinery rather than by sequence variation?
