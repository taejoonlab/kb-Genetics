# Unexpectedly low recombination rates and presence of hotspots in termite genomes

## Citation (NLM)
Everitt T, Rönneburg T, Elsner D, Olsson A, Liu Y, Larva T, Korb J, Webster MT. Unexpectedly low recombination rates and presence of hotspots in termite genomes. Genome Res. 2025;35(3):1-14. doi:10.1101/gr.279180.124

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.279180.124](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.279180.124)

---

## Summary

Meiotic recombination does two jobs: it ensures correct chromosome disjunction, and it creates new allele combinations for selection to act on. It also has costs — it can generate structural mutations and break up favourable allele combinations — so each species' rate reflects a trade-off. Among animals, the **highest recombination rates on record belong to social insects**. In *Apis* honey bees the rate reaches 17–25 cM/Mb (*A. mellifera* 20.8, *A. dorsata* 25.1), and other social Hymenoptera are also high: bumblebee *Bombus terrestris* 8.9, stingless bee *Frieseomelitta varia* 9.3–12.5, wasp *Vespula vulgaris* 9.7, ants *Pogonomyrmex rugosus* 11.1 and *Acromyrmex echinatior* 6.1 cM/Mb. Solitary relatives are ordinary: the solitary bee *Megachile rotundata* is 1.0 and the wasp *Nasonia* 1.5 cM/Mb, against an average of 2.2 cM/Mb for 15 non-hymenopteran insects.

Several hypotheses explain this as an adaptation to **eusociality**: greater within-colony genetic diversity may promote worker task specialization, diversify immune genes against pathogens, reduce variance in relatedness and hence kin conflict, or allow caste-specific gene functions to evolve independently. But every one of these tests had been done in a single insect order. **All the data came from Hymenoptera.** If eusociality drives high recombination, it should do so in eusocial insects that are not Hymenoptera.

Termites provide exactly that test. They are eusocial but belong to **Blattodea** (cockroach relatives), an entirely independent origin of eusociality. The authors inferred fine-scale recombination rates from **linkage disequilibrium** across the genomes of two termites, *Macrotermes bellicosus* and *Cryptotermes secundus*.

Both species have recombination rates of about **1 cM/Mb** — close to the metazoan average and far below social Hymenoptera. **Eusociality is therefore not universally coupled to elevated recombination.** The authors suggest that the hymenopteran pattern instead reflects something specific to that order: intense selection among **haploid males**, whose genomes are directly exposed to selection with no dominance masking.

A second finding concerns fine structure. Recombination in both termite genomes is **highly punctate** — concentrated in narrow intervals, i.e. **hotspots**. This matters because hotspot architecture varies fundamentally between taxa: in mammals, hotspot placement is directed by the zinc-finger protein **PRDM9**, whereas many other lineages (including dogs, birds, and *Drosophila*) lack functional PRDM9 and instead recombine at promoters and other functional features. The authors found **full-length PRDM9 genes** in both termite genomes, raising the possibility that termite hotspots are PRDM9-directed as in mammals. They also report that recombination rate within genes correlates with inferred **germline DNA methylation** levels, connecting the recombination landscape to the epigenome.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly supports the recombination and linkage lecture with real cross-species numbers, and shows that recombination rate is itself an evolving trait with a distribution across species
- A model example of **hypothesis testing by phylogenetic replication**: the eusociality hypothesis was based entirely on one order, and testing an independent origin of eusociality is the correct way to falsify it
- Introduces **recombination hotspots** and the **PRDM9** system — the mammalian mechanism for placing crossovers — and the fact that hotspot control differs among lineages
- Demonstrates the LD-based inference approach: recombination rate can be estimated from population sequence data without pedigrees or crosses
- Connects recombination to **DNA methylation**, linking the linkage lecture to the epigenetics lecture

## Future Research Directions

- Test whether termite PRDM9 is functional — does it bind DNA, is it expressed in the germline, and do its predicted binding motifs coincide with the inferred hotspots?
- Extend sampling to more independent origins of eusociality (e.g. social aphids, thrips, naked mole-rats) to test the eusociality hypothesis more broadly
- Test the haploid-male-selection hypothesis directly by comparing haplodiploid and diplodiploid taxa matched for sociality
- Determine whether the correlation between genic recombination and germline methylation is causal, using methylation-perturbation experiments
- Validate LD-based rate estimates against pedigree- or gamete-based maps in at least one termite species
