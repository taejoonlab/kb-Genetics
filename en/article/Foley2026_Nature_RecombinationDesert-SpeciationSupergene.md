# An ancient recombination desert is a speciation supergene in placental mammals

## Citation (NLM)
Foley NM, Rasulis RG, Wani Z, Mendoza Cerna MN, Figueiró HV, Koepfli KP, Raudsepp T, Murphy WJ. An ancient recombination desert is a speciation supergene in placental mammals. Nature. 2026;649(8088):1228-1237. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09740-2

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09740-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09740-2)

---

## Summary

Gene flow between biological species is common and often adaptive across the tree of life. That creates a serious inference problem: because different genomic regions have different histories when species exchange genes, a genome contains a **complex collection of conflicting gene trees**, and recovering the correct species relationships from them is difficult. The local rate of **meiotic recombination** is central to this, because it determines how signatures of gene flow — and barriers to it — are distributed along chromosomes. Regions of low recombination retain long blocks of ancestry and resist introgression, whereas high-recombination regions are quickly broken up and homogenized between hybridizing lineages. Until recently this relationship could not be examined broadly, because recombination maps existed for very few species.

The authors used **deep learning applied to genome alignments** to infer recombination landscapes at scale, then related those landscapes to phylogenetic signal across placental mammals.

Their central finding is an **ancient recombination desert** — a genomic region that has suppressed recombination over very long evolutionary timescales — that behaves as a **speciation supergene**. A supergene is a set of tightly linked loci inherited as a single unit because recombination between them is suppressed. Because this region does not recombine, alleles within it cannot be shuffled between diverging lineages, so it resists introgression when species hybridize and preserves the true divergence history while the rest of the genome is homogenized by gene flow.

Two consequences follow. Biologically, this region is expected to accumulate the loci contributing to reproductive isolation, since incompatibilities that would otherwise be broken up by recombination remain linked — the standard theoretical expectation for how supergenes and low-recombination regions become barriers to gene flow. Practically, the region provides an unusually reliable source of **phylogenetic signal**, since it is the part of the genome least contaminated by introgression.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Links **recombination rate** directly to **speciation**, connecting two lectures that are usually taught separately
- Introduces **supergenes** and explains why suppressed recombination allows sets of loci to evolve as units
- Explains why **gene trees disagree with species trees**, and identifies which genomic regions to trust when they do
- Shows a modern machine-learning application in comparative genomics, inferring recombination without pedigrees or crosses
- Complements the recombination hotspot review and the termite recombination study in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Identify the genes within the recombination desert and test whether they contribute to hybrid incompatibility
- Determine what suppresses recombination there — a chromosomal inversion, chromatin structure, or absence of PRDM9 binding sites
- Test whether the same region resists introgression in ongoing mammalian hybrid zones
- Re-examine contested mammalian phylogenetic relationships using signal restricted to low-introgression regions
- Ask whether analogous ancient recombination deserts exist in other vertebrate clades, or whether this is specific to placental mammals
