# Phylogenomic timetree-calibrated speciation clocks for Caenorhabditis nematodes reveal slow but disproportionate accumulation of post-zygotic reproductive isolation

## Citation (NLM)
Fusca DD, Dall'Acqua MN, Sánchez-Ramírez S, Cutter AD. Phylogenomic timetree-calibrated speciation clocks for Caenorhabditis nematodes reveal slow but disproportionate accumulation of post-zygotic reproductive isolation. PLoS Genet. 2025;21(9):e1011852. doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1011852

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011852](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011852)

---

## Summary

Species form when reproductive isolation accumulates between diverging populations. Isolating barriers come in kinds — **pre-zygotic** (mate recognition, mating behaviour, gamete incompatibility: the populations do not mate, or mating does not produce a zygote) and **post-zygotic** (hybrids are formed but are inviable or sterile) — and the **"speciation clock"** for each kind can run at a different speed. In many taxa pre-zygotic isolation evolves faster, often because reinforcement selects directly against hybridization. This study asks, for the first time in nematodes, which clock runs faster in *Caenorhabditis*.

Answering the question requires a **timetree** — a phylogeny with branch lengths in absolute time — and nematodes make that unusually hard. There are **no fossils** to calibrate the tree; molecular divergence is deep enough that **synonymous sites are saturated** (multiple substitutions at the same position erase the signal); and **codon usage bias** distorts substitution-rate estimates. The authors combined several molecular-evolutionary strategies in phylogenomic dating to handle each of these problems, inferring the species tree and divergence times across **51 *Caenorhabditis* species**.

They then integrated the timetree with **experimental data on pre- and post-zygotic isolation** measured in interspecific crosses. Two results follow. First, **post-zygotic isolation accumulates faster than pre-zygotic isolation** in *Caenorhabditis* — the opposite of the pattern in many other taxa, and it implies that speciation here is driven mainly by **intrinsic** genetic incompatibilities rather than by ecological or behavioural divergence. Second, within post-zygotic isolation, **hybrid sterility evolves before hybrid inviability**. This is Haldane-adjacent, well-known logic: **germline developmental programs are disproportionately fragile** to genetic disruption, so the capacity to make gametes fails before the capacity to make a viable animal.

The absolute pace is striking. The authors estimate it takes roughly **50 million generations** for intrinsic post-zygotic reproductive compatibility between diverging *Caenorhabditis* pairs to be halved, on average. That is slow. They offer three non-exclusive explanations: *Caenorhabditis* species retain extraordinary **population genetic hyperdiversity**, which may buffer incompatibility accumulation; **global biodiversity is incompletely sampled**, so the closest relatives of many species may simply not be in the dataset; and there are likely **uncharacterized incipient or cryptic species** whose inclusion would compress the young end of the divergence scale.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Makes **speciation** quantitative: not "populations diverge and become species" but a measurable rate at which specific barrier types accumulate per unit time
- Distinguishes **pre-zygotic and post-zygotic** isolation clearly and shows they are independently evolving quantities, not stages of one process
- Introduces **Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities** and intrinsic isolation as the mechanism behind post-zygotic barriers
- The **hybrid sterility before inviability** result gives a mechanistic reason (germline fragility) rather than just a pattern — good for discussing why some tissues are more mutationally sensitive
- Demonstrates the practical difficulty of **molecular dating** without fossils: saturation, codon bias, calibration — worth showing students that a "timetree" is an inference with assumptions, not a measurement

## Future Research Directions

- Sample more *Caenorhabditis* species, especially recently diverged and cryptic ones, to fill the young end of the divergence axis and test whether the clock estimate changes
- Identify the genes underlying hybrid sterility in the youngest species pairs and test whether they are enriched for germline-expressed functions, as the fragility hypothesis predicts
- Test the hyperdiversity hypothesis directly: does higher within-species diversity slow the fixation of incompatibilities?
- Compare selfing and outcrossing *Caenorhabditis* lineages — mating system should strongly affect how fast incompatibilities fix
- Extend the timetree approach to other nematode genera to ask whether slow speciation clocks are a phylum-wide feature or specific to *Caenorhabditis*
