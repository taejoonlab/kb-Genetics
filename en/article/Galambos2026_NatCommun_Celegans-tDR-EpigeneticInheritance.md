# tRNA-derived RNA processing in sperm transmits non-genetically inherited phenotypes to offspring in C. elegans

## Citation (NLM)
Galambos NS, Crocker OJ, Schneider BK, Allerton KS, Gross KE, Schneider AE, Lynch J, Yukselen O, Kucukural A, Conine CC. tRNA-derived RNA processing in sperm transmits non-genetically inherited phenotypes to offspring in C. elegans. Nat Commun. 2026;17:1607. doi:10.1038/s41467-026-70029-7

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-70029-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-70029-7)

---

## Summary

An organism's environment can alter epigenetic information in its gametes, transmitting phenotypes to offspring that are not encoded in DNA sequence. The best-developed mammalian example concerns **tRNA-derived RNAs (tDRs)** — fragments produced by cleavage of mature tRNAs, which are abundant in sperm. In mouse models, paternal diet regulates specific tDRs in sperm; after fertilization these tDRs alter embryonic gene expression and generate metabolic phenotypes in adult offspring. What has been missing is the mechanism connecting the two ends: the developmental changes between an altered sperm tDR payload and an adult metabolic phenotype have remained uncharacterized, and the mammalian system is slow and expensive to dissect.

This study establishes the phenomenon in ***C. elegans***, where genetics is tractable. The authors show that **tDRs also accumulate in *C. elegans* sperm** and **similarly transmit epigenetically inherited phenotypes to offspring** — demonstrating that this is not a mammal-specific curiosity but a conserved route of intergenerational information transfer.

The genetic payoff is the identification of a regulator. The authors identify the **RNase T2 enzyme *rnst-2*** as a controller of *C. elegans* tDR accumulation. **RNST-2 processes or degrades tRNA halves into short fragments of under 30 nucleotides.** This matters because tDRs are not a single species: tRNA halves (roughly 30–35 nt) and shorter fragments have different proposed functions, and an enzyme that converts one class into the other controls the *composition* of the inherited RNA payload, not merely its amount. Having a named gene whose loss changes the tDR profile converts a correlative phenomenon into a manipulable genetic system — one can now ask whether altering tDR processing is sufficient to alter offspring phenotype, rather than only whether the two co-vary.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A rigorous, genetically tractable example of **transgenerational epigenetic inheritance** — a topic where the literature contains many weak claims and few mechanisms
- Establishes **small RNAs in sperm** as a concrete carrier of non-genetic information, distinct from DNA methylation and histone modification
- Demonstrates the value of moving a phenomenon from mouse to *C. elegans*: the same biology, but now with forward genetics available
- Reinforces that "epigenetic inheritance" requires identifying a **molecule and an enzyme**, not just a correlation across generations
- Connects the epigenetics lecture to the *C. elegans* model-organism lecture

## Future Research Directions

- Test sufficiency: inject defined tDR species into embryos and ask whether offspring phenotypes are reproduced
- Determine the downstream targets — which embryonic transcripts respond to altered tDR content, and by what mechanism (translational, silencing, or otherwise)
- Establish how many generations the transmitted phenotype persists and whether it requires continued environmental exposure
- Ask whether environmental conditions regulate *rnst-2* activity, which would complete the causal chain from environment to enzyme to tDR profile to offspring
- Test whether the mammalian RNASET2 orthologue plays the equivalent role in sperm, which would make the worm result directly translatable
