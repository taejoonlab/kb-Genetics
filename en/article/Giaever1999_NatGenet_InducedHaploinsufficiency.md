# Genomic Profiling of Drug Sensitivities via Induced Haploinsufficiency

## Citation (NLM)
Giaever G, Shoemaker DD, Jones TW, Liang H, Winzeler EA, Astromoff A, Davis RW. Genomic profiling of drug sensitivities via induced haploinsufficiency. Nat Genet. 1999;21(3):278-283. doi:10.1038/6791

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/6791](https://doi.org/10.1038/6791)

---

## Summary

Haploinsufficiency, in which loss of one gene copy causes an abnormal phenotype, is thought to be rare: in humans only a handful of genes were then known, and a Drosophila genome survey found 56 haploinsufficient loci (about 0.4% of coding capacity). This paper shows that a heterozygous diploid yeast strain, which carries one copy of a gene instead of two, becomes hypersensitive to a drug that inhibits the product of that gene. This "drug-induced haploinsufficiency" identifies the heterozygous locus as the likely drug target, and the paper turns the observation into a genomic method for drug-target identification.

In a feasibility study the authors built heterozygous strains for six genes encoding known drug targets: HIS3 (3-amino-triazole), ALG7 (tunicamycin), RNR2 (hydroxyurea), TUB1 and TUB2 (benomyl), and ERG11 (fluconazole). Each strain was grown with a sublethal concentration of the matching drug and compared with wild type; all six showed induced haploinsufficiency, and the effect was drug-specific (no sensitivity with other drugs). For example, the alg7/ALG7 strain was clearly more sensitive than wild type at 0.5 ug/ml tunicamycin, where both grew equally without drug and both were inhibited at 2 ug/ml. The tub1/TUB1 heterozygote is also slow-growing without drug but is differentially inhibited at 25 ug/ml benomyl. Adding a plasmid-borne HIS3 copy made the his3/HIS3 strain more resistant, confirming the locus causes the phenotype.

The method was then parallelized using strains tagged with unique 20-base molecular bar codes (the deletion cassette carries a kanamycin marker, two common priming sites and 50-bp homology arms). Pooled strains were grown competitively with or without drug, bar codes were PCR-amplified with a fluorescent primer and hybridized to an oligonucleotide array (Affymetrix); the slope of log signal against time gives each strain's relative growth. In a 12-strain pool at 0.5 ug/ml tunicamycin, only alg7/ALG7 declined, falling at 22 h and undetectable at 48 h. In a 233-strain pool, three strains were drug-sensitive: alg7/ALG7 (the known target), ymr007w/YMR007W (126-aa protein of unknown function) and ymr266w/YMR266W (953-aa, homologous to major facilitator superfamily transporters, so possibly involved in drug export). Both homozygous deletions were also tunicamycin-sensitive, ruling them out as targets, i.e., they are "hypersensitivity" loci. One strain (ymr242c, a ribosomal-protein gene) was haploinsufficient even without drug. The authors discuss implications for drug discovery without a pre-selected target, scaling to all approximately 6,000 yeast genes, and the idea that induced haploinsufficiency in human heterozygotes may explain recessive-looking alleles that act dominantly under stress and variable drug toxicity. ## Significance in Introduction Context

- Reframes dominance and gene dosage: a null allele that is "recessive" under normal conditions can behave as dominant (haploinsufficient) under environmental stress.
- Early demonstration of a genome-scale, bar-coded deletion strain approach that grew into the yeast deletion collection and chemical genomics.
- Connects genetics to pharmacology (target identification, drug transport, pharmacogenomics).

## Future Research Directions

- Genome-wide HIP/HOP screens with the full deletion collection (realized in later studies) and in other organisms (e.g., Candida albicans).
- Systematic study of drug-hypersensitive and transport genes as determinants of drug availability.
- Human genetic variation in gene dosage and its interaction with drugs and environmental toxins.
