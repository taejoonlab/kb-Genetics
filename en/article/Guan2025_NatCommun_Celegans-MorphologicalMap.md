# Cell lineage-resolved embryonic morphological map reveals signaling associated with cell fate and size asymmetry

## Citation (NLM)
Guan G, Li Z, Ma Y, Ye P, Cao J, Wong MK, et al. Cell lineage-resolved embryonic morphological map reveals signaling associated with cell fate and size asymmetry. Nat Commun. 2025;16:3235. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-58878-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58878-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58878-0)

---

## Summary

How cells change shape is central to the development of tissues, organs, and embryos — but shape is hard to study systematically. Lineage tracing tells you a cell's ancestry; transcriptomics tells you its expression state; neither captures its geometry, its volume, or which neighbours it touches. This study fills that gap for *Caenorhabditis elegans*, the one animal where the complete embryonic lineage is already known.

The authors built a **real-time cellular map covering over 95% of the cells formed during *C. elegans* embryogenesis**, comprising **nearly 400,000 three-dimensional cell regions**. For each cell the map records **identity, lineage, fate, shape, volume, surface area, contact area with each neighbour, and gene expression profile** — and it is distributed through software and a public website rather than as a static supplementary table. Contact area is the crucial addition: in an embryo where induction depends on physical juxtaposition, knowing precisely which cell touches which, and over how much surface, is what turns a descriptive atlas into a tool for inferring signalling.

The authors use the map to analyse specific developmental processes — **dorsal intercalation**, **intestinal formation**, and **muscle assembly** — each a well-studied morphogenetic event that can now be described quantitatively rather than qualitatively. They then show how **Notch and Wnt signalling**, together with the geometry of cell contacts, relate to **cell fate decisions and to asymmetric cell division**. Asymmetric division producing daughters of unequal size is a recurring motif in *C. elegans* development, and the map allows the relationship between contact geometry, signalling, and the resulting size asymmetry to be examined across the whole embryo rather than one lineage at a time.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Adds a **geometric and physical dimension** to the developmental genetics students usually meet only as lineage diagrams and gene names
- Shows why *C. elegans*'s **invariant lineage** keeps paying off — a map of this completeness is impossible in an animal without a fixed cell lineage
- Makes **contact-dependent signalling** (Notch especially) concrete: induction requires physical adjacency, and now adjacency is measured
- Illustrates the modern norm of publishing a **community resource** with software and a website rather than a finished conclusion
- Complements the *C. elegans* neurogenesis review in this vault, which covers the transcriptional side of the same developmental process

## Future Research Directions

- Use the contact map to predict which cells can signal to which, then test predictions by ablating specific contacts
- Determine whether contact area quantitatively predicts signalling strength, or whether contact is effectively binary
- Perturb Notch and Wnt components and re-map, to distinguish geometry that causes signalling from geometry that results from it
- Extend the approach to other nematode species to ask whether embryonic geometry is as conserved as lineage
- Integrate the morphological map with single-cell transcriptomic atlases so that shape, contact, and expression are analysed jointly
