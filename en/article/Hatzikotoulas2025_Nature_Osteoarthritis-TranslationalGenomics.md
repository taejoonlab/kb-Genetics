# Translational genomics of osteoarthritis in 1,962,069 individuals

## Citation (NLM)
Hatzikotoulas K, Southam L, Stefansdottir L, Boer CG, McDonald ML, Pett JP, et al.; Genetics of Osteoarthritis Consortium. Translational genomics of osteoarthritis in 1,962,069 individuals. Nature. 2025;641(8065):1217-1224. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-08771-z

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08771-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08771-z)

---

## Summary

Osteoarthritis is the third most rapidly growing cause of disability, after dementia and diabetes, and is projected to affect **1 billion people by 2050**. There are **no disease-modifying treatments**, only symptom management and joint replacement, which makes understanding its aetiopathology urgent.

This study is a genome-wide association meta-analysis across up to **489,975 cases and 1,472,094 controls** — nearly two million individuals — establishing **962 independent associations**, of which **513 were previously unreported**. But the paper's importance lies less in the count than in what the authors do next, which is a model of how to move from association to biology.

**From signal to tissue.** Using single-cell multiomics data, they find signal enrichment in **embryonic skeletal development pathways** — a striking result, because it implies that susceptibility to a degenerative disease of ageing is partly established during development, in how the joint is built.

**From locus to gene.** They integrate orthogonal evidence — **transcriptome, proteome and epigenome profiles of primary joint tissues** — to implicate **700 effector genes**. Using primary joint tissue rather than a convenient cell line is essential, since regulatory effects are tissue-specific and a variant's target gene in cartilage may differ from its target elsewhere.

**Rare and common variation converge.** Within the effector genes, they find **rare coding-variant burden associations with effect sizes consistently larger than the common-variant associations** at the same genes. This is the recurring modern finding: the same gene contributes to disease through common variants of small effect and rare variants of large effect, and each validates the other.

**Convergent processes.** Eight biological processes show involvement of multiple effector genes, including the **circadian clock**, **glial-cell-related processes**, and pathways with established roles in osteoarthritis (**TGFβ, FGF, WNT, BMP, retinoic acid signalling, and extracellular matrix organization**). Circadian and glial involvement were not expected for a joint disease.

**Translation.** **10% of effector genes encode proteins that are targets of approved drugs**, offering concrete repurposing opportunities — the practical payoff that justifies the phrase "translational genomics" in the title.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows what a **mature GWAS** looks like today: association is the beginning, and the work is functional integration, effector gene assignment, and therapeutic mapping
- The **rare/common convergence** at the same genes is one of the most important results in contemporary complex-trait genetics
- Demonstrates why **tissue-specific functional data** are required to interpret non-coding associations
- The developmental-pathway enrichment reframes a degenerative disease as partly developmental — a genuinely surprising conclusion
- Directly illustrates how genetics informs **drug repurposing**, connecting the GWAS lecture to therapeutics

## Future Research Directions

- Validate the highest-priority effector genes functionally in cartilage and synovium models, moving from statistical implication to causal demonstration
- Pursue the drug repurposing candidates through preclinical testing, since these are the fastest route to a disease-modifying therapy
- Investigate the unexpected circadian and glial signals mechanistically — do they reflect joint biology, pain processing, or systemic influences?
- Test whether developmental pathway variants act by shaping joint morphology early, using imaging genetics in young cohorts
- Extend to more ancestrally diverse cohorts, since effector gene assignment depends on molecular data that are largely European in origin
