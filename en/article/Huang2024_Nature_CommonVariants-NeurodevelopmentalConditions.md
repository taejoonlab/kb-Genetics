# Examining the role of common variants in rare neurodevelopmental conditions

## Citation (NLM)
Huang QQ, Wigdor EM, Malawsky DS, Campbell P, Samocha KE, Chundru VK, et al. Examining the role of common variants in rare neurodevelopmental conditions. Nature. 2024;636(8042):404-411. doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08217-y

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08217-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08217-y)

---

## Summary

Rare neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs) have a large **Mendelian** component — many are caused by a single damaging *de novo* variant — but common variants also contribute to risk. What has been unclear is **how polygenic risk is distributed** among affected children and their parents, how it **interacts with rare variants**, and whether it acts **directly** through transmitted alleles or **indirectly** through the family environment.

The last distinction is the conceptual heart of the paper. A parent's genotype can affect a child in two ways: by transmitting alleles (a **direct** genetic effect), or by shaping the environment the child grows up in (an **indirect** genetic effect, sometimes called genetic nurture). Both produce a correlation between parental genotype and child phenotype, and standard association analysis cannot separate them. The separation requires **parent–offspring trios**, where transmitted and untransmitted parental alleles can be compared: transmitted alleles carry both direct and indirect effects, while **untransmitted** alleles can only act indirectly.

Working in a large trio-based NDC cohort (the Deciphering Developmental Disorders framework), the authors quantify polygenic background in probands and parents and examine how it relates to rare-variant status. The key structural question is whether children who carry a damaging *de novo* variant also carry elevated polygenic risk, or whether the two are alternative routes to the same clinical presentation — that is, whether rare and common variant liability **add** on the same scale.

The results place polygenic background as a genuine but secondary contributor whose distribution differs depending on whether a monogenic cause has been identified, and they detect a contribution of **indirect genetic effects** — meaning that part of what looks like inherited risk for a neurodevelopmental condition operates through the family environment rather than through the child's own genome.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Dissolves the textbook dichotomy between **Mendelian** and **complex** disease: a "monogenic" condition still has a polygenic background that modifies it
- Introduces **direct versus indirect genetic effects** and the transmitted/untransmitted design that distinguishes them — one of the most important and least taught ideas in modern human genetics
- Explains why **trio designs** are worth their cost: they answer questions population case-control designs structurally cannot
- Highly relevant to genetic counselling, where families ask why an affected child is affected and whether siblings are at risk
- Complements the Litman autism heterogeneity study and the Wright penetrance guidance in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Determine which aspects of the family environment mediate the indirect effects, moving from a statistical decomposition to a mechanism
- Test whether polygenic background predicts phenotypic severity or specific comorbidities among children with the same *de novo* variant
- Extend the trio design to ancestrally diverse cohorts, since polygenic scores transfer poorly and NDC cohorts are largely European
- Assess whether incorporating polygenic background improves recurrence risk estimates given to families
- Examine whether the same direct/indirect structure holds for other conditions where rare and common variation both contribute
