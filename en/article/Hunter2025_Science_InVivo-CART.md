# In vivo CAR T cell generation to treat cancer and autoimmune disease

## Citation (NLM)
Hunter TL, Bao Y, Zhang Y, Matsuda D, Riener R, Wang A, et al. In vivo CAR T cell generation to treat cancer and autoimmune disease. Science. 2025;388(6753):1311. doi:10.1126/science.ads8473

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ads8473](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ads8473)

---

## Summary

**Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies** have transformed the treatment of B cell malignancies. A CAR is a synthetic receptor combining an antibody-derived antigen-binding domain with T cell signalling domains, so an engineered T cell recognizes a surface antigen — typically CD19 — independently of MHC presentation. The transformative clinical results are not in question. The obstacle to broader use is the **manufacturing process**: cells must be collected from the patient by apheresis, shipped to a facility, transduced, expanded over weeks, quality-controlled, and returned, while the patient receives lymphodepleting chemotherapy. The result is a treatment costing hundreds of thousands of dollars, available only at specialized centres, with a delay that some patients do not survive.

This study reports ***in vivo* CAR T cell generation** — delivering the CAR construct directly into the patient so that T cells are engineered **inside the body**, eliminating the entire *ex vivo* manufacturing chain. If it works, a cell therapy becomes something closer to an injectable drug: no apheresis, no facility, no delay, and a cost structure that could make the approach broadly accessible.

The genetic and delivery problem is targeting. The construct must reach T cells specifically, since expressing a CAR in the wrong cell type would be ineffective at best. The authors' platform uses **targeted lipid nanoparticle delivery** — the same class of vehicle used for mRNA vaccines, directed to T cells — to deliver the CAR to T cells *in situ*.

The application extends beyond cancer. CD19-targeted CAR T cells deplete B cells, and B cell depletion has produced striking results in **autoimmune disease** — lupus and related conditions — where autoantibody-producing B cells drive pathology. Autoimmune patients are far more numerous than those with refractory lymphoma, so a scalable manufacturing route matters even more there. The involvement of **Carl June**, whose laboratory developed the first successful CAR T therapies, signals that this is a serious attempt at the next generation of the platform.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows **genetic engineering as therapy** at its most direct: a synthetic receptor gene delivered into a patient's own cells to redirect immune specificity
- Contrasts ***ex vivo* and *in vivo*** gene delivery and makes clear why the distinction determines who can actually receive a treatment
- Introduces **lipid nanoparticle delivery** and cell-type targeting as the central problem in non-viral gene therapy
- Connects cancer immunotherapy to **autoimmune disease** through a shared mechanism, illustrating how one genetic intervention can serve two indications
- Complements the gene therapy sustainability comment in this vault, which identifies manufacturing cost as the central barrier this work attacks

## Future Research Directions

- Establish durability: do in vivo-generated CAR T cells persist and expand comparably to ex vivo manufactured products?
- Determine whether lymphodepleting conditioning can be avoided, since it is a major source of toxicity and cost
- Characterize off-target transduction of non-T cells and its consequences, which is the principal safety question for this delivery route
- Assess whether the approach permits re-dosing, which ex vivo products largely do not
- Extend to solid tumours, where CAR T therapy has been much less successful for reasons unrelated to manufacturing
