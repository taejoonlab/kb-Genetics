# Alternate genetic paths of adaptation to spectinomycin in Escherichia coli

## Citation (NLM)
Jena C, Deolankar S, Matange N. Alternate genetic paths of adaptation to spectinomycin in Escherichia coli. Genetics. 2025;231(3):iyaf069. doi:10.1093/genetics/iyaf069

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf069](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf069)

---

## Summary

Antimicrobial resistance caused an estimated 4.95 million deaths worldwide in 2019. About 73% of all antibiotic use is in livestock, and antibiotics from farms end up **diluted** in soil and water. Dilution matters enormously for evolution. Lethal concentrations only select among **pre-existing** mutants; sub-lethal concentrations permit growth, so mutations can arise *during* exposure, and the **minimum selective concentration** — the lowest concentration that can select a resistant mutant — is often orders of magnitude below the **minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)**. This study asks what *E. coli* actually does when it adapts to low levels of **spectinomycin**, a translation inhibitor used broadly in veterinary medicine.

Using adaptive laboratory evolution, the authors recovered **two distinct adaptive strategies**, separated by a clean operational criterion: both lineages gained fitness in the drug, but only one showed an appreciable increase in MIC.

**Strategy 1 — canonical resistance.** Raising the MIC required **target-site mutations in ribosomal protein S5**, the direct binding target of spectinomycin. This is the textbook resistance mechanism.

**Strategy 2 — adaptation without an MIC change.** Fitness in the drug improved without conventional resistance. This route used both target-associated mutations and, more interestingly, **non-target loci**. The standout was the **multidrug efflux pump MdfA**. Here the result inverts the naive expectation: it was **loss of MdfA**, not its overproduction, that conferred a growth advantage in spectinomycin — the opposite of what an efflux pump story would predict. The authors show mechanistically that this depends on **MdfA's role in proton homeostasis**: MdfA is a proton antiporter, and losing it alters the proton motive force in a way that benefits cells under spectinomycin stress. The lesson is that a gene's annotated function ("efflux pump → exports drugs → resistance") can be the wrong frame for its actual contribution to fitness.

Both strategies were effective against a **natural spectinomycin-producing strain**, showing the laboratory result is relevant to ecological competition, and the two strategies had a **partially overlapping mutational signature**. The most consequential finding is the relationship between them: bacteria adapted at low drug levels were **"primed"** for resistance acquisition when subsequently challenged with high spectinomycin. Low-level, sub-MIC adaptation is therefore not a dead end but a **stepping stone** — it builds a genetic background from which canonical high-level resistance evolves more readily.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A modern, experimentally rigorous illustration of **evolution by natural selection observed in real time** — the core claim of evolutionary genetics made testable in weeks
- Distinguishes **resistance** (MIC increase) from **tolerance/fitness gain** (growth advantage without MIC change), a distinction students and clinicians alike routinely conflate
- Demonstrates **parallel and alternative adaptive paths**: the same selective pressure produces genetically distinct solutions, which is what "the mutational path is contingent" means in practice
- The **MdfA** result is an excellent teaching case for why gene annotation is a hypothesis, not a fact — loss-of-function of an efflux pump increasing drug fitness inverts the expected mechanism
- Public-health and One Health relevance: connects environmental antibiotic pollution directly to a genetic mechanism of resistance evolution

## Future Research Directions

- Test the "priming" result more broadly: does sub-MIC adaptation to other antibiotic classes similarly accelerate later high-level resistance, and is the effect predictable from the mutations acquired?
- Determine the precise mechanism by which altered proton motive force reduces spectinomycin's effect — is it reduced uptake, altered ribosome function, or changed metabolic state?
- Sequence environmental and livestock-associated *E. coli* isolates for the non-target mutations identified here, to test whether these strategies occur outside the laboratory
- Ask whether MdfA loss carries a cost in other environments, which would explain why it is not fixed in nature
- Model the population dynamics of a two-step trajectory (sub-MIC adaptation, then resistance) to estimate how much environmental antibiotic pollution accelerates resistance emergence
