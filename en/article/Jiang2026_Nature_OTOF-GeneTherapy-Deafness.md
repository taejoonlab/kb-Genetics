# Multicentre gene therapy for OTOF-related deafness followed up to 2.5 years

## Citation (NLM)
Jiang L, Cheng X, Lv J, Chen Y, Chen X, Zhai R, et al. Multicentre gene therapy for OTOF-related deafness followed up to 2.5 years. Nature. 2026;653(8107):1170-1180. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10393-y

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10393-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10393-y)

---

## Summary

**Autosomal recessive deafness 9 (DFNB9)**, caused by mutations in ***OTOF***, produces severe-to-complete congenital deafness. *OTOF* encodes **otoferlin**, a protein required for synaptic vesicle release at the inner hair cell ribbon synapse. The genetics make it an unusually favourable gene therapy target: the hair cells themselves are structurally intact and survive, so the defect is a **failure of neurotransmission rather than loss of the sensory cells**. Restoring otoferlin restores function in cells that are still present — unlike most inherited deafness, where the therapeutic problem would be regenerating dead hair cells.

The cochlea is also anatomically favourable: a small, enclosed, fluid-filled compartment that can be reached surgically, so a comparatively small dose of **AAV vector** can transduce a high proportion of target cells with limited systemic exposure. A practical constraint is that *OTOF* exceeds the AAV packaging limit, which is why these therapies use a **dual-AAV** strategy splitting the coding sequence across two vectors that reconstitute the full protein in transduced cells.

This paper reports a **multicentre trial with follow-up to 2.5 years**. Both features matter. Earlier reports of *OTOF* gene therapy described small single-centre cohorts with short follow-up; a multicentre design tests whether results reproduce across surgical teams and sites, and extended follow-up addresses the questions that matter most for a one-time treatment — whether restored hearing is **durable**, whether it continues to improve, and whether delayed adverse effects emerge.

For a congenital sensory deficit, timing is central. Auditory pathway development depends on input during critical periods, so restoring cochlear function does not guarantee normal auditory perception or spoken language if intervention comes too late. Extended follow-up across patients treated at different ages is what allows this to be assessed.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A clinical success story for **AAV gene therapy** in a well-chosen Mendelian disease, with the reasoning for that choice made explicit
- Illustrates why **target selection matters**: intact cells, an enclosed compartment, and a defined molecular defect
- Introduces the **AAV packaging limit** and the dual-vector workaround — a concrete engineering constraint on gene therapy
- Raises **critical periods** in development, connecting molecular therapy to developmental neurobiology
- Complements the gene therapy sustainability comment and the base-editing thalassaemia trial in this vault, covering *in vivo* rather than *ex vivo* delivery

## Future Research Directions

- Continue follow-up beyond 2.5 years to determine whether AAV-driven otoferlin expression persists for life or declines
- Define the age window within which intervention produces normal speech and language outcomes
- Establish whether anti-AAV immunity precludes re-dosing if expression wanes, which is the central limitation of AAV therapy
- Extend the approach to other deafness genes where hair cells survive, and assess which are similarly tractable
- Compare gene therapy with cochlear implantation on functional outcomes, since the implant is the established standard of care
