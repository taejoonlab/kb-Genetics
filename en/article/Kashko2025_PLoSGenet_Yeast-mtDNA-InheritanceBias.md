# Inheritance bias of deletion-harbouring mtDNA in yeast: the role of copy number and intracellular selection

## Citation (NLM)
Kashko ND, Muravyov G, Karavaeva I, Glagoleva ES, Logacheva MD, Garushyants SK, et al. Inheritance bias of deletion-harbouring mtDNA in yeast: The role of copy number and intracellular selection. PLoS Genet. 2025;21(6):e1011737. doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1011737

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011737](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011737)

---

## Summary

Eukaryotic cells carry many copies of mitochondrial DNA, and these copies need not be identical — a state called **heteroplasmy**. Heteroplasmy is linked to mitochondrial disease and to aging. It also creates a hierarchy of biological levels at which natural selection can act: among mtDNA molecules within a cell, among cells within an organism, and among organisms. That hierarchy makes **"selfish" mtDNA** possible: a variant can gain an advantage *within* a cell while being neutral or actively harmful *to* the cell.

*Saccharomyces cerevisiae* is the ideal system for studying this because both parents contribute mitochondria at mating, so heteroplasmic zygotes form naturally. The striking observation the paper starts from: crossing a wild-type (**rho+**) strain with a strain carrying a large mtDNA deletion (**rho−**) can yield **99–100% rho− diploid progeny**. A mutant genome missing essential protein-coding genes — one that cannot support respiration — nearly always wins.

Two mechanisms could explain this, and the paper's contribution is to separate them.
1. **Copy number.** If rho− cells simply carry more mtDNA molecules per cell, then rho− molecules are numerically dominant in the zygote from the start, and the outcome follows without any competitive advantage.
2. **Intracellular fitness.** If rho− mtDNA replicates or is transmitted better inside a heteroplasmic cell, it will displace rho+ over successive generations regardless of the starting ratio.

The authors characterized the genotypes and phenotypes of **22 different rho− strains**. They found that rho− cells do indeed have **higher mtDNA copy number per cell** than rho+ strains — mechanism 1 is real. They then built an ***in silico* model of mtDNA selection and random drift in heteroplasmic yeast cells** and used it to estimate the **intracellular fitness** of each mutant variant. The model shows that **both factors contribute**: the initial numerical advantage from higher copy number *and* a genuine replicative advantage within heteroplasmic cells.

The intuition behind the replicative advantage is instructive: a deleted mtDNA is shorter, and if replication origins are retained while much of the sequence is lost, the molecule can be copied faster. Being smaller is an advantage in the race to replicate, and the fact that the deleted molecule can no longer make a functional respiratory chain is irrelevant to that race. The authors also report that **secondary deletions frequently occur** after the initial one — the process compounds. Drift matters too: mtDNA populations pass through bottlenecks, and the model incorporates random drift alongside selection.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The clearest available demonstration of **selection acting at multiple levels of biological organization** — a variant can be favoured within a cell while harming the cell
- Supports the organellar-genetics lecture: **non-Mendelian inheritance**, heteroplasmy, mitochondrial bottlenecks, and why mtDNA does not follow the rules taught for nuclear genes
- Introduces the **selfish genetic element** concept in a second context (compare meiotic drive), showing that "selfishness" is a general consequence of any hierarchical replication system
- Excellent example of **combining experiment with modelling** to separate two hypotheses that predict the same endpoint — neither dataset alone would have resolved it
- Directly relevant to human mitochondrial disease, where deletion-bearing mtDNA clonally expands in tissues over a lifetime by an analogous mechanism

## Future Research Directions

- Test the replication-origin hypothesis directly by engineering rho− genomes with defined origin content and measuring intracellular fitness
- Determine whether the model's fitness estimates predict outcomes in novel crosses not used to fit it
- Investigate the mechanism of increased mtDNA copy number in rho− cells — is it a regulatory response to respiratory failure, or a passive consequence of smaller genome size?
- Characterize the secondary-deletion process: is it random, or are particular regions repeatedly lost?
- Ask whether the same copy-number-plus-intracellular-fitness framework explains clonal expansion of deleted mtDNA in human tissues and single-cell mitochondrial disease heterogeneity
