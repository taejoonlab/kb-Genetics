# Dual recognition of structurally unrelated mildew effectors underlies the broad-spectrum resistance of Pm3e in wheat

## Citation (NLM)

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72199-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72199-w)

## Summary

Broad-spectrum disease resistance genes are the most valuable resource in sustainable crop protection, because they work against many pathogen strains and are slower to be defeated. Yet the molecular basis of "broadness" is usually unknown. The **Pm3 allelic series** in wheat encodes **NLR receptors** (nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat immune receptors) that recognize **avirulence (AVR) effectors** of wheat powdery mildew. This is a textbook **gene-for-gene** system: a resistance allele confers resistance only against pathogen isolates carrying the matching AVR gene, and the pathogen escapes by losing or altering that AVR.

The *Pm3* series is an unusually good model because its alleles are **near-identical in sequence yet vary greatly in resistance efficacy and breadth**. Sequence similarity means the differences in performance can be attributed to a small number of residues rather than to wholesale divergence. The authors first characterized this variation against a **global mildew isolate collection**, establishing which alleles are broad and which narrow.

Two alleles, ***Pm3d* and *Pm3e***, provide resistance against most isolates worldwide. The mechanistic explanation is the paper's central finding: **each recognizes two AVR genes**, rather than one. Under gene-for-gene logic, a receptor recognizing a single AVR is defeated as soon as the pathogen loses that one gene. A receptor with **dual recognition** requires the pathogen to lose or alter *two* independent effectors simultaneously, which is a far less probable evolutionary event — **thereby lowering the risk of resistance breakdown**. Broad-spectrum resistance here is not a vaguely stronger receptor; it is a receptor with two independent triggers.

Notably, the two recognized effectors are **structurally unrelated**, so the receptor is not simply detecting a conserved motif shared by a protein family. A single NLR with near-identical sequence to its narrow-spectrum siblings can therefore engage two distinct molecular surfaces — an unexpected flexibility in effector recognition.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A modern, mechanistic treatment of the **gene-for-gene** model, one of the foundational concepts in plant genetics
- Explains **durability** of resistance in explicitly evolutionary terms: dual recognition raises the mutational barrier the pathogen must cross
- The **allelic series** design is again the key: near-identical alleles with different phenotypes localize function to few residues
- Directly relevant to agricultural genetics and to the coevolutionary arms race between host and pathogen
- Connects plant immunity to the broader theme of how recognition specificity evolves

## Future Research Directions

- Determine structurally how one NLR engages two unrelated effectors — direct binding at distinct surfaces, or indirect guarding of a shared host target
- Test whether dual recognition can be engineered into narrow-spectrum *Pm3* alleles by transferring the responsible residues
- Monitor global mildew populations for isolates that have escaped both effectors, to measure the real-world durability advantage
- Stack dual-recognition alleles with other resistance genes and model how much this extends durability
- Search other NLR allelic series in crops for dual recognition, to test whether this is a general route to broad-spectrum resistance
