# Rare jackpot individuals drive rapid adaptation in Threespine Stickleback

## Citation (NLM)
Kwakye A, Reid K, Wund MA, Heins DC, Bell MA, Veeramah KR. Rare jackpot individuals drive rapid adaptation in Threespine Stickleback. Nat Commun. 2026;17:2211. doi:10.1038/s41467-026-71236-y

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-71236-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-71236-y)

---

## Summary

Threespine stickleback colonizing freshwater from the sea adapt rapidly and, remarkably, **in parallel** — independent populations converge on the same freshwater phenotypes using the same alleles. The standard explanation is the **transporter hypothesis**: freshwater-adaptive alleles persist at low frequency in the marine population as standing variation, are carried into each new freshwater habitat by colonizing founders, and are then selected up. **Recombination** is central to this account, because it is supposed to assemble the many freshwater-adaptive alleles scattered across different marine individuals into single adapted genomes.

This study tested that mechanism using **whole-genome data from an evolutionary time series** — sampling a real freshwater colonization as it happened, rather than comparing endpoints. Time-series data are what make the difference: they show how allele frequencies actually moved, not merely where they ended up.

The result revises the mechanism. Rapid adaptation did **not** depend on recombination gradually assembling adaptive alleles from many founders. It depended on **a few individuals among the anadromous founders who already carried large haploblocks of freshwater-adaptive alleles** — the authors call these **"jackpot carriers"** — present at low frequency in the marine source population. Adaptation proceeded by amplifying these pre-assembled blocks rather than by building them.

**Biological kinship analysis** reinforced this. Mating among jackpot carriers, and between jackpot carriers and non-carriers, shaped the genomic dynamics of the colonization. The founding event was therefore not a random draw from the marine gene pool followed by selection on independent loci; it was strongly influenced by which few individuals happened to carry the large adaptive blocks and by who they mated with.

The distinction matters for prediction. If adaptation requires assembling alleles by recombination, its speed depends on recombination rate and population size. If it requires the chance inclusion of rare jackpot individuals, it depends on founder sampling — making parallel adaptation partly a matter of which rare individuals happen to be present.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly tests and revises a textbook example of **rapid parallel adaptation from standing variation**
- Distinguishes two mechanisms that produce the same endpoint — **recombination-assembled** versus **pre-assembled haploblocks** — and shows why only time-series data can tell them apart
- Introduces **supergenes and large haploblocks** as units of adaptation, in contrast to independently segregating loci
- Illustrates the role of **chance in founder sampling**, connecting selection to drift and demography
- Complements the recombination and linkage lecture by showing what happens when recombination is *not* the rate-limiting step

## Future Research Directions

- Determine the frequency of jackpot carriers in marine populations, which sets how often parallel adaptation is possible at all
- Establish how the large freshwater haploblocks are maintained in the marine population despite selection against them there — balancing selection, migration, or reduced recombination
- Test whether colonization success predicts jackpot carrier inclusion, using replicate experimental colonizations
- Ask whether the same jackpot mechanism operates in other rapidly adapting systems, or whether stickleback are unusual in having such large pre-assembled blocks
- Model how founder number and jackpot frequency jointly determine adaptation speed, to make the process quantitatively predictable
