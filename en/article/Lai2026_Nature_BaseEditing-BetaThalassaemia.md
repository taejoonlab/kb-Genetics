# Clinical application of base editing for treating β-thalassaemia

## Citation (NLM)
Lai Y, Liu R, Wang L, Ma XK, Li Y, Yang G, et al. Clinical application of base editing for treating β-thalassaemia. Nature. 2026;653(8106):923-932. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10342-9

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10342-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10342-9)

---

## Summary

β-Thalassaemia is caused by reduced or absent production of β-haemoglobin. A therapeutic strategy that bypasses the defective gene entirely is to **reactivate fetal haemoglobin (HbF)**, which is normally silenced after birth: γ-globin can substitute functionally for β-globin, and patients who naturally maintain high HbF have milder disease. The molecular target is well defined — the transcriptional repressor **BCL11A** binds motifs in the ***HBG1* and *HBG2* promoters** to silence γ-globin, so disrupting that binding site de-represses HbF.

The approved CRISPR therapy for this indication works by making a **double-strand break** in a *BCL11A* erythroid enhancer. This study takes a different approach: **base editing**, which chemically converts one base to another **without cutting both DNA strands**. That distinction matters clinically. Double-strand breaks are repaired by error-prone pathways and can produce large deletions, translocations, or chromosome loss; a base editor makes a defined single-base change without generating a break, so the outcome is more predictable and the genotoxic risk profile differs.

The authors had previously performed laboratory-scale electroporation of patient **CD34⁺ haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells** using a **transformer base editor**, targeting the BCL11A binding motif in the *HBG1/HBG2* promoters. Here they report the results of a **phase 1 clinical trial (NCT06024876)** in **five patients** who received autologous CD34⁺ cells modified with that editor — moving from cells in a dish to a treatment administered to people.

The design is *ex vivo* autologous gene editing: the patient's own stem cells are harvested, edited outside the body where editing efficiency and outcome can be verified before infusion, and returned after conditioning. This is the same architecture as approved HSC gene therapies, with base editing substituting for viral gene addition or nuclease-mediated disruption.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A clinical endpoint for the CRISPR material: **base editing in patients**, not in cells or models
- Explains why **base editing** was developed — avoiding double-strand breaks and their unpredictable repair outcomes
- The **HbF reactivation** strategy is an elegant genetics lesson: rather than repairing the broken gene, exploit a silenced paralogue that can substitute
- Illustrates ***ex vivo* autologous editing** and why that design allows verification before the product is given to a patient
- Connects directly to the haematopoiesis review, the somatic editing monitoring review, and the CRISPR off-target Perspective in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Follow the treated patients long term to establish durability of HbF induction and persistence of edited stem cell clones
- Compare base editing directly with nuclease-based editing for the same indication on efficacy and genotoxicity
- Characterize off-target base edits genome-wide, including RNA off-targets, which are specific to deaminase-based editors
- Reduce the burden of conditioning chemotherapy, currently required for engraftment and a major source of toxicity
- Extend the approach to sickle cell disease and assess whether *in vivo* delivery could eventually replace the *ex vivo* workflow
