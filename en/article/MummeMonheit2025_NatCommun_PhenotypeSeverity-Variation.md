# A quadratic paradigm describes the relationship between phenotype severity and variation

## Citation (NLM)
Mumme-Monheit A, Gustafson GE, Hopkins CA, Bailon-Zambrano R, Sucharov J, Lippincott MJ, Way GP, Colborn KL, Nichols JT. A quadratic paradigm describes the relationship between phenotype severity and variation. Nat Commun. 2025;16:8422. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-63316-2

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-63316-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-63316-2)

---

## Summary

In 1942 Waddington observed that mutant animals vary more than wild types — that disrupting a genotype releases phenotypic variation that the wild-type genotype normally suppresses. This became the foundation of the concept of **canalization**: developmental systems are buffered against perturbation, and mutation reveals the buffering by removing it. The straightforward extrapolation is that **more severe mutations should produce more variation**. This paper tests that extrapolation and finds it is only half right.

The experimental system is a zebrafish ***mef2ca* allelic series** spanning a range of craniofacial phenotype severities. An allelic series is essential here: comparing a wild type to a null tells you nothing about the *shape* of the severity–variation relationship, whereas a graded series of alleles lets severity be treated as a continuous variable. The authors quantified craniofacial phenotypes across this series and measured both mean severity and variation.

Severity and variation were **positively correlated — but only up to a point**. In the most severe conditions, **variation collapsed**. Fitting the relationship formally, the best description is not linear but **quadratic**: variation rises with severity, peaks at intermediate severity, and falls again at the extremes. The authors show the same quadratic pattern holds across both zebrafish craniofacial phenotypes and human genetic disease, indicating it is not an artifact of one system.

The interpretation is intuitive once stated. At the wild-type end, buffering suppresses variation. At intermediate severity, buffering is partially lost and the underlying variation is expressed. At the most severe end, the developmental process is so constrained — or so completely broken — that there is little room for outcomes to differ: variation collapses because the phenotype has hit a floor or ceiling. Waddington saw the rising half of the curve; the falling half only becomes visible with a graded allelic series and quantitative phenotyping.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Revisits and corrects a classical observation (**Waddington 1942**) with modern quantitative methods — a good illustration of how a foundational idea gets refined rather than overturned
- Explains **canalization** and **variable expressivity** as measurable quantities, not just descriptive labels
- Shows why an **allelic series** is the right experimental design for characterizing a relationship, rather than a single mutant versus wild type
- Directly relevant to clinical genetics: it predicts that variable expressivity should be greatest for intermediate-severity alleles, which is testable in patient data
- Connects developmental genetics, quantitative genetics, and human disease within one framework

## Future Research Directions

- Identify the mechanism causing variation to collapse at extreme severity — is it a hard developmental limit, or lethality selectively removing the most variable outcomes?
- Test the quadratic prediction prospectively in a different organ system and organism to establish generality
- Determine whether modifier loci explain the peak of variation at intermediate severity, by mapping in a segregating background
- Apply the framework to clinical variant interpretation: can allele severity predict how variable a patient's presentation will be?
- Ask whether the quadratic curve shifts under environmental stress, which classical canalization theory predicts should reduce buffering
