# Rescue of imprinted genes by epigenome editing in human cellular models of Prader-Willi syndrome

## Citation (NLM)
Nemoto A, Imaizumi K, Miya F, Hiroi Y, Yamada M, Ideno H, Saitoh S, Kosaki K, Okuno H, Okano H. Rescue of imprinted genes by epigenome editing in human cellular models of Prader-Willi syndrome. Nat Commun. 2025;16:9714. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-64932-8

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64932-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64932-8)

---

## Summary

**Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS)** is a genomic imprinting disorder caused by loss of function of the **paternal** copy of chromosome **15q11-13**, producing a spectrum of symptoms associated with hypothalamic dysfunction. The genetics of PWS contain an unusual therapeutic opportunity. Patients lack expression of the **paternally expressed genes (PEGs)** in this locus — but they still *possess* an intact copy of those genes on the maternal chromosome. That maternal copy is not deleted or mutated; it is **epigenetically silenced** by DNA methylation at the **PWS imprinting control region (PWS-ICR)**. The information is present and merely switched off. Reactivating the silenced maternal allele is therefore a rational therapeutic target, and one that requires changing an epigenetic mark rather than repairing a sequence.

The authors used a **CRISPR-based epigenome editing system** — catalytically dead Cas9 fused to a demethylating effector, targeted by guide RNAs to the PWS-ICR — in **induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from PWS patients**. Using patient-derived cells matters: the maternal allele carries the patient's own genetic background and imprinting state, so a rescue demonstrated here is a rescue of the actual disease configuration rather than of an engineered model.

**Successful demethylation of the PWS-ICR restored PEG expression from the maternal allele.** This is the central result: an epigenetic edit, without any change to DNA sequence, converted a silent maternal locus into an expressing one, effectively creating a functional second copy of genes the patient had lost.

The approach is conceptually distinct from conventional gene therapy. There is no gene to deliver and no sequence to correct — the therapeutic act is erasing a methylation mark. That makes PWS an unusually clean test case for epigenome editing as a therapeutic modality, and raises the questions that will determine whether it can work in patients: whether demethylation persists through cell division, whether it can be achieved in the hypothalamic neurons that matter, and whether off-target demethylation elsewhere in the genome is tolerable.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The clearest possible demonstration that **imprinting is epigenetic, not genetic** — the gene is intact, only the mark differs, and removing the mark restores expression
- Directly supports the epigenetics lecture with a therapeutic application of **DNA methylation** and **imprinting control regions**
- Introduces **epigenome editing** (dCas9 fused to an effector) as distinct from genome editing, and shows why the distinction matters clinically
- Illustrates the value of **patient-derived iPSCs** for modelling and treating a disease in the correct genetic context
- Connects to the DNA methylation review in this vault, which covers the machinery being manipulated here

## Future Research Directions

- Determine whether restored expression is **heritable through cell division** or decays as maintenance methylation re-establishes the imprint
- Differentiate the edited iPSCs into hypothalamic neurons and test whether the rescue holds in the disease-relevant cell type
- Assess genome-wide off-target demethylation, particularly at other imprinted loci, which would be the most consequential unintended effect
- Solve delivery: epigenome editors are large, and reaching the hypothalamus in vivo is a substantial obstacle
- Test whether the same strategy rescues Angelman syndrome, the reciprocal imprinting disorder at the same locus
