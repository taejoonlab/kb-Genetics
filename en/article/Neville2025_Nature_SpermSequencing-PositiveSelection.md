# Sperm sequencing reveals extensive positive selection in the male germline

## Citation (NLM)
Neville MDC, Lawson ARJ, Sanghvi R, Abascal F, Pham MH, Cagan A, et al. Sperm sequencing reveals extensive positive selection in the male germline. Nature. 2025;647(8084):421-428. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09448-3

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09448-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09448-3)

---

## Summary

Mutations arising in the cell lineages that produce sperm or eggs can be transmitted to offspring. In humans, some mutations do more than arise — they are **positively selected during spermatogenesis**, giving the mutant spermatogonial clone a proliferative advantage so that its descendants come to occupy a growing share of the testis. The consequence is medically striking: such mutations are transmitted far more often than their mutation rate predicts, **increasing the birth prevalence of certain developmental disorders**. This is the mechanism behind "selfish spermatogonial selection," classically demonstrated for Apert syndrome (*FGFR2*) and achondroplasia (*FGFR3*), where paternal-age-related birth prevalence far exceeds what a constant mutation rate would give.

Characterizing how widespread this is has been limited by **sequencing error rates**. The relevant mutations are present in a small fraction of sperm, at frequencies well below conventional sequencing error, so they cannot simply be counted.

The authors used **NanoSeq**, a duplex sequencing method that reads both strands of the original DNA duplex and calls a variant only when both agree, driving the error rate low enough to count somatic and germline mutations directly. They applied it to **81 bulk sperm samples**, measuring the mutational landscape of the male germline at scale rather than inferring it from offspring.

The finding is that positive selection in the male germline is **extensive** — not confined to the handful of classic "selfish" genes but affecting many loci. This changes the expected relationship between mutation and transmission: for a substantial set of genes, the frequency of a variant in sperm is shaped by selection within the testis, not by mutation rate alone. Since selected mutations are concentrated in growth-signalling and developmental genes, the mutations that spermatogonial selection favours are disproportionately those with phenotypic consequences in offspring.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Demonstrates **natural selection acting within an individual's body**, on a cell population, with consequences for the next generation — an unusual and conceptually rich level of selection
- Explains why **paternal age effects** for some disorders exceed what mutation rate alone predicts
- Introduces **duplex sequencing (NanoSeq)** and why ultra-low-error methods were required before this question could be asked
- Connects the mutation lecture to human disease prevalence in a direct, quantitative way
- Forms a trio with the Seplyarskiy and Lawson papers in this vault, all published together on somatic mutation and selection

## Future Research Directions

- Determine the full catalogue of genes under positive selection in the germline and assess how many are associated with developmental disorders
- Quantify how much selected transmission contributes to the population burden of *de novo* disease, relative to unselected mutation
- Test whether selection intensity varies among men, which would create individual differences in transmission risk beyond age
- Establish whether sperm variant allele fraction can be measured clinically to refine recurrence risk counselling
- Compare germline selection with somatic selection in other tissues to ask whether the same genes and pathways are favoured
