# Cancer-independent somatic mutation of the wild-type NF1 allele in normal tissues in neurofibromatosis type 1

## Citation (NLM)
Oliver TRW, Lawson ARJ, Lee-Six H, Tollit A, Jung H, Hooks Y, et al. Cancer-independent somatic mutation of the wild-type NF1 allele in normal tissues in neurofibromatosis type 1. Nat Genet. 2025;57(3):515-521. doi:10.1038/s41588-025-02097-2

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02097-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02097-2)

---

## Summary

Knudson's **two-hit hypothesis** explains recessive tumour predisposition: one allele of a tumour suppressor is mutated in the germline, and a somatic **second hit** inactivates the remaining copy, producing a tumour. The implicit assumption is that a second hit *causes* neoplasia. This study tests that assumption directly and finds it is wrong.

Recent work on normal adult tissues has shown that bona fide cancer driver mutations accumulate with age and mutagen exposure, especially in exposed epithelia, without producing tumours. The authors had also previously observed, in children with malignant rhabdoid tumours, normal tissues sharing a genetic ancestor with a nearby tumour and carrying the same somatic *SMARCB1* hit yet remaining histologically normal. They therefore asked whether second hits occur in normal tissues of predisposed individuals **independently of any tumour lineage and without hypermutation**.

**Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)** is a multisystem cancer predisposition syndrome caused by constitutive **monoallelic *NF1* inactivation** in the germline. The authors performed **whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing on 479 tissue biopsies from a single child with NF1**. The scale and design are the key: many biopsies from one individual, targeting histologically **normal** tissue rather than tumours.

They identified **multiple independent *NF1* driver variants in histologically normal tissues**. As a control, no equivalent findings appeared in **610 biopsies from two children without predisposition** — establishing that this is specific to the NF1 germline background, not a general feature of somatic mutation. They then corroborated the result with **targeted duplex sequencing** (which suppresses sequencing error well enough to detect very low-frequency variants) in a further **nine adults with the same syndrome**.

The central finding is that **truncating *NF1* mutations are under positive selection in normal tissues** of people with NF1. Cells that lose the second *NF1* copy have a proliferative or survival advantage and expand clonally — but remain phenotypically normal tissue, not tumour. So the second hit is **necessary but not sufficient** for transformation. The clinical implication the authors draw is that the **extent and pattern of biallelic *NF1* loss across normal tissues may underpin the syndrome's cancer phenotype** — the tissue distribution of second hits could explain which tissues in NF1 give rise to tumours and why the disease affects so many organ systems.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly examines and revises **Knudson's two-hit hypothesis**, one of the foundational models in cancer genetics
- Demonstrates **positive selection acting within a normal human tissue** — somatic evolution measured in a living person, not a tumour
- Distinguishes **necessary from sufficient** causation with data, a distinction students often state but rarely see tested
- Introduces **duplex sequencing** as a technique for detecting mutations at frequencies below normal sequencing error
- The 479-versus-610 biopsy design is an excellent illustration of how a well-chosen control makes an observation interpretable

## Future Research Directions

- Determine what additional events are required to convert an *NF1*-null normal clone into a neurofibroma or malignancy
- Map the tissue distribution and clone sizes of second hits across all affected organ systems to test whether they predict tumour site
- Test whether the same phenomenon occurs in other recessive predisposition syndromes (*TP53*, *RB1*, *BRCA1/2*, *SMARCB1*)
- Assess whether second-hit burden in accessible tissue could serve as a biomarker for cancer risk in NF1 patients
- Investigate why *NF1*-null cells are positively selected in normal tissue — which pathway confers the fitness advantage without transformation
