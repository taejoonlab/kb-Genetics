# Complete human recombination maps

## Citation (NLM)
Palsson G, Hardarson MT, Jonsson H, Steinthorsdottir V, Stefansson OA, Eggertsson HP, et al. Complete human recombination maps. Nature. 2025;639(8054):700-707. doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08450-5

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08450-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08450-5)

## Summary

Human recombination maps underpin association and linkage studies and are essential to inferences about population history and natural selection. Every existing map, however, is built **solely from crossovers (COs)** — the reciprocal exchanges that are visible as recombinant haplotypes in pedigrees. This omits the other, and **more common**, product of meiotic recombination: **non-crossovers (NCOs)**, in which a programmed double-strand break is repaired using the homologue as template, producing a short **gene conversion** tract without exchanging the flanking chromosome arms. NCOs have been left out not because they are unimportant but because they are hard to detect: a conversion tract is typically only tens to hundreds of base pairs long, and detecting one requires an informative marker inside the tract in a specific parent–offspring transmission.

Working with the deCODE Icelandic resource — **whole-genome sequence data in large families**, where transmissions can be followed directly — the authors estimated the **number of NCOs transmitted from parent to offspring** and used them to derive **complete, sex-specific recombination maps including both NCOs and COs**. Sex specificity is essential because male and female meiosis differ profoundly: females have far more crossovers overall, and the two sexes place them differently along the chromosome.

The maps change the picture in ways that matter beyond bookkeeping. Because NCOs are more numerous than COs but transfer sequence over very short intervals, adding them alters the fine-scale **genetic map**, the inferred relationship between physical and genetic distance, and estimates of how much sequence is actually exchanged per meiosis. It also affects **GC-biased gene conversion**, which acts through conversion tracts and therefore scales with NCO number rather than CO number — meaning a map built only from COs systematically misrepresents this evolutionary force.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Corrects a simplification present in essentially every genetics course: recombination is usually taught as crossing over, and **non-crossovers are the more common outcome**
- Makes **gene conversion** concrete and quantitative rather than a footnote to double-strand break repair
- Explains the **sex difference** in recombination, a striking and under-taught feature of human meiosis
- Shows why **large sequenced pedigrees** remain irreplaceable — this measurement is impossible from population data alone
- Directly complements the recombination hotspot review and the Carioscia meiosis-gene study in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Determine whether NCO and CO rates are independently regulated, or whether the CO/NCO decision is made downstream of a common DSB pool
- Quantify the revised contribution of GC-biased gene conversion to genome base composition using NCO-inclusive maps
- Test whether individual variation in NCO rate is heritable and whether it associates with aneuploidy risk, as CO variation does
- Re-run population-genetic inferences that depend on recombination maps — selection scans, demographic inference, LD-based methods — with the complete maps
- Extend complete mapping to other populations, since the Icelandic resource may not represent global recombination variation
