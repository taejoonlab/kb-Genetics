# Solid phase transitions as a solution to the genome folding paradox

## Citation (NLM)
Pulupa J, McArthur NG, Stathi O, Wang M, Zazhytska M, Pirozzolo ID, Nayar A, Shapiro L, Lomvardas S. Solid phase transitions as a solution to the genome folding paradox. Nature. 2025;643(8072):820-829. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09043-6

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09043-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09043-6)

---

## Summary

Ultra-long-range genomic contacts are central to neuronal genome architecture, and they present a genuine biochemical puzzle. Regulatory DNA elements make **selective and stable contacts with sequences hundreds of kilobases away**, while *not* interacting with much closer sequences occupied by **the exact same transcription factors**. Simple affinity-based models cannot explain this: if binding is driven by the factors present, proximal sites with identical factors should out-compete distal ones. Something makes distant, specific contacts more stable than nearby, equivalent ones.

The clearest example is **olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs)**. Each OSN expresses a single olfactory receptor (OR) gene, chosen from over a thousand, and this choice depends on **multi-chromosomal enhancer hubs**: only a fraction of the sites bound by **LHX2, EBF1 and LDB1** converge into these highly selective hubs, which then activate one OR allele. The selectivity is extreme and the mechanism has been unexplained.

The authors took the problem into a test tube. They **assembled OR enhancer hubs in vitro with recombinant proteins**, reconstituting the system biochemically rather than inferring its properties from nuclear imaging. This is the methodological move that makes the paper: it converts a cell-biological observation into a question about the physical chemistry of a defined mixture.

Their answer is that hub formation is a **solid phase transition** rather than the liquid–liquid phase separation that has dominated recent thinking about nuclear condensates. The distinction matters mechanistically. Liquid condensates are dynamic, exchange components rapidly, and would not confer the observed selectivity or stability — components would sample many partners. A **solid-like** assembly is kinetically trapped: once formed it does not readily exchange, which explains both why hubs are stable over long times and why they are selective. Selectivity here is not thermodynamic preference but the persistence of whichever contacts formed first.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Explains **long-range enhancer–promoter specificity**, a problem that the standard looping model does not resolve
- Introduces **biomolecular condensates and phase transitions** in the nucleus, and importantly distinguishes liquid from solid behaviour — a distinction usually blurred
- **Olfactory receptor choice** is the most extreme case of monogenic, monoallelic expression known, and a superb example for the gene regulation lecture
- Demonstrates the value of **in vitro reconstitution**: taking a nuclear phenomenon apart and rebuilding it from purified components
- Connects gene regulation to physical chemistry, showing that some regulatory specificity is a materials property rather than a coding one

## Future Research Directions

- Determine what molecular features drive solid rather than liquid behaviour — which domains, interactions, or valencies are responsible
- Test in vivo whether disrupting the solid-like state randomizes olfactory receptor choice, which would establish necessity
- Ask how a kinetically trapped assembly is ever disassembled, since neurons must establish hubs during development and maintain them for life
- Determine whether other long-range regulatory systems, such as immunoglobulin or protocadherin loci, use the same physical mechanism
- Reassess nuclear condensates generally for solid-like behaviour, since the field has largely assumed liquid properties
