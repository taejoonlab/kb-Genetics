# Escherichia coli with a 57-codon genetic code

## Citation (NLM)
Robertson WE, Rehm FBH, Spinck M, Schumann RL, Tian R, Liu W, et al. Escherichia coli with a 57-codon genetic code. Science. 2025;390(6771):351. doi:10.1126/science.ady4368

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ady4368](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ady4368)

---

## Summary

All life on Earth uses the **universal genetic code**, or minimal variations of it: 64 codons specifying 20 amino acids plus stop, with extensive **redundancy** — most amino acids are encoded by several synonymous codons. That redundancy is not neutral. Synonymous codon choice influences gene expression, co-translational folding, and protein levels, with effects that differ by sequence context.

Emerging methods for **total genome synthesis** make it possible to explore genome sequences radically different from anything natural evolution has produced. If an organism's genome is written rather than inherited, redundant codons can be systematically removed: every instance of a chosen codon replaced by a synonym across the entire genome, and the corresponding tRNA and release factor deleted. The codon is then **unassigned** — it no longer appears, and nothing reads it.

This study reports an ***E. coli*** with a **57-codon genetic code**: seven codons removed from the genome-wide coding repertoire. This is a substantial extension of previous work, where genome-wide compression had reached fewer reassignments.

Two consequences make this more than a technical demonstration. First, **freed codons can be reassigned** to non-canonical amino acids, so the organism can incorporate synthetic building blocks at defined positions using codons that no longer have a natural meaning — with seven freed codons, multiple distinct non-canonical amino acids can be encoded simultaneously. Second, an organism whose code differs from the universal one is **genetically isolated**: bacteriophages arriving with universally encoded genomes cannot be translated correctly, conferring broad **phage resistance**, and horizontally transferred genes cannot function, providing a **biocontainment** barrier against escape of engineered sequences.

The result is a direct experimental statement about the genetic code: its redundancy is **not required**, and the code as we find it is a frozen accident that can be rewritten.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The most direct possible engagement with the **genetic code and its redundancy** — testing whether synonymous codons are needed by removing them
- Demonstrates **whole-genome synthesis** as a mature technology, and what becomes possible when a genome is designed rather than edited
- Explains **codon reassignment** and non-canonical amino acid incorporation, extending the central dogma into synthetic biology
- Introduces **genetic isolation** as an engineered property with two useful consequences — phage resistance and biocontainment
- Complements the synonymous mutations review in this vault: one argues synonymous codons matter functionally, the other removes them entirely

## Future Research Directions

- Determine the fitness cost of recoding and whether it can be relieved by laboratory evolution
- Encode multiple distinct non-canonical amino acids simultaneously to synthesize polymers unavailable to natural biology
- Test the robustness of phage resistance against phages given the opportunity to evolve countermeasures
- Evaluate biocontainment rigorously, since the barrier depends on recoded genes being non-functional in natural organisms
- Establish how many codons can be removed before translation fails, defining the minimum viable genetic code
