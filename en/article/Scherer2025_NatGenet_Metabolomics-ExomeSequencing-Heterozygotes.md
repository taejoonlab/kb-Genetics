# Coupling metabolomics and exome sequencing reveals graded effects of rare damaging heterozygous variants on gene function and human traits

## Citation (NLM)
Scherer N, Fässler D, Borisov O, Cheng Y, Schlosser P, Wuttke M, et al. Coupling metabolomics and exome sequencing reveals graded effects of rare damaging heterozygous variants on gene function and human traits. Nat Genet. 2025;57(1):193-205. doi:10.1038/s41588-024-01965-7

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01965-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01965-7)

---

## Summary

Human metabolism is maintained by thousands of enzymes and transporters. When one fails severely, the result is an **inborn error of metabolism (IEM)** — rare, usually early-onset, usually autosomal recessive. IEMs have identified many metabolite-related genes, but the approach is limited by the tiny number of people homozygous for such variants. GWAS provides the opposite trade-off: thousands of common variants associated with metabolite levels across large populations, but with unknown functional effects and effect sizes too modest for direct clinical use.

This study occupies the middle ground using **gene-based rare variant aggregation testing**. Rather than testing single rare variants, which are individually too infrequent for power, all rare putatively damaging variants in a gene are collapsed into one test. Applied to whole-exome sequencing data against **1,294 plasma and 1,396 urine metabolites**, this discovered **235 gene–metabolite associations**, many previously unreported. Testing both plasma and urine matters: a transporter defect may leave plasma levels normal while changing urinary excretion dramatically, and the two fluids together localize where in the body a process fails.

The paper's central conceptual claim is that **studying rare damaging variants in the heterozygous state supports inferences concordant with those from inborn errors of metabolism**. In other words, a single damaged copy produces a milder, measurable version of the biochemical phenotype seen in the homozygous IEM patient — heterozygotes are not silent carriers but partially affected individuals whose biochemistry is shifted. The authors supported this with three orthogonal lines of evidence: genetic (allelic series and dose-response), computational (**in silico gene knockouts in whole-body models of human metabolism**, which predict the metabolic consequence of removing a reaction), and one experimental proof of principle.

The worked example is **transcellular sulfate reabsorption**, mediated by the transporters **SLC13A1** and **SLC26A1**. Allelic series of functional variants in these genes showed **graded effects on plasma sulfate** — the more damaging the variant, the lower the sulfate — and, strikingly, **graded effects on human height**. The same alleles were associated with increased odds of diverse **musculoskeletal traits and diseases** in the population. Sulfate is required for sulfation of extracellular matrix proteoglycans in cartilage and bone, so a transporter defect propagates from a biochemical measurement to a visible anthropometric trait to clinical disease.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Dissolves the textbook boundary between **rare Mendelian disease and common complex traits**: the same gene, with variants of graded severity, produces an IEM at one extreme and a shift in height at the other
- Demonstrates **allelic series** and **dose-dependence** in humans, concepts usually only illustrated in model organisms
- Reframes **heterozygous carriers** of recessive disease alleles as measurably affected, which has implications for carrier screening and counselling
- Shows how **intermediate molecular phenotypes** (metabolites) bridge genotype and organismal phenotype far more directly than disease endpoints
- Introduces rare-variant **burden/aggregation testing**, the standard method for exome-based association, as a contrast to single-variant GWAS

## Future Research Directions

- Extend rare-variant metabolite association to larger biobanks with paired plasma and urine metabolomics to increase power for the many genes still undetected
- Functionally validate additional gene–metabolite associations experimentally, since only one proof of principle was performed here
- Test whether heterozygous carriers of other IEM genes show measurable, clinically relevant phenotypes, which would change how carrier status is interpreted
- Determine whether the sulfate–height relationship is causal using Mendelian randomization, and whether sulfate supplementation affects musculoskeletal outcomes
- Improve whole-body metabolic models by feeding back the empirical gene–metabolite associations discovered here
