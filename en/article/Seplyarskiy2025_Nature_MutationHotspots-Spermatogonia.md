# Hotspots of human mutation point to clonal expansions in spermatogonia

## Citation (NLM)
Seplyarskiy V, Moldovan MA, Koch E, Kar P, Neville MDC, Rahbari R, Sunyaev S. Hotspots of human mutation point to clonal expansions in spermatogonia. Nature. 2025;647(8084):429-435. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09579-7

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09579-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09579-7)

---

## Summary

In renewing tissues, a mutation conferring a selective advantage produces a clonal expansion. Spermatogonia are a renewing tissue with a distinctive property: **mutations driving clonal expansion in spermatogonia (CES) are also transmitted to the next generation**. The consequence is an **effective increase in the *de novo* mutation rate** for CES driver genes — a variant that expands its clone in the testis appears in offspring far more often than its intrinsic mutation rate would predict. CES was originally discovered through the extreme recurrence of *de novo* mutations causing **Apert syndrome**, where a handful of *FGFR2* positions mutate at apparent rates orders of magnitude above the genome average.

This paper turns that observation into a systematic discovery method. Rather than sequencing testis tissue, the authors reason that CES drivers should appear as **hotspots of human *de novo* mutation** — positions or genes where *de novo* variants recur far more often than expected. Detecting such hotspots requires enormous numbers of transmissions, and the authors assemble them: **54,715 trios ascertained for rare conditions**, **6,065 control trios**, and population variation from **807,162 mostly healthy individuals**. The disease-ascertained and control trios together allow hotspots to be distinguished from ascertainment artifacts, and the population data provide the background expectation.

Their analysis identifies genes manifesting *de novo* mutation rates well above expectation, nominating CES drivers genome-wide rather than one syndrome at a time. The approach is elegant because it detects selection occurring in the father's testis using only data from children — the signature of a cellular process is recovered from transmission statistics.

The implication is that a subset of *de novo* disease mutations arise not because those positions are mutationally fragile but because the resulting cells outcompete their neighbours in the testis. Mutation rate and selection are conflated in the observed data, and separating them changes how recurrence and paternal age effects should be interpreted.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows that an apparent **mutation hotspot** may reflect **selection**, not elevated mutability — a distinction that is easy to state and hard to make
- Demonstrates inference across biological levels: a cellular process in the testis detected purely from population transmission data
- Explains why some *de novo* disease mutations recur far more often than mutation rate predicts
- Illustrates the value of very large trio collections and of pairing disease-ascertained with control cohorts
- Published alongside the Neville sperm-sequencing paper in this vault, which approaches the same phenomenon by direct measurement — a good example of convergent evidence

## Future Research Directions

- Validate the nominated CES drivers by direct deep sequencing of testis or sperm, closing the loop with the direct-measurement approach
- Determine what proportion of severe *de novo* developmental disorders is attributable to CES rather than to baseline mutation
- Test whether CES driver genes converge on particular signalling pathways, as the classic examples in RTK/RAS signalling suggest
- Model how CES alters expected recurrence risk within families, which currently assumes mutation-rate-driven recurrence
- Ask whether analogous transmitted clonal selection occurs in the female germline, where the cell biology is fundamentally different
