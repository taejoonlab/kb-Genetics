# Heritability of intrinsic human life span is about 50% when confounding factors are addressed

## Citation (NLM)
Shenhar B, Pridham G, Lopes De Oliveira T, Raz N, Yang Y, Deelen J, Hägg S, Alon U. Heritability of intrinsic human life span is about 50% when confounding factors are addressed. Science. 2026;391(6774):504. doi:10.1126/science.adz1187

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz1187](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz1187)

---

## Summary

How heritable is human lifespan? The answer determines how much effort to invest in finding longevity genes. Current estimates are discouraging: **twin studies give heritability of only 20–25%**, and recent large pedigree studies suggest it may be **as low as 6%**. Those figures have been taken to mean that lifespan is overwhelmingly environmental and that longevity genetics has limited scope.

The authors argue these estimates are **confounded by extrinsic mortality** — deaths from accidents, infections, violence, and other causes unrelated to the biology of ageing. The argument is a clean statistical one. Heritability is the proportion of **phenotypic variance** explained by genetic variance. Extrinsic deaths add variance to observed lifespan that has essentially no genetic component, inflating the denominator. Heritability is therefore **diluted** — not because genes matter little for ageing, but because the measured phenotype is a mixture of two processes, only one of which is under genetic control. The problem is worse in historical cohorts, where extrinsic mortality was high, and those are exactly the cohorts with complete lifespan data.

Using **mathematical modelling** together with analyses of **twin cohorts raised together and apart**, the authors correct for extrinsic mortality and estimate the heritability of lifespan attributable to **intrinsic mortality** — death from the ageing process itself. Twins raised apart are the key comparison, since they separate shared genes from shared family environment. The corrected estimate is **above 50%**, comparable to the heritability of most other complex human traits, and two to eight times the uncorrected figures.

The implication reverses the standard conclusion. The biology of ageing is substantially genetically determined; what obscured this was that people also die of things that have nothing to do with ageing.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A precise, instructive lesson in what **heritability** actually measures — a variance ratio, sensitive to everything that contributes variance, not an intrinsic property of a trait
- Demonstrates that **phenotype definition determines the answer**: "lifespan" and "intrinsic lifespan" are different traits with very different heritabilities
- Explains why **twins raised apart** remain uniquely informative
- Directly relevant to the ageing and complex traits material, and to the Diversity Outbred mouse lifespan study in this vault, where controlled environments avoid this confounder entirely
- Should be read alongside the evolutionary genetics of ageing review in this vault, which assumes the low heritability figure

## Future Research Directions

- Re-power longevity GWAS using intrinsic-mortality-adjusted phenotypes, which should substantially increase discovery
- Validate the correction in modern cohorts where extrinsic mortality is low, providing an independent test
- Determine whether the same confounding deflates heritability estimates for other late-life traits
- Examine whether genetic effects concentrate on specific causes of intrinsic mortality rather than on overall ageing rate
- Reconcile the revised human estimate with model organism longevity genetics, where heritability has always appeared much higher
