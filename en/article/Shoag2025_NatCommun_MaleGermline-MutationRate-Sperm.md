# Direct measurement of the male germline mutation rate in individuals using sequential sperm samples

## Citation (NLM)
Shoag JE, Srinivasa A, Loh CA, Liu MH, Lassen E, Melanaphy S, et al. Direct measurement of the male germline mutation rate in individuals using sequential sperm samples. Nat Commun. 2025;16:2077. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-57507-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57507-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57507-0)

---

## Summary

Mutations accumulating in the male germline with age are a major source of genetic diversity and a major contributor to new genetic disease. The paternal age effect is one of the most robust findings in human genetics — older fathers transmit more *de novo* mutations. But the standard way of measuring it is **indirect**: sequence parent–offspring trios, count the *de novo* mutations in the child, and regress against paternal age at conception. That gives a population-level slope, not a measurement in any individual, and it confounds germline mutation with post-zygotic events in the embryo.

This study measures the rate **directly, in sperm, within individual men, over time**. The design is the elegant part: the authors **recalled 23 sperm donors who had banked samples years earlier** and asked them to provide new samples. The old and new samples from the same man were separated by **10 to 33 years**. Each donor therefore serves as his own control, and the difference between his two samples is the mutation accumulated in his own germline over a known interval — an internally controlled longitudinal measurement rather than a cross-sectional inference.

The technical obstacle is that *de novo* mutations in sperm are present at extremely low frequency, far below the error rate of standard sequencing. The authors used **high-fidelity duplex sequencing**, which independently reads both strands of the same original DNA molecule and calls a mutation only when both strands agree. Errors introduced during library preparation or sequencing appear on one strand only and are discarded, pushing the error rate low enough that genuine somatic and germline mutations can be counted. The paper demonstrates that direct high-fidelity sequencing of sperm is a viable route to measuring germline mutation rates at the level of the individual.

The design opens up questions that trio studies cannot address: how much do men differ from one another in germline mutation rate, is an individual's rate stable over decades, and does it respond to exposures or health status?

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Converts the **paternal age effect** from a population regression into a direct, longitudinal measurement in individuals
- Explains why **duplex sequencing** exists and what problem it solves — detecting variants below the sequencing error floor
- Illustrates a superb study design: **the same person sampled twice, decades apart**, removing between-individual confounding entirely
- Directly supports the mutation lecture and complements the dog *de novo* mutation study in this vault, which uses the trio approach
- Raises the possibility of **individual variation in mutation rate**, which matters for reproductive counselling and for models of disease risk

## Future Research Directions

- Expand the cohort to establish how much men genuinely differ in germline mutation rate, and whether the difference is heritable
- Test whether environmental exposures, chemotherapy, or disease measurably alter an individual's sperm mutation rate
- Compare sperm mutation spectra with the *de novo* mutation spectra observed in that man's own children, to determine how faithfully sperm predicts transmission
- Determine whether selection acts between the sperm measurement and successful fertilization, which would decouple the two rates
- Apply the same longitudinal, internally controlled design to oocytes, which is far harder but where the aneuploidy-relevant biology differs fundamentally
