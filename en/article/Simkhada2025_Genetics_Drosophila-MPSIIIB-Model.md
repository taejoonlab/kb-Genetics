# A Drosophila model of mucopolysaccharidosis IIIB

## Citation (NLM)
Simkhada B, Nazario-Yepiz NO, Freymuth PS, Lyman RA, Shankar V, Wiggins K, et al. A Drosophila model of mucopolysaccharidosis IIIB. Genetics. 2025;229(3):iyae219. doi:10.1093/genetics/iyae219

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyae219](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyae219)

---

## Summary

Mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIB (MPS IIIB, Sanfilippo syndrome type B) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder affecting roughly 1 in 100,000 births. It is caused by loss-of-function mutations in *NAGLU*, which encodes alpha-N-acetylglucosaminidase — the lysosomal enzyme that removes terminal α-N-acetylglucosamine from the non-reducing end of the glycosaminoglycan **heparan sulfate (HS)**. HS is degraded stepwise in the lysosome; block any step and partially degraded HS accumulates. Over 224 *NAGLU* mutations are known, mostly point mutations. Affected children appear normal at birth, then show language and developmental delay at 3–5 years followed by progressive, severe central nervous system degeneration. Why lysosomal HS accumulation destroys neurons — the causal chain from enzyme defect to neurodegeneration — has not been established.

The authors built a *Drosophila melanogaster* model using the functional *NAGLU* ortholog, *Naglu*, and deliberately made **three allele types that mirror the classes of mutation seen in patients**: a deletion (*Naglu^KO*), a missense allele (*Naglu^Y160C*), and a nonsense/truncating allele (*Naglu^W422X*). All were placed in a common genetic background, so allele-to-allele differences reflect the mutations themselves rather than background variation.

The mutants reproduce the core cell-biological and behavioral features of the disease. Enzyme activity was significantly reduced in both sexes and HS levels rose. Lysotracker staining of mutant brains and ventral nerve cords showed a marked increase in acidic compartments — fluorescent puncta consistent with swollen, HS-engorged lysosomes and a disrupted autophagic pathway. Using the *Drosophila* Activity Monitoring system, the authors found **hyperactivity and sleep defects**: reduced total night sleep with increased sleep fragmentation, and elevated daytime activity with no sleep rebound. These effects were **sex- and age-dependent**, a pattern typical of *Drosophila* phenotypes generally (as seen for longevity, sleep, and starvation resistance). Notably, allele severity tracked mutation type: the deletion and the truncation had large effects, while the missense allele produced milder sleep and activity phenotypes — an allelic series, in the classical genetic sense.

RNA sequencing of mutant brains identified differentially expressed genes enriched for **nervous system development** processes. Building a genetic interaction network from known interaction partners of these genes revealed **two major subnetworks**: one enriched for synaptic function, the other for neurodevelopmental processes. The authors interpret this as evidence that failure to break down HS perturbs the steady state of **intracellular vesicle transport** broadly, including the vesicles used in synaptic transmission — a mechanistic link from a lysosomal enzyme deficiency to neuronal dysfunction. The model did not show late-onset locomotor loss or shortened lifespan, which the authors attribute to the fly's short lifespan relative to the decades over which human symptoms develop.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A clean, current example of **disease modeling in a model organism**: take a human disease gene, find the ortholog, engineer patient-equivalent mutations, and ask what breaks
- Illustrates an **allelic series** (null vs truncation vs missense) and why generating multiple allele types in one genetic background is the right experimental design — severity differences become interpretable
- Demonstrates why **evolutionary conservation** is the licence for model-organism work: HS degradation and lysosomal function are ancient enough that a fly can inform a human disease
- Shows the limits of a model as clearly as its strengths — the fly's short lifespan means late-onset human phenotypes simply cannot appear, which is a lesson about model choice, not a failure
- Connects the lysosome/cell-biology material to behavior genetics (sleep, activity) and to transcriptomics/network analysis in a single study

## Future Research Directions

- Use the fly model for **therapeutic screening**: enzyme replacement, substrate reduction, chaperone rescue of the *Naglu^Y160C* missense protein, or gene therapy, read out on the sleep/activity and Lysotracker phenotypes
- Determine whether the synaptic and neurodevelopmental subnetworks are causally required — knock down hub genes and ask whether behavioral phenotypes are rescued or worsened
- Distinguish developmental from degenerative contributions by restricting *Naglu* loss temporally with conditional expression systems
- Test whether the vesicle-transport defect is cell-autonomous by rescuing *Naglu* in specific neuronal or glial populations
- Extend the same allele-series strategy to the other MPS III subtypes (A, C, D) in flies to ask whether all four converge on the same network
