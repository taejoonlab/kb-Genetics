# Dominant contribution of Asgard archaea to eukaryogenesis

## Citation (NLM)
Tobiasson V, Luo J, Wolf YI, Koonin EV. Dominant contribution of Asgard archaea to eukaryogenesis. Nature. 2026;650(8091):141-149. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09960-6

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09960-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09960-6)

---

## Summary

Two findings constrain any scenario for the origin of eukaryotes. First, the **last eukaryotic common ancestor (LECA) already contained the mitochondrion**, an endosymbiotic organelle derived from an alphaproteobacterium. Second, the **Asgard archaea** were discovered to be the closest archaeal relatives of eukaryotes. Together these establish that eukaryotes arose from a partnership between an Asgard archaeal host and an alphaproteobacterial endosymbiont. What remains contested is the **relative contribution** of each partner to the genes and cellular systems that LECA possessed.

The authors address this by tracing **the phylogenetic origin of each core eukaryotic gene** that can be assigned to LECA, within a **rigorous statistical framework centred on evolutionary hypothesis testing using constrained phylogenetic trees**. The methodology matters here more than usual. Gene trees for ancient genes are noisy, and it is easy to assign origins by eye or by best-tree topology and obtain whatever answer one's prior favours. Constrained-tree hypothesis testing instead asks explicitly whether the data significantly reject an archaeal origin versus a bacterial one for each gene family — converting origin assignment from an inspection exercise into a statistical test.

The result is a **dominant contribution of Asgard archaea to the origin of most conserved eukaryotic functional systems and pathways**, with a **limited contribution from Alphaproteobacteria**. The mitochondrial ancestor contributed the organelle and a set of associated functions, but the bulk of eukaryotic cellular machinery — the systems underlying information processing, membrane traffic, cytoskeleton, and regulation — traces to the archaeal host lineage rather than to the endosymbiont or to other bacteria.

This is a substantive position in an active dispute. Other analyses, including the companion paper by Bernabeu and colleagues in the same period, emphasize **multiple waves of horizontal gene transfer from diverse bacteria** during eukaryogenesis. Reading the two together is instructive: the disagreement is less about the data than about how confidently ancient gene origins can be assigned, and what statistical standard should be applied.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Addresses the composition of the first eukaryote directly: which partner supplied which parts
- Excellent illustration that **method determines conclusion** in deep phylogenetics — the constrained-tree testing framework is the paper's real argument
- Reinforces **horizontal gene transfer** and **endosymbiosis** as the two mechanisms by which genomes acquire foreign genes
- Places **Asgard archaea** in the tree of life lecture as the closest prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes
- Best read against the Bernabeu paper in this vault, which reaches a different emphasis from similar data — a live scientific disagreement students can evaluate

## Future Research Directions

- Apply the constrained-tree testing framework to additional gene families and assess how many origins can be assigned with statistical confidence versus how many remain ambiguous
- Determine whether disagreement with HGT-emphasizing analyses stems from gene sampling, tree model, or the statistical threshold applied
- Incorporate additional Asgard genomes and cultured representatives as they become available, since the archaeal side of the comparison is still expanding
- Test whether functional categories differ systematically in origin, which would indicate that the two partners contributed distinct cellular modules
- Combine gene-origin assignment with the duplication-dating approach to build a single reconstruction that is both compositional and chronological
