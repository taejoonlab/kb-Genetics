# Three open questions in polygenic score portability

## Citation (NLM)
Wang JY, Lin N, Zietz M, Mares J, Smith OS, Rathouz PJ, Harpak A. Three open questions in polygenic score portability. Nat Commun. 2026;17:1204. doi:10.1038/s41467-026-68565-3

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-68565-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-68565-3)

---

## Summary

Polygenic scores (PGS) predict poorly in people who differ from the GWAS sample used to build them, and the standard account attributes this to **genetic ancestry**. This paper tests that account directly by measuring PGS prediction accuracy as a **continuous function of an individual's genome-wide genetic dissimilarity to the GWAS sample** — "genetic distance" — rather than by comparing discrete ancestry groups. Treating the variable continuously is the methodological move that makes the three findings possible.

**First: genetic distance is a weak predictor of individual accuracy.** Variation in how well a PGS predicts one person's phenotype is only weakly explained by how genetically distant that person is from the training sample. Strikingly, it is explained **comparably well by socioeconomic measures**. This does not mean ancestry is irrelevant, but it does mean the standard framing is incomplete: a substantial part of what is called "portability" is not about genetics at all, and PGS accuracy is entangled with environment and social context in ways the ancestry framing obscures.

**Second: portability trends differ by trait.** There is no single portability curve. For several **immunity-related traits**, prediction accuracy **drops to near zero even at intermediate genetic distances** — a far steeper decay than for other traits. The authors suggest this may reflect **fast evolutionary turnover of immunity-associated variants**, which are among the strongest targets of natural selection and of population-specific pathogen pressure. Where the underlying genetic architecture itself differs between populations, no amount of statistical correction will transfer a score.

**Third: even qualitative expectations fail in places.** The paper documents departures from the assumed monotonic relationship between distance and accuracy, indicating the phenomenon is not merely quantitatively mis-estimated but sometimes qualitatively different from the standard model.

The overall message is a caution against treating portability as a single number attached to an ancestry label. It is trait-specific, only loosely tied to genetic distance, and confounded with social environment.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Sharpens the PGS portability discussion beyond "scores don't transfer across ancestries" into a precise, measurable, and more troubling picture
- The **socioeconomic** result is the most important teaching point: a genetic prediction tool's accuracy depends partly on non-genetic factors, which complicates any claim that a PGS measures innate liability
- Demonstrates the value of treating **genetic ancestry as continuous** rather than as discrete categories — methodologically and conceptually
- The immunity-trait result links **natural selection** to a practical limitation of clinical genomics
- Directly complements the Kullo PGS review and the Pan-UK Biobank study in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Disentangle the socioeconomic contribution mechanistically: is it gene–environment interaction, differential phenotype measurement, or participation bias?
- Test the fast-turnover hypothesis for immunity traits by relating portability decay to signatures of selection at the contributing loci
- Develop portability diagnostics that are trait-specific rather than reporting one accuracy figure per ancestry group
- Determine whether within-family PGS analyses, which remove population structure and much of the environmental confounding, show the same trait-specific patterns
- Establish reporting standards requiring that PGS accuracy be characterized across the genetic distance continuum before clinical deployment
