# Rapid adaptation and extinction in synchronized outdoor evolution experiments of Arabidopsis

## Citation (NLM)
Wu X, Bellagio T, Peng Y, Czech L, Lin M, et al. Rapid adaptation and extinction in synchronized outdoor evolution experiments of Arabidopsis. Science. 2026;391(6779):1346. doi:10.1126/science.adz0777

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz0777](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz0777)

---

## Summary

Contemporary evolution in natural environments is being documented in many plant and animal species, but an integrative understanding of the **dynamics of rapid adaptation to different climates** — its **tempo**, **genetic architecture**, **predictability**, and **population feedbacks** — remains unclear for most species. The gold standard for studying evolutionary dynamics experimentally has been **microbial long-term laboratory experiments** combined with genome resequencing, which give exquisite control and dense temporal sampling. But such experiments remain very difficult in multicellular macroorganisms, especially under ecologically realistic conditions, so the best-characterized evolutionary dynamics come from the least ecologically realistic systems.

This study closes that gap using ***Arabidopsis thaliana*** in **synchronized outdoor evolution experiments** — replicated populations established in parallel across different climates and followed over generations with genomic sampling. The design imports the analytical rigour of microbial experimental evolution (replication, temporal sequencing, allele frequency trajectories) into a multicellular organism in a **real, variable outdoor environment** rather than a growth chamber. Synchronization across sites is what makes the climates comparable: the same starting populations experience different environments simultaneously, so differences in outcome are attributable to climate rather than to founding conditions or timing.

The headline result pairs two outcomes that are usually studied apart. Populations showed **rapid adaptation** in some settings and **extinction** in others. Studying both together is the important move. Experimental evolution normally reports on populations that survived, which selects for the systems in which adaptation was possible and quietly omits the cases where evolution was too slow or the environment too harsh — precisely the outcome that matters most for predicting responses to climate change. Whether a population adapts or dies is the question, and this design can observe both.

The framework also addresses **population feedbacks**: adapting populations change their own demography, and population size in turn governs how efficiently selection operates relative to drift, coupling ecology and evolution on the same timescale.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Brings **experimental evolution** — usually a microbial method — into a multicellular organism under realistic field conditions
- Studies **adaptation and extinction together**, correcting a survivorship bias built into most evolution experiments
- Makes **evolutionary predictability** an empirical question with replicated populations across climates
- Directly relevant to climate change biology: whether populations can adapt fast enough is a genetics question with a measurable answer
- Complements the experimental evolution review and the stickleback jackpot adaptation study in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Identify the loci underlying rapid adaptation and determine whether the same alleles respond in independent replicate populations
- Establish what predicts extinction versus adaptation — initial genetic variation, population size, or the severity and rate of environmental change
- Extend the experiment over more generations to test whether early rapid adaptation is sustained or reverses
- Use the results to calibrate evolutionary rescue models used in climate change projections
- Compare outdoor dynamics with matched growth-chamber experiments to quantify how much environmental realism changes the conclusions
