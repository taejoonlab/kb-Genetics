# Determinants of de novo mutations in extended pedigrees of 43 dog breeds

## Citation (NLM)
Zhang SJ, Ma J, Riera M, Besenbacher S, Niskanen JE, Salokorpi N, et al. Determinants of de novo mutations in extended pedigrees of 43 dog breeds. Genome Biol. 2025;26:305. doi:10.1186/s13059-025-03804-2

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-025-03804-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-025-03804-2)

---

## Summary

The **germline mutation rate** sets the pace of evolution and the baseline risk of new genetic disease. It is not a constant: it depends on parental age, sex, life history, and genomic context. Most of what we know comes from humans and a few model species. Domestic dogs are an unusually informative system because they combine two features that let you disentangle variables normally confounded: they **lack the recombination regulator PRDM9**, and they have been subjected to **intense artificial selection** producing breeds that differ enormously in body size and developmental rate while remaining one species.

The authors sequenced **390 parent–offspring trios across 43 breeds** — an unusually large trio dataset outside humans — and counted **de novo mutations (DNMs)** directly.

The headline estimate is a germline mutation rate of **4.89 × 10⁻⁹ per base pair per generation**, roughly comparable in order of magnitude to the human rate. As in humans, **parental age strongly influences the rate, and paternal age dominates** — but the **paternal age effect is 1.5-fold stronger in dogs than in humans**. This is consistent with the standard explanation: sperm are produced by continuous spermatogonial division throughout life, so older fathers transmit more replication-associated mutations, and the strength of the effect scales with how fast that division cycle turns over.

Body size gave a second result. **Larger breeds show elevated early-life mutation counts**, which the authors relate to accelerated developmental trajectories — bigger dogs grow faster and pass through more cell divisions early, and those divisions leave mutations. Notably, despite centuries of extreme artificial selection and severe inbreeding, **germline mutation rates remain stable across breeds** — breeding has reshaped dog morphology dramatically without destabilizing the mutation process.

The most distinctive finding concerns **CpG islands**. In dogs, CpG islands show a **2.6-fold higher mutation rate than the genomic average**. This is not the case in humans, where CpG islands — being hypomethylated — actually escape the elevated CpG→TpG deamination rate seen elsewhere. The authors attribute the canine pattern to **recombination**: in the absence of PRDM9, recombination in dogs is directed to CpG-island-like promoter regions, and recombination is mutagenic. The loss of one gene therefore reshapes the mutational landscape of the entire genome — a striking demonstration that recombination and mutation are coupled processes.

Finally, they identify a **tenfold hypermutated individual** and propose a **maternal mechanism involving MLH1-mediated germline instability during gametogenesis** — a mismatch-repair route to hypermutation acting on the maternal side.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Gives the mutation lecture a concrete, measured number for a non-human mammal and shows how a mutation rate is actually estimated (trio sequencing, not inference from divergence)
- The **paternal age effect** is explained mechanistically through spermatogonial divisions, connecting mutation to gametogenesis
- The **CpG island result** is a memorable demonstration that mutation rate is not an intrinsic property of a sequence but depends on the cellular processes acting on it — here, recombination
- Shows the consequences of **PRDM9 loss** genome-wide, tying the mutation lecture to the recombination lecture
- Illustrates why **dogs are an exceptional genetic model**: breed structure creates extreme, controlled variation in body size and life history within one species

## Future Research Directions

- Confirm the MLH1-mediated maternal hypermutation mechanism by sequencing additional hypermutated individuals and testing MLH1 function directly
- Determine whether the CpG island effect scales with local recombination rate within dogs, which would establish causality rather than correlation
- Test the body-size hypothesis across other domesticated species with strong size variation, and directly count early developmental cell divisions
- Examine mutation spectra (not just rates) across breeds to test whether inbreeding alters the *kinds* of mutations arising
- Compare dogs with other PRDM9-lacking lineages (birds, some fish) to test whether elevated CpG-island mutation is a general consequence of PRDM9 loss
