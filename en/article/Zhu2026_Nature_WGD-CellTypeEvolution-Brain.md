# Whole-genome duplication shaped cell-type evolution in the vertebrate brain

## Citation (NLM)
Zhu Y, Zhang S, Wei J, Dolgetta-Garcia D, Jindrich K, Liu H, et al. Whole-genome duplication shaped cell-type evolution in the vertebrate brain. Nature. 2026;656(8095):659-669. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10629-x

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10629-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10629-x)

---

## Summary

Vertebrate brains contain more cell types than the brains of their closest invertebrate relatives. Separately, it is established that **two rounds of whole-genome duplication (WGD)** occurred during early vertebrate evolution, producing large families of **ohnologues** — paralogues retained from whole-genome duplication rather than from small-scale duplication. The long-standing hypothesis that these duplicated genes enabled the elaboration of vertebrate complexity has been difficult to test, because it requires comparing cell types, not just genes, between vertebrates and their outgroups.

Single-cell transcriptomics makes that comparison possible. The authors assembled **brain single-cell transcriptomes from five chordates** — **human, mouse, lizard, lamprey and amphioxus**. The sampling is the design. **Amphioxus** is an invertebrate chordate that diverged **before** the vertebrate WGDs and retains a largely unduplicated genome; **lamprey** is a jawless vertebrate; the other three are jawed vertebrates. The comparison therefore brackets the duplication events.

Two findings follow. First, **many vertebrate cell-type families that share conserved core transcription factors do not show one-to-one homology with amphioxus cell types.** Vertebrate cell types are not simply amphioxus cell types with more genes; the correspondence broke down as new types arose, and conserved transcription factor identity does not imply conserved cell type.

Second, **ohnologues — particularly those retained from the first WGD — were more important** than other duplicates in this process. This is the key result, because it distinguishes whole-genome duplication from duplication in general. WGD duplicates every gene simultaneously, preserving stoichiometry across entire regulatory networks; small-scale duplication creates dosage imbalance. That difference may be exactly why WGD-derived paralogues were disproportionately available for building new cell identities: an entire regulatory module could be duplicated intact and then diverge as a unit.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly connects **gene duplication** to the origin of morphological and cellular complexity, the central claim of Ohno's hypothesis, now testable
- Distinguishes **ohnologues from ordinary paralogues** and explains why whole-genome duplication is qualitatively different from local duplication
- Demonstrates modern **comparative single-cell transcriptomics** and the concept of cell-type homology, which is subtler than gene homology
- Reinforces the orthology/paralogy lecture with an evolutionary consequence at the level of organismal complexity
- Amphioxus as the pre-duplication outgroup is a clean illustration of why outgroup choice determines what a comparison can show

## Future Research Directions

- Test functionally whether specific ohnologue pairs are required for vertebrate-specific cell identities, using knockouts in lamprey or fish
- Determine whether first-round and second-round ohnologues differ systematically in retention and expression, and why the first round dominates
- Extend sampling to additional pre- and post-duplication lineages, including tunicates and cartilaginous fish
- Ask whether teleost-specific WGD produced a comparable burst of cell-type novelty, which would provide an independent replicate
- Develop rigorous criteria for cell-type homology, since the conclusions depend on how homology between transcriptomic clusters is defined
