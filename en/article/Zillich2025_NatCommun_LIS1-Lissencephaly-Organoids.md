# Capturing disease severity in LIS1-lissencephaly reveals proteostasis dysregulation in patient-derived forebrain organoids

## Citation (NLM)
Zillich L, Gasparotto M, Rossetti AC, Fechtner O, Maillard C, Hoffrichter A, et al. Capturing disease severity in LIS1-lissencephaly reveals proteostasis dysregulation in patient-derived forebrain organoids. Nat Commun. 2025;16:9755. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-64980-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64980-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64980-0)

---

## Summary

**LIS1-lissencephaly** is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by reduced cortical folding ("smooth brain") and severe neurological impairment. Genetically it looks simple: **all cases result from heterozygous mutations in the *LIS1* gene**. Yet patients present across a broad spectrum of severity. This is the classic problem of **variable expressivity** — one gene, one mode of inheritance, and radically different outcomes — and it is difficult to study in humans because the affected tissue is developing brain.

The authors addressed this using **patient-derived forebrain organoids** from individuals representing **mild, moderate, and severe** LIS1-lissencephaly. Organoids are the key enabling technology: they recapitulate early human cortical development in a dish from the patient's own cells, so severity differences that arise during neurodevelopment can be observed rather than inferred from postnatal imaging. Deliberately sampling three severity classes, rather than comparing patient to control, converts the study from a disease-versus-normal comparison into an analysis of what makes one patient worse than another.

The first result is that **LIS1 protein levels vary across patients** in a manner tracking clinical severity. This is important because it means severity is at least partly a **quantitative dosage** phenomenon rather than a qualitative difference in mutation type: how much functional LIS1 protein remains predicts how severe the phenotype is. Different heterozygous mutations evidently leave different residual amounts of protein, whether through nonsense-mediated decay, protein instability, or partial function.

The mechanistic finding is **proteostasis dysregulation** — disruption of the cellular systems that maintain protein folding, quality control, and degradation — in patient organoids, with severity-dependent differences. LIS1 functions in the dynein motor complex regulating nuclear migration and mitotic spindle orientation in neural progenitors, and the proteostasis result connects that established cell-biological role to a broader cellular stress phenotype that scales with disease severity.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Tackles **variable expressivity** experimentally rather than descriptively, in a disorder where every patient has a mutation in the same gene
- Demonstrates **dosage sensitivity** and **haploinsufficiency** as quantitative concepts: residual protein level, not mutation identity, tracks severity
- Introduces **organoids** as a way to study human neurodevelopment in the patient's own genetic background — the only accessible route to developing human brain
- Shows the value of sampling a **severity gradient** rather than a binary case/control design
- Connects to the *mef2ca* severity–variation study in this vault, which addresses the same severity question from the allelic-series direction

## Future Research Directions

- Establish whether proteostasis dysregulation is causal or consequential by pharmacologically or genetically restoring proteostasis and asking whether the organoid phenotype improves
- Determine what sets residual LIS1 protein level for each mutation — nonsense-mediated decay efficiency, protein stability, or modifier variants
- Test whether measured LIS1 protein level in an accessible patient tissue could predict clinical severity prospectively
- Extend the severity-gradient organoid design to other neurodevelopmental disorders with wide expressivity
- Investigate whether proteostasis modulators represent a therapeutic direction, given that partial restoration might shift a severe case toward a milder one
