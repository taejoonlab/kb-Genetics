# Experimental evolution in an era of molecular manipulation

## Citation (NLM)
Ascensao JA, Desai MM. Experimental evolution in an era of molecular manipulation. Nat Rev Genet. 2026;27(1):81-95. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00867-6

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00867-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00867-6)

## Summary

Laboratory evolution experiments in microbial and viral populations turned evolution into an experimental science: you can watch adaptation happen, replicate it, and ask whether the outcome is repeatable. High-throughput sequencing over the past two decades massively expanded the scale and resolution of these experiments — it became routine to track allele frequencies through time in hundreds of parallel populations. But sequencing alone tells you *which mutations rose*, not *what they do*. The field's limitation was the gap between genotype and phenotype, and between correlation and mechanism. This review argues that new tools for measuring and manipulating both genotypes and phenotypes are now closing that gap.

**Manipulating and measuring at scale.** The technical core of the review. Barcoding lineages allows the fitness of hundreds of thousands of distinct lineages to be tracked simultaneously, which makes it possible to measure the **distribution of fitness effects** of new mutations directly rather than infer it. CRISPR-based editing and multiplexed genome engineering allow candidate mutations to be **reconstructed individually and in combination** in a clean background — turning an observational claim ("this mutation swept, therefore it was beneficial") into a causal test. Deep mutational scanning maps genotype–phenotype relationships for entire genes. Single-cell and high-throughput phenotyping connect genotypes to intermediate phenotypes rather than only to fitness.

**Predicting and manipulating evolution.** With mutations reconstructable, the field can address whether evolution is predictable: how repeatable are trajectories across replicate populations, how much does **epistasis** constrain accessible paths, and can the outcome be steered — for example, by choosing drug sequences that make resistance evolutionarily inaccessible.

**Eco-evolutionary interactions.** Classical experiments used single genotypes in well-mixed, constant environments. Real populations are structured, interact, and modify their own environment. The review covers cross-feeding, frequency-dependent selection, and coexistence, where ecology and evolution operate on the same timescale.

**Laboratory versus natural evolution.** The review's most valuable section is its honest treatment of external validity. Laboratory conditions are simplified in specific ways — constant environments, large population sizes, asexuality in many systems, short timescales — and these choices systematically bias which evolutionary phenomena can be observed. The authors discuss where laboratory results generalize and where they should not be expected to.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows that evolution is directly **experimentally testable**, not only inferred from comparative data — the strongest possible answer to the question of whether evolution is a science of the past
- Introduces the **distribution of fitness effects**, **epistasis**, and **evolutionary repeatability** as measurable quantities
- Connects population genetics theory (drift, clonal interference, selection coefficients) to observable data in a tractable system
- Directly complements the *E. coli* spectinomycin adaptation study in this vault, which is an example of exactly this methodology
- The laboratory-versus-nature discussion is a model of how to state the limits of an experimental system honestly

## Future Research Directions

- Extend high-throughput lineage tracking and mutation reconstruction to sexual and multicellular systems, where most current tools do not apply
- Determine how much epistasis constrains adaptation in realistic genetic backgrounds rather than in a single clean laboratory strain
- Test evolutionary steering — designing selective regimes that make resistance inaccessible — in clinically relevant pathogens
- Bridge laboratory and natural evolution by applying the same measurement tools to wild populations
- Move from measuring fitness to measuring intermediate phenotypes systematically, so that adaptation can be explained mechanistically rather than described statistically
