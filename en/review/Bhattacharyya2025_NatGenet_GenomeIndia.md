# Mapping genetic diversity with the GenomeIndia project

## Citation (NLM)
Bhattacharyya C, Subramanian K, Uppili B, Biswas NK, Ramdas S, Tallapaka KB, et al.; GenomeIndia Consortium. Mapping genetic diversity with the GenomeIndia project. Nat Genet. 2025;57(4):767-773. doi:10.1038/s41588-025-02153-x

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02153-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02153-x)

## Summary

India has 1.44 billion people distributed across more than **4,600 distinct endogamous groups** — arguably the greatest concentration of ethnolinguistic, sociocultural, and genetic structure anywhere on Earth. Yet the global genomic landscape remains overwhelmingly **Euro-centric**, and India has been severely underrepresented even in projects designed to capture worldwide diversity, from the 1000 Genomes Project through to modern biobanks. This Comment reports the **GenomeIndia** project, which sequenced the **whole genomes of 10,000 healthy, unrelated individuals from 83 populations**.

The scientific rationale rests on India's unusual demographic history. The subcontinent experienced several **waves of migration**, with population expansions during the Palaeolithic and Neolithic, exposure to widely varying environments, and extensive **admixture** — followed by the formation of numerous **endogamous groups** that have maintained distinct identities through centuries of within-group marriage. This history produces a genetic structure quite unlike a large outbred population. Many contemporary Indian populations descend from small founding groups and have remained closed, so each carries **founder effects**: variants that are rare or absent globally can reach appreciable frequency within a single group, including **disease-causing mutations amplified within specific communities**. This makes India simultaneously a place where global reference panels perform badly and a place where population-specific genetics can be unusually tractable.

The consortium was constituted in late 2017 with **20 institutions**, coordinated by the Centre for Brain Research at the Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore, and launched in January 2020 with funding from the Department of Biotechnology, Government of India. The Comment describes the sampling design across 83 populations, the sequencing and analysis infrastructure, and the resource being made available.

The practical consequences the authors emphasize are concrete. Variant interpretation depends on knowing population allele frequencies; a variant absent from European reference databases may be common and benign in a particular Indian group, and clinical misclassification follows directly from missing data. Polygenic risk scores derived from European GWAS transfer poorly. Ancestry inference, admixture history, and drug response genetics all require population-appropriate references. GenomeIndia is positioned as the foundational resource for genomic medicine in India and as a substantial contribution to global genomic diversity.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Makes the **Euro-centric bias of human genomics** concrete with the clearest possible counterexample — the world's most populous country, largely missing from reference databases
- **Endogamy and founder effects** are illustrated at national scale, connecting directly to the population genetics lecture (inbreeding, effective population size, drift, runs of homozygosity)
- Explains why **population-specific reference panels** are a scientific necessity, not a political gesture — variant interpretation literally depends on allele frequency
- Complements the polygenic risk score review in this vault, which identifies cross-ancestry portability as the central barrier to clinical use
- A good example of national-scale consortium science and the infrastructure it requires

## Future Research Directions

- Expand beyond 10,000 genomes and 83 populations toward coverage proportionate to the 4,600 endogamous groups
- Catalogue population-specific pathogenic variants systematically to enable accurate clinical interpretation within each community
- Use the endogamous structure deliberately: founder populations are ideal for mapping recessive disease genes with small sample sizes
- Develop and validate polygenic risk scores in Indian populations rather than transferring European scores
- Integrate GenomeIndia with global resources so that Indian allele frequencies are available in standard variant interpretation tools
- Address the governance, consent, and benefit-sharing framework for a national genomic resource of this scale
