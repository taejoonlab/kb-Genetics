# The genetic basis of human height

## Citation (NLM)
Bicknell LS, Hirschhorn JN, Savarirayan R. The genetic basis of human height. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(9):604-619. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00834-1

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00834-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00834-1)

---

## Summary

Human height is *the* model polygenic trait. It is easy to measure, highly heritable (roughly 80% in well-nourished populations), continuously distributed, and it has been the proving ground for quantitative genetics from Galton onward. This review unifies two literatures usually kept apart: the **polygenic** genetics of normal height variation, and the **monogenic** disorders that produce extreme short or tall stature.

**Monogenic height conditions.** Single-gene variants can shift stature dramatically. The review covers skeletal dysplasias (achondroplasia from *FGFR3* gain-of-function, the most common form of disproportionate short stature), growth hormone and IGF1 axis defects, primordial dwarfism from mutations in genes controlling cell division and DNA replication, and overgrowth syndromes (Marfan, Sotos, Beckwith–Wiedemann). These are informative precisely because a large effect at one gene identifies a pathway that matters.

**Polygenic contributors.** GWAS of height has been the discipline's scaling experiment. Early studies with thousands of participants found a handful of loci explaining a trivial fraction of variance; meta-analyses of over five million individuals now implicate more than 12,000 independent variants and account for the great majority of common-variant heritability. Height demonstrated **polygenicity** — thousands of variants of individually tiny effect — and remains the clearest empirical case for it. Rare-variant studies from exome sequencing have added a further tier of larger-effect alleles.

**From genes to biology.** The review's central and most satisfying argument is that the monogenic and polygenic maps **converge on the same pathways**. Common variants associated with normal height variation cluster around genes that, when severely mutated, cause skeletal dysplasia or growth failure — the growth plate, chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation, extracellular matrix, and endocrine growth signalling. A monogenic disorder and a GWAS hit can be the same biology at different doses. This is the strongest available refutation of the idea that common and rare variant genetics describe separate worlds.

**Bidirectional regulation.** Height is not controlled by a set of "growth promoters" alone; growth-restraining pathways exist and their loss causes overgrowth. Both directions of deviation are genetically informative.

Finally, the review addresses **why this matters medically**: height deviation is epidemiologically and genetically associated with altered risk of cancer (taller) and cardiovascular disease (shorter), so understanding height genetics informs disease genetics beyond stature itself.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The canonical worked example for the complex traits and GWAS lectures — every concept (heritability, polygenicity, effect size distribution, missing heritability, polygenic scores) has a concrete instantiation here
- The **convergence of monogenic and polygenic maps** is the single most important teaching point: rare severe variants and common small-effect variants often implicate the same genes and pathways
- Connects directly to Galton and the biometric tradition, and thus to the "Beyond Mendel" perspective also in this vault
- Provides an accessible clinical dimension — achondroplasia, growth hormone deficiency — that grounds abstract quantitative genetics in medicine
- Demonstrates how sample size transformed a field: the same trait, studied at n = 10³ and n = 5 × 10⁶, gives qualitatively different answers

## Key References

1. **Galton (1886, 1889)** — Regression toward mediocrity in hereditary stature; *Natural Inheritance* — The origin of quantitative genetics, using height
2. **Fisher (1918)** — The correlation between relatives on the supposition of Mendelian inheritance — Reconciles polygenic height with Mendelian inheritance
3. **Yengo et al. (2022)** — A saturated map of common genetic variants associated with human height — The 5.4-million-person GWAS identifying >12,000 variants
4. **Lango Allen et al. (2010)** — Hundreds of variants clustered in genomic loci and biological pathways affect human height — Early demonstration of pathway convergence
5. **Shiang et al. (1994)** — *FGFR3* mutations in achondroplasia — The canonical monogenic height gene
6. **Marouli et al. (2017)** — Rare and low-frequency coding variants alter human adult height — The rare-variant tier
7. **Baron et al. (2015)** — Short and tall stature: a new paradigm emerges — Growth plate biology as the unifying mechanism

## Future Research Directions

- Move from associated variants to causal variants and their target genes at the >12,000 height loci, most of which are non-coding
- Determine the cell types and developmental windows in which height-associated variants act, using growth plate single-cell data
- Test whether polygenic height scores can improve diagnosis or management in monogenic growth disorders, for example by explaining variable expressivity
- Improve cross-ancestry portability of height polygenic scores, which remains poor and is a general problem for polygenic prediction
- Establish whether the height–cancer and height–cardiovascular associations are causal, using Mendelian randomization with the now very large instrument set
