# Life history in Caenorhabditis elegans: from molecular genetics to evolutionary ecology

## Citation (NLM)
Braendle C, Paaby A. Life history in Caenorhabditis elegans: from molecular genetics to evolutionary ecology. Genetics. 2024;228(3):iyae151. doi:10.1093/genetics/iyae151

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyae151](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyae151)

## Summary

"Life history" refers to the traits that determine an organism's schedule of survival and reproduction — developmental rate, age at maturity, fecundity, offspring size, lifespan — and therefore its Darwinian fitness. This WormBook review argues that *Caenorhabditis elegans*, long the workhorse of molecular and developmental genetics, has in the past two decades become a uniquely powerful system for life-history research, because it now combines deep mechanistic knowledge with a growing catalogue of natural variation.

The review opens with the defining feature of life history: **trade-offs**. Traits are not independently optimizable — an individual can make large offspring or many offspring, but not many large offspring. Explaining how selection resolves such trade-offs has been the field's central problem since Fisher (1930), Lack (1954), and Williams (1966). The authors then use *C. elegans* to make this concrete. Its reproductive mode is **androdioecious**: self-fertilizing hermaphrodites plus rare males. Selfing guarantees reproductive assurance for a colonizing worm but collapses effective recombination and heterozygosity; outcrossing with males restores variation at a cost. Its life cycle is also strikingly **plastic**: under crowding, starvation, or high pheromone, larvae divert into the stress-resistant, non-feeding, long-lived **dauer** stage instead of continuing to adulthood. Dauer is the textbook example of an adaptive developmental switch — a single environmental input reprogramming the entire life-history schedule.

A large section reconstructs one of the field's most instructive controversies: **the cost of reproduction**. Evolutionary theory predicts that investment in reproduction should trade off against somatic maintenance and hence lifespan. Yet *C. elegans* aging genetics produced long-lived mutants (*daf-2* insulin/IGF receptor mutants, germline-ablated animals; Klass 1983; Friedman and Johnson 1988; Kenyon et al. 1993) that appeared to double lifespan with little or no fecundity cost. Developmental geneticists read this as evidence that the trade-off is not obligatory; evolutionary biologists countered that laboratory conditions systematically hide fitness costs that would be exposed in a natural, competitive, fluctuating environment. The authors treat this as a methodological lesson rather than a settled dispute: fitness values are context-dependent, and a trait measured on a Petri dish is not the trait selection sees.

The second half covers **intraspecific variation and its genetic architecture**. Community collections of wild isolates (Barrière and Félix; the CaeNDR/CeNDR resource; Cook et al. 2017; Crombie et al. 2024) and mutation-accumulation experiments now allow the mutational variance, standing genetic variance, and QTL architecture of life-history traits to be measured directly. Life-history traits behave as classic complex traits: many loci of small effect, pervasive genotype-by-environment interaction, and low but nonzero heritability. Finally, the review widens to **major life-history transitions** across the genus and phylum — repeated independent origins of self-fertility from gonochoristic (male/female) ancestors within *Caenorhabditis*, and the evolution of parasitism across Nematoda — showing how a mechanistic model system can be used to ask why the same transition happens again and again.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Connects two halves of a genetics course that students often experience as unrelated: molecular/developmental genetics (how a trait is built) and evolutionary/population genetics (why the trait has the value it does)
- Gives a rigorous definition of **fitness** and shows why measuring it is genuinely hard — a corrective to casual classroom use of the word
- **Dauer** is an ideal teaching example of phenotypic plasticity and gene-by-environment interaction
- The selfing/outcrossing (androdioecy) system makes the population-genetic consequences of mating system — inbreeding, effective recombination, heterozygosity, effective population size — concrete and observable
- The long-lived-mutant controversy is a case study in how the same data support opposite conclusions depending on whether "cost" is measured in the lab or in nature

## Future Research Directions

- Measure fitness under ecologically realistic conditions (rotting fruit microcosms, boom-and-bust resource cycles, competition) and re-test whether long-lived mutants really escape the cost of reproduction
- Map the QTLs underlying natural variation in developmental rate, dauer propensity, and fecundity to specific variants, and ask whether the same genes recur across wild isolates
- Use the repeated independent evolution of selfing within *Caenorhabditis* as a replicated natural experiment: is the genetic route to self-fertility the same each time?
- Quantify genotype-by-environment interaction systematically — which life-history trade-offs appear only in specific environments, and can that be predicted?
- Extend life-history genetics beyond *C. elegans* to parasitic nematodes, where the same developmental switches (dauer/infective larva) have been co-opted for a completely different ecology
