# The effects of loss of Y chromosome on male health

## Citation (NLM)
Bruhn-Olszewska B, Markljung E, Rychlicka-Buniowska E, Sarkisyan D, Filipowicz N, Dumanski JP. The effects of loss of Y chromosome on male health. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(5):320-335. doi:10.1038/s41576-024-00805-y

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-024-00805-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-024-00805-y)

## Summary

**Loss of Y chromosome (LOY)** is the most common post-zygotic (somatic) mutation in men. It is not present in the germline; it accumulates in somatic cells during life, most detectably in blood leukocytes, and it is strongly age-dependent — rare in young men, present in a substantial fraction of cells in a large proportion of elderly men. For decades LOY was dismissed as an unimportant artifact of ageing, on the assumption that the Y carries little beyond the male-determining gene *SRY* and spermatogenesis factors. The past decade of work, which this review synthesizes, has overturned that assumption.

**Predisposition and prevalence.** LOY frequency rises steeply with age and is strongly increased by **smoking**, a dose-dependent and partly reversible effect. Inherited germline variants also predispose to LOY, and the loci implicated are enriched for genes controlling **cell-cycle regulation and genome stability** — LOY is therefore not purely random attrition but a phenotype with its own heritability.

**Mechanisms.** LOY arises from mis-segregation of the Y in mitosis, and the review examines why the Y is especially vulnerable and how LOY-bearing clones then expand, since a clone that has lost the Y appears to enjoy a proliferative advantage in the haematopoietic compartment.

**Consequences.** This is the review's central contribution. LOY in leukocytes is not silent: it changes the **transcriptome of the affected cells**, producing what the authors and others term a "LOY-associated transcriptional effect" extending to autosomal genes. Functionally, this **shapes the activity of the immune system**, which provides the mechanism connecting a chromosome loss in blood cells to disease in entirely different organs.

**Disease associations.** LOY has been associated with **cancer** (both haematological and solid, including bladder cancer), **neurodegeneration** including Alzheimer disease, **cardiovascular disease**, and severity of **acute infection**. Critically, the field has moved from epidemiological association to functional demonstration: causal roles for LOY have been supported in **cardiac fibrosis** and **bladder cancer**, in the former case through experimental models showing LOY in immune cells promoting fibrosis. The review is careful about the mosaic nature of the phenomenon — LOY is present in a fraction of cells, so effects are dose-dependent and reversibility or clonal dynamics matter.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Makes **mosaicism** and **somatic mutation** concrete: not every cell in your body has the same genome, and the difference has health consequences
- Directly extends the sex chromosome lecture past sex determination — the Y matters after development, in somatic tissues, throughout life
- Introduces **clonal haematopoiesis** logic: somatic mutations in blood stem cells expand clonally with age and affect systemic disease risk
- A good example of a phenomenon dismissed as artifact for decades, then shown to be causal — useful for discussing how fields revise their assumptions
- Offers a partial genetic explanation for the **male–female difference in lifespan and disease risk**, connecting to the human disease lectures

## Future Research Directions

- Determine the mechanism of the LOY-associated transcriptional effect — how does losing the Y alter autosomal gene expression?
- Establish which Y-linked genes are responsible; the Y carries a small set of broadly expressed genes whose loss could plausibly have systemic effects
- Test whether LOY is causal in additional diseases where only association currently exists, using the experimental strategy that worked for cardiac fibrosis
- Determine whether LOY is reversible or preventable — smoking cessation reduces LOY, but can existing LOY clones be depleted?
- Develop LOY as a clinical biomarker: standardize measurement, define thresholds, and test whether it adds predictive value over age
- Investigate whether analogous somatic sex chromosome loss (mosaic loss of X in women) has parallel consequences
