# Diversity and consequences of structural variation in the human genome

## Citation (NLM)
Collins RL, Talkowski ME. Diversity and consequences of structural variation in the human genome. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(7):443-462. doi:10.1038/s41576-024-00808-9

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-024-00808-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-024-00808-9)

## Summary

**Structural variants (SVs)** — deletions, duplications, insertions, inversions, translocations, mobile-element insertions, and complex rearrangements, conventionally at least 50 bp — are ubiquitous in human populations, heterogeneous in the mutational processes that create them, key substrates for evolution and adaptation, and profound drivers of human disease. Yet they have been chronically under-studied relative to single-nucleotide variants, because short-read sequencing detects them poorly. New long-read technologies and population-scale sequencing have changed this, and this review synthesizes what has emerged.

**Mutational properties.** SV classes arise by distinct mechanisms: **non-allelic homologous recombination** between segmental duplications creates recurrent deletions and duplications at the same loci in unrelated people; **non-homologous end joining** and **microhomology-mediated break-induced replication** create non-recurrent, breakpoint-idiosyncratic events; **mobile element insertion** (Alu, LINE-1, SVA) adds new sequence; and catastrophic events (chromothripsis, chromoanasynthesis) generate complex multi-breakpoint rearrangements in a single cell cycle. Because mechanism determines recurrence, some SVs appear repeatedly in the population while others are unique to a family.

**Population genetics.** SVs are subject to stronger purifying selection than SNVs on average, because they can delete or duplicate whole genes and regulatory elements. Their frequency spectrum, linkage disequilibrium with SNVs, and representation across ancestries all differ from SNVs — and much of the population catalogue is still incomplete, especially in repetitive and segmentally duplicated regions and in non-European ancestries.

**Functional consequences.** SVs act through more mechanisms than point mutations: **gene dosage** change (deletion or duplication of coding sequence), **gene disruption**, **gene fusion**, and — increasingly recognized as important — **position effects**, in which a rearrangement leaves genes intact but moves regulatory elements or disrupts topologically associating domain boundaries, rewiring enhancer–promoter contacts.

**Disease and traits.** SVs cause classical genomic disorders (recurrent microdeletion and microduplication syndromes such as 22q11.2, 16p11.2, 7q11.23), contribute substantially to developmental disorders, autism, and schizophrenia, drive cancer genome evolution, and contribute to common complex traits, though quantifying that contribution has been hindered by incomplete SV ascertainment in GWAS.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The definitive current review for the mutation/CNV and rearrangement lectures — covers every class of structural change in one framework
- Explains why **mechanism determines recurrence**: NAHR between segmental duplications produces the same deletion in unrelated families, which is why genomic disorders exist as recognizable syndromes
- Introduces **position effects** and the 3D genome as a mode of pathogenicity that does not touch a coding sequence
- Explains why **SVs are missing from most GWAS** — a methodological limitation with real consequences for missing heritability
- Directly complements the 3D genome structural variants review and the genome assembly review in this vault

## Future Research Directions

- Complete the SV catalogue using long reads across globally diverse populations, particularly in segmental duplications and satellite regions
- Integrate SVs properly into GWAS and polygenic scores, and quantify how much missing heritability they explain
- Predict the functional consequence of a novel SV from its breakpoints and the surrounding regulatory architecture — currently largely impossible
- Determine how often position effects, rather than dosage changes, are the pathogenic mechanism in developmental disorders
- Develop clinical interpretation frameworks and constraint metrics for SVs comparable to those now standard for SNVs
