# Evolutionary genetics of ageing

## Citation (NLM)

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-026-00959-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-026-00959-x)

## Summary

Modern humans routinely survive to advanced ages in proportions no ancestral population approached, and so experience the consequences of molecular pathways optimized for youth that remain active in old age. This review argues that an evolutionary perspective is not decorative but explanatory: it accounts for patterns of ageing that mechanistic biology alone cannot.

**Evolutionary genetic theories.** The foundational insight is that **the force of natural selection weakens with adult age**. An allele that kills at 15 is removed efficiently; one that kills at 75 is nearly invisible to selection, because most reproduction has already occurred. This creates a **selection shadow** with two consequences. **Mutation accumulation** (Medawar): deleterious late-acting mutations drift to appreciable frequency because selection cannot remove them. **Antagonistic pleiotropy** (Williams): alleles with early-life benefits are actively favoured even if they impose late-life costs. The **disposable soma** theory (Kirkwood) recasts this as resource allocation — investment in somatic maintenance trades off against reproduction, and it is never optimal to maintain the body indefinitely.

**Conserved features.** Despite these theories predicting no dedicated "ageing program," the molecular hallmarks of ageing and the pathways that modulate lifespan — insulin/IGF-1 signalling, mTOR, dietary restriction responses, mitochondrial function, proteostasis — are strikingly **conserved from yeast and worms to flies and mice**. The review addresses this apparent paradox: conservation reflects the conservation of the growth and nutrient-sensing machinery that is being traded off, not of an ageing program as such.

**Variation across species.** Maximum lifespan varies more than 100-fold among vertebrates. This variation is not noise but predictable from ecology: species with low extrinsic mortality — those protected by flight, body size, armour, or subterranean life — evolve slower ageing, exactly as the selection-shadow argument predicts, because more of their reproduction occurs late.

**Variation within species.** Human lifespan heritability is low (under 20%, arguably under 10% after correcting for assortative mating), yet GWAS and model-organism studies do identify variants affecting healthy lifespan, and the review discusses how the evolutionary theories predict what that architecture should look like.

**Age-related disease.** The review's most practically important argument: many common late-onset diseases — Alzheimer disease, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer — may be manifestations of antagonistic pleiotropy and mutation accumulation rather than discrete pathologies, which implies that targeting ageing itself could address several diseases at once.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Explains a genuinely counterintuitive idea rigorously: ageing is not selected for, it is what happens when selection stops paying attention
- **Antagonistic pleiotropy** and **mutation accumulation** are the clearest applications of population genetic reasoning to a phenotype students care about personally
- Connects the population genetics lecture (selection coefficients, effective selection, drift) to human disease and ageing
- The 100-fold lifespan variation across vertebrates makes the extrinsic-mortality prediction testable and comparative
- Complements the Diversity Outbred mouse lifespan QTL study in this vault, which supplies the empirical genetic architecture

## Future Research Directions

- Test antagonistic pleiotropy directly by identifying alleles with measurable early-life benefit and late-life cost in humans and model organisms
- Determine whether the conserved longevity pathways are conserved because they are the trade-off machinery, or for reasons unrelated to ageing
- Use comparative genomics across the 100-fold lifespan range to identify the genomic changes that accompany slow ageing
- Test whether interventions targeting ageing biology delay multiple age-related diseases simultaneously, as the evolutionary framework predicts
- Reconcile the low heritability of human lifespan with the substantial genetic effects seen in model organisms — is the difference biological or a consequence of environmental variance in humans?
