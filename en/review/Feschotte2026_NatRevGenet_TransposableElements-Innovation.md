# Transposable elements as catalysts of evolutionary innovation

## Citation (NLM)
Feschotte C. Transposable elements as catalysts of evolutionary innovation. Nat Rev Genet. 2026;27(7):501-502. doi:10.1038/s41576-026-00980-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-026-00980-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-026-00980-0)

## Summary

When Barbara McClintock identified transposable elements (TEs) in maize in the late 1940s, she called them **"controlling elements"** — she believed she had found the mechanism by which genes are switched on and off during development. Her framing prefigured what Jacob and Monod later formalized for the *lac* operon: a *trans*-acting factor governing *cis*-regulatory sequences. This Comment by Cedric Feschotte, revisiting an argument he made in the same journal 18 years earlier, argues that McClintock's original view has now been vindicated.

**Why TEs were overlooked.** Feschotte identifies two reasons, one dogmatic and one technical. Dogmatically, TEs were assumed to be "selfish" or "junk" DNA — inert relics of past genomic invasions, silenced by host defences. Technically, TEs are repetitive (the human *Alu* family exceeds a million copies per haploid genome), so the multi-mapping reads they generate were routinely **discarded** by functional genomics pipelines. Longer sequencing reads have made that exclusion impossible, and the resulting studies have transformed the picture.

**TEs as a source of non-coding regulatory elements.** Conservative estimates now indicate that TEs contribute **at least 25% of all candidate cis-regulatory elements (CREs) in the human genome** — roughly 500,000 of about 2 million predicted CREs. Because TEs have proliferated continually, these are drawn from every evolutionary stratum: about half of human TEs inserted in the primate lineage, about 1% are human-specific, and roughly 1,000 insertions per haploid genome are still **polymorphic** in humans. TEs are therefore not a historical deposit but a continuous supply of new regulatory DNA. Validated adaptive examples include the TE insertion in the *cortex* gene causing industrial melanism in the peppered moth, an insertion near *ruby* producing the crimson colour of blood oranges, and an insertion conferring infection-inducibility on the human immune gene *AIM2*.

**Population genetics of the burden.** Because many TEs carry potent built-in CREs, insertions can immediately perturb expression. Selection removes strongly deleterious insertions efficiently, but the removal of **slightly deleterious** ones depends on **effective population size**. Species with small *N*e — vertebrates especially — carry a greater TE-induced regulatory burden, which Feschotte suggests drove the evolution of systems to manage it. Vertebrates deploy expandable batteries of **KRAB zinc finger proteins (ZFPs)** that recognize newly invading TEs sequence-specifically and silence them at the chromatin level. Crucially, this silencing is **reversible** and ZFP expression varies by cell type, stage, and stimulus — so what looks like a defence system is better understood as a **taming** system that finely modulates TE-derived CRE activity and thereby facilitates their co-option into regulatory networks.

**TEs as a source of new proteins.** Some of the most consequential vertebrate proteins have TE origins — the **RAG recombinase** of adaptive immunity and the **syncytins** required for placenta formation. Transposases and Gag proteins are disproportionately co-opted because they supply ready-made DNA- and RNA-binding domains and scaffolding modules. Fusing a transposase DNA-binding domain to an effector instantly creates a regulatory protein with a large pool of pre-existing binding sites already dispersed through the genome — a shortcut to a complete regulatory network.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Reframes **transposable elements** from parasitic junk to a principal engine of evolutionary novelty, correcting a view still common in textbooks
- Connects directly to McClintock and the history of gene regulation, giving the transposon lecture a narrative arc
- Explains why **effective population size** determines how much junk a genome tolerates — a concrete application of population genetics to genome architecture
- The **KRAB-ZFP taming** argument is subtle and worth discussing: the same system that silences TEs is what makes their regulatory output tunable and therefore co-optable
- *RAG* and syncytins are memorable examples that a whole biological system (adaptive immunity, the placenta) can originate from a domesticated transposon

## Future Research Directions

- Determine what fraction of TE-derived CREs are actually functional in vivo rather than merely accessible, using systematic enhancer deletion
- Test the effective-population-size prediction comparatively: do species with large *N*e carry measurably less TE regulatory burden?
- Characterize polymorphic TE insertions in human populations as a class of regulatory variation, and test their contribution to trait variation and disease
- Identify further domesticated TE proteins by systematic search for transposase- and Gag-derived domains in host genomes
- Determine whether KRAB-ZFP silencing is genuinely reversible in a physiologically meaningful way, and how often that reversibility is used developmentally
