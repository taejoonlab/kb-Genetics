# Monitoring biological effects of somatic cell genome editing

## Citation (NLM)
Freedman BS, Bulte JWM, Conklin BR, Judge LM, Dwinell MR, Geurts AM, et al.; SCGE Consortium. Monitoring biological effects of somatic cell genome editing. Nat Rev Genet. 2026;27(4):323-342. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00916-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00916-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00916-0)

---

## Summary

CRISPR-based genome editing therapies have entered the clinic. **Somatic cell genome editing (SCGE)** — editing a patient's own non-germline cells, so that changes are not heritable — raises a practical question that determines whether these therapies can be evaluated and approved: **how do you know what the edit actually did?** This review, from the NIH Somatic Cell Genome Editing Consortium, is a systematic treatment of the measurement problem across three domains.

**Assessing edits in DNA.** The most obvious requirement is knowing what changed at the target site and elsewhere. On-target outcomes are heterogeneous — small insertions and deletions, larger deletions, inversions, and even loss of the whole chromosome arm can follow a double-strand break. **Off-target editing** must be found without knowing in advance where to look, requiring unbiased genome-wide methods (GUIDE-seq, CIRCLE-seq, DISCOVER-seq and successors) followed by targeted deep sequencing to quantify. Editing is also **mosaic** — different cells receive different outcomes — so a bulk measurement reports an average across a heterogeneous population, and single-cell or long-read approaches are needed to see the distribution.

**Assessing effects in human disease models.** DNA-level accuracy is necessary but not sufficient. The review covers human cell and organoid models, iPSC-derived tissues, and animal models used to test whether an edit produces the intended functional correction and to detect unintended cellular consequences — including p53 activation, transcriptional changes, immune responses to the editing machinery, and effects on cell fitness that could select for edited or unedited cells over time.

**Assessing delivery and incorporation by in vivo imaging.** This is the section that distinguishes the review. For *in vivo* editing, a critical unknown is **where the editor actually went**. Imaging approaches — MRI with reporter genes, PET, and other non-invasive modalities — allow biodistribution and editing to be tracked longitudinally in a living animal, and eventually in patients, rather than inferred from terminal tissue sampling.

The consortium framing matters: this is a **toolkit and standards** paper as much as a review, aimed at making editing outcomes comparable across laboratories and interpretable to regulators.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Extends the CRISPR and gene therapy lectures from "how editing works" to "how you verify it worked and did nothing else" — the question that actually gates clinical translation
- Makes the **somatic versus germline** distinction concrete and explains why it is the central ethical and regulatory line in genome editing
- Introduces **off-target effects** and **mosaicism** as measurement problems with specific technical solutions, not just caveats
- Illustrates how consortium science establishes standards, a mode of research students rarely see described
- Complements the CRISPR off-target Perspective also in this vault, which addresses the clinical interpretation side of the same problem

## Future Research Directions

- Establish standardized, cross-laboratory benchmarks for reporting on-target and off-target editing outcomes so that therapies can be compared
- Develop single-cell and long-read approaches that routinely capture the full distribution of editing outcomes rather than a bulk average
- Validate in vivo imaging readouts against terminal molecular measurement, and advance them toward human use
- Determine the long-term fate of edited cells — do edited or unedited cells have a fitness advantage, and does the edited fraction drift over years?
- Define what level of off-target editing is acceptable for a given disease severity, which requires linking measurement to clinical risk
