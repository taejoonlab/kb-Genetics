# Applications of Chemogenomic Library Screening in Drug Discovery

## Citation (NLM)
Jones LH, Bunnage ME. Applications of chemogenomic library screening in drug discovery. Nat Rev Drug Discov. 2017;16(4):285-296. doi:10.1038/nrd.2016.244

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd.2016.244](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd.2016.244)

---

## Summary

Jones and Bunnage (Pfizer) address a recurring problem: most clinical attrition is due to lack of efficacy, arguably because of poor or biased target selection, which has pushed the field towards disease-relevant phenotypic screening. Phenotypic hits, however, are hard to optimize because the target is unknown, structure-based design is impossible and mechanism-based toxicity is hard to predict. Classical target-deconvolution methods (chemoproteomic affinity capture, thermal proteome profiling) are costly and often fail. The authors propose chemogenomic library screening as a faster route. A chemogenomic library is a collection of selective, well-annotated small-molecule pharmacological modulators; a hit in a phenotypic screen suggests that the annotated target of that compound is involved in the phenotype, turning a phenotypic project into a target-based one. Unlike RNAi or CRISPR-Cas9, small molecules are easy to apply across assay formats, including patient-derived primary cells that tolerate transfection poorly, and can probe modalities that genetic perturbation cannot, such as specific protein-protein interactions and allosterism.

Because disease-relevant phenotypic assays are low throughput, chemogenomic libraries hold hundreds to thousands of compounds chosen to cover pharmacological space efficiently, rather than millions. Library design requires curation of large public and private bioassay datasets; compounds must be cell-permeable, potent against their annotated target and broadly selective, ideally with several chemotypes per target so that a putative target is supported by independent chemical matter. The review compares existing sets: the Pfizer chemogenomic library (mined from millions of compounds and billions of data points, with potency below 500 nM against the primary target and exclusion of promiscuous compounds), the Sigma-Aldrich LOPAC1280, the Prestwick library (approved drugs only), the GlaxoSmithKline Biologically Diverse Compound Set (with notably lower ion-channel and nuclear receptor coverage than Pfizer's), the NIH MIPE collection of advanced preclinical and clinical agents (well suited to repositioning because safety data exist), and kinase-focused sets such as the GSK Published (Protein) Kinase Inhibitor Set, PKIS. Table 1 lists size and coverage: Pfizer 2,984 compounds and 1,043 targets, LOPAC1280 1,280 compounds and 453 targets (more than half GPCR-directed), Prestwick 1,280 approved drugs, GSK BDCS 5,853 compounds and 736 targets (dominated by GPCRs, 27%, and kinases, 22%, with up to 10 compounds per target), MIPE 3.0 459-465 compounds (oncology-focused, 45% kinase inhibitors; version 4.0 has 1,912 agents), NIH Molecular Libraries Program probes (375 probes, open data in BARD) and PKIS (367 inhibitors, profiled in 224 kinase and 24 GPCR assays, with 6% active in a luciferase counter-screen). Pitfalls include pan-assay interference compounds, off-target activity of even good probes, and false positives/negatives; the review stresses that negative hits are informative too.

Downstream, hits are followed up with counter-screens (cytotoxicity, fluorescence), pathway analysis (for example the Causal Reasoning Engine, which uses omics data and literature causal statements to infer upstream events), and target validation with RNAi, CRISPR-Cas9, CETSA, chemical probes and activity-based protein profiling. Applications covered include target identification, drug repositioning (since many library members are existing drugs), biological pathway elucidation, predictive toxicology, and discovery of new pharmacological modalities; examples include combining CRISPR/RNAi with chemogenomic screens in a rare sarcoma, and CRISPR-Cas9 in zebrafish to validate SRC in cell migration.

Design principles in more detail. Because the annotated target of a hit may not be the one causing the phenotype, libraries should include several structurally diverse compounds per target and, ideally, inactive negative-control analogues; even if the annotated target is ruled out it can point to a related protein. Libraries mirror the availability of probes, so initiatives such as the Structural Genomics Consortium aim to fill gene families (kinases, bromodomains, methyltransferases) more evenly. Screening concentration matters: full dose-response curves are preferable because primary-cell phenotypic assays often give bell-shaped curves, so a single high-concentration screen yields false negatives; cell viability and fluorescence/luciferase counter-screens should run in the same system, and highly lipophilic compounds (polypharmacology) should be avoided. The Pfizer library selected compounds with potency below 500 nM using tailored multiparameter optimisation (selectivity, permeability, solubility, cytotoxicity), removed PAINS and predicted frequent hitters, favoured different chemical templates per target and used human curation; in a myotonic dystrophy type 1 MBNL1-upregulation screen (1 uM, 3.3% hit rate) it yielded histone deacetylase inhibitors, suggesting epigenetic regulation of MBNL1.

Applications with the examples given. Target identification: LOPAC1280 in a 3D lymphangiogenic sprouting assay (2.8% hit rate) pointed to mevastatin and HMG-CoA reductase; the GSK BDCS in an H3K27me3 imaging assay (8.5% hit rate) recovered 8 of 8 known EZH2 inhibitors as a positive control and implicated other upstream pathways (most targets had only one probe); LOPAC1280 in an invadopodia assay (0.4% hit rate) found the CDK inhibitor purvalanol A and, because its cell-cycle effect was weak, CDK5 was proposed and validated by RNAi, while other CDK inhibitors had been discarded as irreproducible, a reminder about false negatives; MIPE 3.0 plus siRNA identified Aurora B kinase in neuroblastoma; the Broad Informer Set combined with CRISPR/RNAi found CDK4 and XPO1 in a rare sarcoma. Drug repurposing: PKIS in chordoma supported an EGFR hypothesis now in clinical trials; LOPAC1280 in patient-derived leukaemia cells identified quinacrine; a MIPE 3.0 KRAS-G12V destabilisation screen found that only some ABL inhibitors (ponatinib, nilotinib, rebastinib) acted, implicating off-targets (ponatinib inhibits 57 wild-type kinases with IC50 below 1 uM); combination screens with ibrutinib identified PI3K/mTORC1 and BCL-2 inhibitor synergy in lymphoma, and ruxolitinib with ABT-263 in Hodgkin lymphoma; a 1,001-cell-line, 265-drug pharmacogenomic landscape linked U2AF1 mutations to FLT3 inhibitor sensitivity. Predictive toxicology: BioMAP primary-cell panels (a compound was correctly predicted to be a MAPK inhibitor with a rash-like toxicity profile seen clinically) and the LINCS L1000 expression assay (about 1,000 transcripts, more than 30,000 compounds) linked to adverse drug reactions by machine learning. High-content and in vivo screening: LOPAC1280 across 12 isogenic knockout cell lines gave a Pharmacogenetic Phenome Compendium; zebrafish lateral-line migration screens found SRC inhibitors that also inhibited metastasis in mice (SRC validated by CRISPR-Cas9 in zebrafish); a tadpole LOPAC screen found an adenosine A1 antagonist that blocked VEGFA-induced neovascularisation; a Prestwick screen in a Duchenne zebrafish model highlighted PDE5 inhibitors; in vivo false negatives arise from poor exposure or metabolites. Novel on-target pharmacology: screening kinase-focused sets revealed that TAK1 can be inhibited in the DFG-out conformation (confirmed crystallographically) and led to compounds binding the TYK2 pseudokinase domain; ATP-site kinase inhibitors can cause on-target degradation by reducing HSP90/CDC37 recruitment. Small-molecule enhancers of other modalities: brefeldin A and L755507 enhanced homology-directed repair in CRISPR-Cas9 editing, guanabenz enhanced siRNA uptake, and 13 drugs enhanced AAV transduction in a screen of over 2,000 FDA-approved drugs.

Conclusions and outlook. Phenotypic and pathway screening is undergoing a renaissance using primary cells, tissue and patient iPSC "disease-in-a-dish" models where genetic tools are difficult and small molecules are convenient. Common library criteria are broad coverage of biological space with selective agents and multiple distinct chemotypes per target. Challenges are annotation accuracy in bioassay databases (addressed by STRENDA, BARD, the IUPHAR/BPS Guide to PHARMACOLOGY and Open PHACTS), screening reproducibility (cell density and media change pharmacogenomic results), the four pillars of chemical-probe target validation (free-drug exposure, target occupancy, functional pharmacology, relevant phenotype), broad off-target activity of many reported probes (The Chemical Probes Portal), and the bias of libraries toward the druggable genome, with statistical under-power where few differentiated agents exist. For this reason chemogenomic screens should ideally run in parallel with unbiased genome-wide RNAi or CRISPR-Cas9 screens, with counter-screens and computational probe selection (for example a tool score for potency and selectivity). The authors expect a substantial increase in chemogenomic screening, industry-academia collaboration (industry-curated libraries seeding academic phenotypic assays of modest throughput) and open-innovation libraries, calling chemogenomics a bridge between disease-relevant phenotypic screening and well-defined molecular targets.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Direct modern descendant of forward chemical genetics: phenotype first, target later, using annotated chemical perturbations in place of gene perturbations.
- Contrasts small-molecule and genetic (RNAi, CRISPR) perturbation and shows that they are complementary for target validation.
- Illustrates why annotation quality, selectivity and false-positive control matter in any screen.

## Future Research Directions

- Expand library target coverage beyond the druggable genome by industry-academia consortia that develop selective probes for novel targets (the SGC model) and "open innovation" libraries of the best reported probes.
- Run chemogenomic screens in parallel with unbiased RNAi or CRISPR-Cas9 genome-wide screens, with counter-screens, to maximise target and pathway coverage and reduce false positives and negatives.
- Improve bioassay annotation, standardised reporting and probe-quality tools (STRENDA, BARD, IUPHAR/BPS Guide to PHARMACOLOGY, Open PHACTS, The Chemical Probes Portal, quantitative tool scores) and screening reproducibility.
- Apply the approach to predictive toxicology and to new modalities, including small-molecule enhancers of gene editing, RNAi and viral gene delivery, with industry libraries seeding academic phenotypic assays of modest throughput.
