# Why recombination hotspots?

## Citation (NLM)
Joseph J, Brazier T, Raynaud M, Glémin S, Baudat F, de Massy B, et al. Why recombination hotspots? PLoS Genet. 2026;22(5):e1012152. doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1012152

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1012152](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1012152)

## Summary

Meiotic recombination exchanges genetic information between homologous chromosomes during gamete production. It begins with **programmed double-strand breaks (DSBs)**, which locally destroy sequence. A break can be repaired from the sister chromatid, or from the homologue — and in the latter case, **gene conversion** occurs. If the chromosome arms on either side of the conversion tract are exchanged, the outcome is a **crossover (CO)**; if not, a **non-crossover (NCO)**. Recombination is near-universal in eukaryotes for two reasons: it increases the efficacy of selection by breaking linkage between alleles of different selective value, and in most sexually reproducing species it is mechanically **required for proper chromosome segregation** at meiosis I.

But recombination is also costly. It is **mutagenic**, and it drives **GC-biased gene conversion (gBGC)**, a fixation bias toward GC alleles that acts like selection but is not — it can fix deleterious GC variants and mimic or oppose real selection. So a high recombination rate buys independent assortment of beneficial and deleterious alleles at the price of increased mutational load and gBGC distortion. This trade-off motivates the central idea of the review: the **recombination landscape** — where along the genome recombination happens — may itself evolve to capture the benefits while limiting the costs.

The review lays out the hierarchy of landscape variation. At **chromosome scale**, because meiosis generally requires at least one crossover per chromosome, shorter chromosomes have higher per-base recombination rates. At **regional scale**, many species show elevated recombination toward telomeres and suppression around centromeres, and because DSB formation is coupled to DNA replication, early-replicating regions recombine more. At the **kilobase scale**, in many eukaryotes recombination concentrates into short segments — **hotspots**.

The core of the review is the competing explanations for why hotspots exist, evaluated against recent data on the diversity and determinants of fine-scale landscapes. The central tension is the **hotspot paradox**: a hotspot allele is destroyed by its own activity, because gene conversion during repair copies the non-initiating (cold) allele onto the initiating (hot) chromosome. Hotspots should therefore self-eliminate — yet they persist. **PRDM9** resolves this in mammals by decoupling hotspot specification from the target sequence: a rapidly evolving zinc-finger protein directs DSBs to motifs it recognizes, and as its targets erode the protein itself evolves new binding specificity, producing rapid hotspot turnover. In lineages lacking functional PRDM9 (dogs, birds, *Drosophila*, plants, yeast), recombination instead concentrates at **promoters and other open-chromatin features**, and hotspot positions are far more conserved. The authors ask which of these arrangements is adaptive, which is a byproduct of chromatin accessibility, and which is simply the current state of an ongoing conflict — and outline what evidence would distinguish them.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Provides the conceptual depth behind the recombination and linkage lecture: not just *that* crossovers happen but *where*, *why there*, and what determines the map
- Introduces **DSB repair outcomes** — crossover, non-crossover, gene conversion — which explains why a genetic map is not simply a physical map
- Explains **GC-biased gene conversion**, a process that mimics selection and is a common source of misinterpretation in genome scans
- Presents the **hotspot paradox** — a self-destroying genetic element that persists — as an elegant example of how an evolutionary puzzle drives mechanistic discovery
- Puts **PRDM9** in context as one solution among several, showing that lineages differ fundamentally in how they place crossovers

## Future Research Directions

- Determine whether PRDM9-free hotspot placement at promoters is adaptive or simply a consequence of chromatin accessibility, using species where accessibility can be manipulated
- Test the evolutionary origin of PRDM9-directed hotspots directly — reconstruct ancestral PRDM9 alleles and measure where they direct DSBs
- Characterize fine-scale landscapes in more phylogenetically diverse taxa, particularly lineages that have lost PRDM9 independently
- Quantify the actual fitness cost of gBGC and recombination-associated mutation, to test whether landscape evolution is really cost-driven
- Separate DSB placement from crossover resolution: many DSBs become non-crossovers, and the two maps may be governed differently
