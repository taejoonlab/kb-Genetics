# Hogness at one hundred

## Citation (NLM)

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf171](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf171)

## Summary

This *GENETICS* Perspectives piece marks the centenary of David S. Hogness's birth (17 November 1925) by publishing, with light editorial annotation, an autobiographical essay Hogness wrote in 1992 — seven years before he retired — on becoming the first holder of the Munzer Professorship at Stanford. Kingsley and Talbot, both trained in or influenced by the Hogness lab, supply the framing.

Hogness's career is a compressed history of how genetics became molecular. Trained at Caltech in chemistry (he arrived intending to be a physicist or a Supreme Court justice), he was pulled into biology when Max Delbrück asked him to present a manuscript from Jacques Monod to the Caltech phage group. He then went to Paris as a postdoc with Monod (1952–1954), working on gene regulation in *Escherichia coli* during the years that produced the operon concept. As a faculty member at Washington University and then Stanford (1955–1968) he worked on bacteriophage lambda, establishing colinearity between genetically defined traits and physical features of DNA — the demonstration that the abstract genetic map corresponds to a real molecule. From 1968 until 1999 he turned to *Drosophila* and pioneered the molecular analysis of animal development.

The technical legacy is enormous. Hogness's laboratory developed **chromosome walking** — the method of moving stepwise along a chromosome by using the end of one cloned fragment to fish out the next overlapping clone. This was the technique that made **positional cloning** possible: starting from a mutant phenotype and a genetic map position, with no biochemical clue about the gene product, and walking to the DNA. His lab used it to clone the first *Drosophila* **homeotic gene**, opening the molecular era of developmental genetics — the 1992 EMBO Ascona workshop on "The Homeobox and the Genetic Control of Development," at which Hogness faxed in this very essay, was largely a celebration of what followed. Cloning, fractionating, and reassembling whole genomes in the Hogness lab established methods that became the foundation of modern genomics; his 1992 essay already anticipates having "the DNA sequences of the entire human genome" and "the awesome puzzle of what to do with that information."

The authors emphasize the second legacy: people. Talbot applied Hogness-style positional cloning to zebrafish developmental mutants; Kingsley used it to isolate genes controlling skeletal traits in mice and then the major loci underlying evolutionary differences in wild stickleback. The Hogness lab's trainees seeded molecular approaches across a wide range of organisms and problems. Hogness received both the GSA Medal (1984) and the Thomas Hunt Morgan Medal (2003).

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Provides the historical arc from **classical genetics to molecular genetics to genomics** through one career — Monod's operon, lambda colinearity, chromosome walking, homeotic genes, the anticipation of the Human Genome Project
- Explains where **positional cloning** came from and why it mattered: it is the method that lets you go from phenotype to gene with no prior knowledge of the gene product, which is exactly the logic of **forward genetics**
- Directly supports the forward-genetics lecture and the *Drosophila* model-organism lecture
- Shows students that methods, not just discoveries, are a primary scientific contribution — chromosome walking enabled thousands of experiments its inventor never ran
- A useful corrective to the idea that scientific fields advance by isolated results: the essay documents how a single lab's training lineage propagated a way of thinking across zebrafish, mouse, and stickleback genetics

## Future Research Directions

- Positional cloning has been largely replaced by whole-genome sequencing; a worthwhile classroom exercise is to ask what conceptual steps of the walk are still required today, just performed computationally
- Revisit the "phenotype-first" logic Hogness embodied in the era of saturating CRISPR screens: which biological questions still demand forward genetics rather than reverse genetics?
- Trace how the homeobox discovery propagated into comparative developmental biology (evo-devo) and what remains unresolved about homeotic gene regulation
- Use the Hogness training lineage as a case study in how methods diffuse across model systems, and ask which of today's methods are at a similar diffusion stage
