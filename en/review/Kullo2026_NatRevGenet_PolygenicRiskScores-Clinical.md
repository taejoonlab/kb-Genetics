# Clinical use of polygenic risk scores: current status, barriers and future directions

## Citation (NLM)

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00900-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00900-8)

## Summary

Genome-wide association studies have identified thousands of variants associated with complex traits — cardiometabolic diseases, cancers, neurological disorders. Individually these variants have effects far too small to be clinically meaningful. A **polygenic risk score (PRS)** aggregates them into a single number, weighting each allele by its estimated effect, to stratify individuals by genetic liability. Because PRSs are only weakly correlated with conventional risk factors (age, blood pressure, lipids, smoking, family history), they carry **incremental predictive value**, and they are beginning to enter clinical practice for early detection and prevention. This review, by a clinician-scientist, assesses that transition honestly.

**Analytical validity** — does the score measure what it claims, reproducibly? Genotyping platform, imputation reference panel, variant weighting method, and score normalization all affect the result. Two laboratories can return different PRSs for the same person, and standardization remains incomplete.

**Clinical validity** — does the score predict disease? This is where PRSs perform best, with well-replicated associations for coronary artery disease, breast cancer, type 2 diabetes, and others. The critical caveat, which the review treats at length, is **poor cross-ancestry portability**: scores derived mainly from European-ancestry GWAS lose substantial accuracy in African, East Asian, and other ancestries, because of differences in allele frequency, LD structure, and effect sizes. Deploying such scores clinically risks amplifying existing health disparities.

**Clinical utility** — does using the score improve outcomes? This is the weakest link. Prediction is not benefit. Utility requires evidence that a PRS changes a clinical decision and that the changed decision improves outcome, which requires trials. Relatively few exist.

**Ethical, legal and social implications.** Communicating probabilistic genetic risk to patients and clinicians is difficult; there are concerns about genetic determinism, insurance and employment discrimination, psychological effects, and equity of access, alongside the ancestry-portability problem.

**Barriers and infrastructure.** Clinician education, integration into electronic health records, laboratory standards, reimbursement, and regulatory oversight — a PRS sits awkwardly between a laboratory test and a risk model, and regulatory frameworks were not designed for it.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The natural endpoint of the GWAS lecture: what do you actually *do* with thousands of small-effect variants?

## Future Research Directions

- Conduct randomized trials testing whether PRS-guided screening or prevention improves clinical outcomes, not just risk classification
- Expand GWAS in non-European ancestries and develop methods that transfer effect estimates across populations, to close the portability gap
- Standardize PRS computation, reporting, and laboratory practice so results are comparable across providers
- Develop and test communication tools that convey probabilistic genetic risk accurately to patients and clinicians
- Integrate PRSs with conventional risk factors and clinical data into unified prediction models, and evaluate the combined model rather than the PRS alone
- Resolve the regulatory status of PRSs and establish oversight proportionate to their clinical use
