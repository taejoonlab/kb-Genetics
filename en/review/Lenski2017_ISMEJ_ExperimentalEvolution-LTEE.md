# Experimental Evolution and the Dynamics of Adaptation and Genome Evolution in Microbial Populations

## Citation (NLM)
Lenski RE. Experimental evolution and the dynamics of adaptation and genome evolution in microbial populations. ISME J. 2017;11(10):2181-2194. doi:10.1038/ismej.2017.69

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2017.69](https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2017.69)

## Summary

This Winogradsky Review by Richard Lenski summarizes what the Long-Term Evolution Experiment (LTEE) has revealed after ~425 years and ~60,000 generations (the text notes the experiment had passed 66,000 generations at writing). Twelve populations of *Escherichia coli* were founded in 1988 from one ancestral strain with no plasmids or functional prophages (and E. coli is not naturally transformable, so there is no horizontal gene transfer). Each population is propagated daily in glucose-limited minimal medium at 37 degC by a 1% (100-fold) transfer, giving log2(100) = ~6.7 generations per day, and samples are frozen at -80 degC, so ancestors and descendants can be revived and compared directly ("time travel"). Fitness is measured as relative fitness: the ratio of realized growth rates of evolved and ancestral cells in head-to-head competition, distinguished by a selectively neutral genetic marker (after acclimation to remove non-heritable physiological differences).

Dynamics of adaptation: in the first 2,000 generations fitness of one population rose ~30% in roughly three steps of ~10%, as expected for sequential selective sweeps of large-effect beneficial mutations in a large asexual population (a 10% advantage takes ~250 generations to reach majority), with clonal interference among competing beneficial lineages. Across populations the grand-mean fitness reached ~1.7 (about 70% faster growth) by 50,000 generations, and the rate of improvement declines over time. A two-parameter power-law model (no upper bound, gains scale with log time) fit better than a hyperbola with an asymptote and predicted the 50,000-generation trajectory from the first 5,000 generations; extrapolation to 2.5 billion generations gives a doubling time of ~23 min, vs ~55 min for the ancestor, which is still biophysically plausible. Relative fitness appears approximately transitive in this simple environment.

Evolving complexity: despite the deliberately simple design, ecology and genetics grew complex. Population Ara-2 diverged into stably coexisting L and S ecotypes (>50,000 generations) maintained by negative frequency-dependent selection and cross-feeding (the L type lost the ability to use acetate secreted as a by-product, which S exploits); in Ara-1 two clades coexisted from ~7,000 to ~14,000 generations before one went extinct. Six populations evolved hypermutability (~100-fold higher point mutation rate) via mismatch repair or oxidized-base repair defects, and Ara+1 increased IS150 transposition; several lineages later evolved reduced mutation rates, reflecting an evolutionary tension between beneficial supply and deleterious load. After ~31,000 generations, population Ara-3 evolved aerobic growth on citrate (Cit+), a defining E. coli taboo. Replay experiments showed historical contingency (no Cit+ mutants from the ancestor or clones before generation 20,000, but 17 from later clones); the actualizing mutation was a tandem duplication of citT (a citrate-succinate antiporter) placing a copy under a new promoter ("promoter capture"), with epistasis between this module and the genetic background. Cit+ and Cit- types coexisted for >10,000 generations.

Further detail from the text. The LTEE was built to ask whether adaptation is slow and gradual or has rapid change and stasis, whether fitness reaches a maximum, whether replicates reach the same peaks, whether parallel fitness trajectories imply parallel genetic changes, and how phenotypic and genetic evolution are coupled; populations are restarted from the most recent whole-population frozen sample after accidents. Competitions usually start 1:1 (other ratios test frequency dependence) between the evolved sample and an ancestral variant differing at a neutral marker, after both have been thawed and acclimated for a full transfer cycle. In the Ara-1 trajectory, clone sequencing found five mutations, each beneficial (Khan et al. 2011), despite only three steps, implying some sweeps involved several mutations. The power-law extrapolation to 2.5 billion generations is framed as 50,000 generations of scientists each running the LTEE for 50,000 generations. Transitivity (relative fitnesses multiply) is expected to hold because the low glucose limits density and by-product accumulation; subtle frequency-dependent by-product interactions exist but are small, and any error from competing against the ancestor should underestimate continued adaptation. In Ara-2, L grows faster on glucose but cannot grow in its own spent medium, which S can use (mainly acetate); coexistence is by negative frequency dependence, evolution continued in both lineages causing large abundance fluctuations, and the Ara-1 polymorphism probably ended sooner because the dominant glucose specialist retained acetate use. Hypermutators evolve by indirect selection: the mutator allele hitchhikes with a beneficial mutation it generates, which is likelier when the population is far from its optimum; as adaptation proceeds, deleterious load dominates and lower mutation rates evolve (Ara-1 partially compensated in parallel in two lineages). For citrate, none of the other populations evolved Cit+ despite every point mutation having been tested many times; replays plated large numbers of cells on citrate-without-glucose agar for weeks, and all replay Cit+ mutants carried citT-involving rearrangements, so no point mutation suffices. The duplicated region was further amplified in Ara-3 to improve growth. The citT module on a high-copy plasmid allowed growth on citrate in three of four backgrounds but with long delays, whereas a 32,000-generation clone related to the Cit+ lineage switched seamlessly, indicating epistasis; later work found chromosomal mutations that, with the module, enable vigorous growth (Quandt et al. 2014, 2015). Cit+ and Cit- coexisted partly because Cit+ had a longer lag; the CitT antiporter exports succinate, fumarate or malate, and both lineages evolved improved growth on succinate, so interactions grew more complex.

Genome evolution: about 7,500 days of dilution and regrowth, ~3x10^8 cells per regrowth, ~5x10^6 bp genome and a mutation rate of ~10^-10 per bp imply >10^9 point mutation events per population, yet Tenaillon et al. (2016) sequencing of 264 clones found >17,000 mutations in total (mostly in hypermutators) and only ~75 mutations per non-hypermutable clone at generation 50,000. Most of these appear beneficial (a mutation accumulation curve with a declining curvilinear term, and a nonsynonymous/synonymous excess of ~17-fold early and ~3.4-fold at 50,000 generations). Parallel evolution identifies targets: 57 genes (~2% of coding sequence) carry half of nonsynonymous mutations (pyruvate kinase, peptidoglycan synthesis, DNA supercoiling, stringent response), and parallel deletions reduced genome size by ~1.4%. Many deletions arose by recombination between repeated sequences, especially IS elements, so some may reflect high-frequency events rather than selection alone. Not all mutations are beneficial, since some are hitchhikers or too recent to have been tested by selection; parallel evolution is therefore the tool used to separate targets of selection from passengers, and the hypermutable lineages were excluded or truncated for that analysis. Isogenic single-mutation strains in several of these genes were usually confirmed beneficial in competition, though why they are beneficial is mostly unknown; a plausible but unproven idea is that they fine-tune regulation and expression to the simple, predictable environment. The ~75 mutations per clone are small next to differences between E. coli and Salmonella or between two natural E. coli isolates, as expected on this short evolutionary time scale.

Where to go from here. Lenski hopes the LTEE will continue for many generations of scientists because evolution takes time and surprises are possible: could cross-feeding evolve into predation, or could sex evolve through prophage reactivation or DNA uptake from lysed cells? New technology might let one image and understand a whole cell's metabolism through time, and new generations will bring new ideas. He then lists, in chronological order, several dozen other microbial evolution experiments of interest to ecologists, from Chao et al. (1977) on phage-bacteria coevolution through Rainey and Travisano (1998) on adaptive radiation in structured environments, Gerstein et al. (2006) on convergence to diploidy in yeast, Ratcliff et al. (2012) on evolved multicellularity, Levy et al. (2015) on barcode-tracked lineages, Baym et al. (2016) on antibiotic landscapes and Kacar et al. (2017) on an ancient gene in modern E. coli. Most such experiments ran only a few hundred generations, and he argues that extending them, perhaps running long experiments in the background of short-term projects given freezable samples and falling costs, would reveal more. Finally he proposes the ESENCE ("embedded species evolving in a natural community experiment") strategy to bring evolution experiments to whole communities tractably: choose one focal member of a natural community, mark an isolate genetically, introduce marked and unmarked variants into replicate microcosms under one or more treatments, follow their relative abundance (a shift signals a beneficial mutation), sequence and compare genomes, construct isogenic strains to separate mutations (using parallelism across replicates), compete them to test benefit in the community context, monitor effects on other community members to probe eco-evolutionary feedbacks, and test whether benefits are treatment specific. Scheinin et al. (2015) on a marine diatom under elevated CO2 and Gomez and Buckling (2011, 2013) on soil microcosms illustrate the approach.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows evolution as an observable, replicable experiment: selection, drift, mutation, and clonal interference acting on identical founder populations.
- Illustrates relative fitness, selective sweeps, frequency-dependent selection, mutation-rate evolution, epistasis and historical contingency (Cit+) with concrete data.
- Links phenotype to genome: parallel evolution as a statistical signature of selection, and the number of mutational events versus fixed differences.

## Future Research Directions

- Could cross-feeding evolve into predation, or sex evolve via prophage reactivation or DNA uptake? (suggested in the review)
- Whole-cell imaging and metabolic profiling over time as new technologies mature.
- Why most populations never evolved Cit+; why the reasons many parallel mutations are beneficial remain unknown (possible fine-tuning of regulation).
- Extending evolution experiments well beyond a few hundred generations, possibly run in the background of short-term projects.
- ESENCE experiments tracking a marked focal species inside a natural microbial community to study eco-evolutionary feedbacks.
