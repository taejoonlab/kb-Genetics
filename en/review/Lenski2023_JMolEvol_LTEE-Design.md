# Revisiting the Design of the Long-Term Evolution Experiment with Escherichia coli

## Citation (NLM)
Lenski RE. Revisiting the design of the long-term evolution experiment with Escherichia coli. J Mol Evol. 2023;91(3):241-253. doi:10.1007/s00239-023-10095-3

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/s00239-023-10095-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00239-023-10095-3)

## Summary

Invited by the editors of a special collection to answer "if I were setting up a similar experiment today, would I do things differently?", Lenski reflects on the design of the LTEE, begun in 1988 with 12 *E. coli* populations that recently reached 75,000 generations. He frames three sets of founding questions: how repeatable is evolution (determinism of selection versus stochastic mutation, drift and contingency), what are the dynamics (gradual versus punctuated, bounded versus open-ended fitness gains), and how phenotypic and genomic evolution are coupled (parallelism at the level of mutations, genes or pathways; specialization through pleiotropic trade-offs versus neutral accumulation). The design grew from an earlier ~400-generation experiment (Lenski 1988) comparing phage T4-resistant and sensitive populations; the sensitive progenitor became the LTEE ancestor. The first LTEE paper inferred ~3.7 selective sweeps per population in 2,000 generations; later genome sequencing found ~7.5 mutations on average in that period, many of them beneficial drivers.

The review walks through each design choice and its rationale. Culture regime: daily serial transfer of 0.1 mL into 9.9 mL fresh medium (~6.6 generations per day) rather than a chemostat; serial transfer is cheap, precise, and contamination-resistant, wall-adhering cells are discarded at each transfer, and it makes thousands of fitness assays feasible (two studies ran >1,000 assays), whereas chemostats are costly, contamination-prone and subject to wall growth. Medium: chemically defined Davis Mingioli medium with glucose at 25 ug/mL (DM25), a single limiting resource, chosen to minimize negative frequency-dependent selection (FDS) so that one fitness metric suffices; the stationary density is only ~5x10^7 cells/mL (~5x10^8 cells per 10 mL culture), low to limit accumulation of metabolic by-products. Nonetheless some or most populations later showed FDS, though its effects are small relative to total fitness gains. Citrate is present as an iron chelator (removing it made growth erratic, likely reflecting water quality); its presence let one population evolve aerobic citrate use, which none of the others did even by 75,000 generations. Water quality is the one uncontrolled variable; smooth mean fitness trajectories and cross-laboratory fitness checks (UC Irvine, Michigan State, Texas) suggest it is inconsequential, though an unexplained extinction of the Cit- lineage that could not be replayed in 20 restarts from each of two time points might reflect it or another inadvertent perturbation.

Ancestor and markers: REL606 is a derivative of *E. coli* B (the strain used by Luria and Delbruck), chosen from the author's experience rather than the better-mapped K-12; Lenski argues that linkage mapping suits discrete phenotypes, not subtle growth gains, and that whole-genome sequencing later solved the genetics. REL606 carries an Ara- mutation (unable to grow on arabinose) and a neutral Ara+ revertant, REL607, serves as a marker: populations alternate marker states to detect cross-contamination, and competitors of opposite marker states are enumerated as red and white colonies in fitness assays. The ancestor has no plasmids or functional prophages and E. coli is not naturally competent, so there is no horizontal gene transfer to dissociate marker from fitness alleles. Each population began from a single haploid cell (separate colonies), so no standing variation exists and all mutations arose independently; convergence therefore means true parallel rather than collateral evolution, unlike selection experiments on sexual multicellular organisms that start with shared variation. Spontaneous mutation is used rather than mutagens because adaptation in large asexual populations is limited by clonal interference rather than mutation supply, deleterious load rises with mutation rate, and more mutations produce more passenger hitchhikers that obscure drivers (gene-level parallelism is blurred in hypermutators).

Measurement: frozen samples are revived in parallel and acclimated for a full transfer cycle to separate acclimation from adaptation. Relative fitness is the ratio of realized growth rates in direct competition against the ancestor, which integrates lag, exponential growth and later phases and exposes both competitors to the same perturbations; transitivity was supported by de Visser and Lenski (2002), in contrast with non-transitivity in yeast (Paquin and Adams 1983). As fitness rises, ancestral colony counts become low, so intermediate-generation competitors are sometimes used. Growth rate and lag duration account for most fitness gains. The choice of 12 replicates was practical (incubators hold multiples of 12); today he might use 96-well plates with robotics, but would proceed cautiously. Project management: freezer stocks every 100 then every 500 generations, split among vials (now ~10); he would adopt more informative sample identifiers (e.g., LTEE/Ara-6/75 instead of REL12244, after a one-digit mislabel when copies were sent to the Barrick lab at UT Austin); samples are shared under material transfer agreements; mistakes are handled by restarting from refrigerated previous-day cultures rather than freezer stocks, and cross-contaminants by restarting only the affected population from frozen samples, leaving some populations 500-generation multiples behind.

Additional design details. Lenski opens by noting that the LTEE has been productive in three ways (new findings, training for many students and postdocs, and a model for other experiments) and that his preference for abstract questions, simple designs and likely-to-yield-data projects is personal ("To thine own self be true"). The 1988 precursor experiment (400 generations) showed that serial transfer was sustainable and fitness gains appeared quickly, but he suspected only one or two mutations had fixed, so after discussion with Michael Rose he ran a longer experiment from a single common ancestor to explore the fitness landscape more widely and to get a larger sample of how variation emerges between populations from identical genotypes; the original goal was only 2,000 generations. Chemostats were weighed against serial transfer (no daily transfers, constant growth rate, easier modelling, no bottlenecks) but rejected for cost, difficulty, contamination and wall growth. The low population size (~5x10^8 cells per flask) might slow adaptation if beneficial mutations were rare, but he was not concerned because 400 generations had sufficed and hoped for a strong-selection-weak-mutation regime in which sweeps are visible in fitness trajectories alone; in fact beneficial mutations proved abundant even at this size. On citrate: the ancestor cannot use citrate even anaerobically, the few natural aerobic Cit+ E. coli probably acquired the ability by plasmid transfer (not possible in the LTEE), and citrate serves as an iron chelator (ferric citrate) because iron in laboratory water is enough only when citrate is present. Selection experiments on multicellular organisms deliberately begin with abundant shared variation, which favors collateral evolution and repeatable outcomes ("stacks the deck"), whereas the LTEE preserves the tension between stochastic mutation and deterministic selection. Against mutagenesis he lists: the earlier experiment showed gains in reasonable time, clonal interference limits adaptation rate, deleterious load rises with mutation rate, mutagens add workflow and safety complexity, and passengers increase (if more mutation is wanted, use DNA-repair-defective hypermutator strains). In the LTEE, Ara markers are now less crucial for detecting contamination because each population has evolved unique alleles that can be sequenced. The Ara marker also presumes no HGT; no HGT-mediated recombination has been seen between lineages that coexisted for thousands of generations. Other conditions are sometimes used in assays (other media, temperatures) to measure correlated responses and the specificity of adaptation. Early work (Vasi et al. 1994) showed that faster exponential growth and shorter lag accounted for nearly all fitness gains over the first 2,000 generations, supporting the view that fitness is largely scramble competition for glucose. Freezer management: samples are frozen every 500 generations (every 100 earlier), split between two vials (now ~10), and users who reuse samples should make their own working stocks; identifiers could also be unique per copy and labelled redundantly. Samples are shared under an institutional material transfer agreement and only if the requested samples are mentioned in a publication (through 60,000 generations; not yet the 75,000-generation samples), with recipients paying shipping.

Mistakes and contamination are handled differently: after a transfer error the previous day's refrigerated cultures (kept rather than discarded) are used to repeat the transfer for all populations, so that all stay on the same freezing schedule; this perturbs selection slightly but far less than freezing and thawing. Suspected cross-contaminants (wrong-colored colonies on arabinose indicator agar) are now checked by sequencing; recent ones have usually been false alarms, namely Ara- colonies in populations founded by the Ara+ ancestor that later evolved hypermutability.

Alternative designs ("descendant experiments"). Lenski stresses that simplicity was a key criterion, since more complex designs would have been hard to sustain for decades, and that his lab has run many shorter experiments with different designs. Izutsu et al. (2021) varied the dilution factor and found 100-fold dilution reasonably close to optimal for the rate of fitness gain; de Visser et al. (1999) varied dilution together with founder mutation rate and showed that adaptation speeds up far more slowly than mutation supply because of clonal interference. Plasmid-mediated horizontal gene transfer accelerated adaptation in Cooper (2007) by letting beneficial mutations recombine, whereas Souza et al. (1997) saw no speed-up with K-12 donors and LTEE-derived B recipients, even though later analysis found substantial K-12 introgression (Maddamsetti and Lenski 2018). Other descendant experiments varied resources and temperature, tested acclimation (Leroi et al. 1994), used diverged LTEE strains to measure the effect of history on adaptation to new resources, temperatures (Travisano et al. 1995) and antibiotics (Card et al. 2019), examined compensatory evolution after transposon-mutagenized deleterious mutations (Moore et al. 2000), and compared initial diversity with new mutations (Izutsu and Lenski 2022: strong selection among genotypes in admixtures in the first ~50 generations, but populations relying only on new mutations had caught up by 500 generations). These projects also let students and postdocs take ownership of questions arising from the LTEE and can move with them to new labs. Because most evolution experiments are cheap, time and imagination are the limits, and he encourages microbiology labs not focused on evolution to run curiosity-driven experiments, aided by whole-genome sequencing.

Closing thoughts. Experimental evolution complements rather than replaces paleontology, phylogenetic comparative methods and field studies of natural populations, by giving controlled, statistically replicated analyses of dynamics and outcomes. Unlike hypothesis-targeted experiments, the LTEE addresses general open-ended questions, and its deliberately simple design minimizes human error while its results have proved rich and nuanced. Using Levins' (1966) trio of generality, realism and precision from model building, Lenski argues the LTEE favors generality and precision over realism: its specific phenotypic and genetic changes are organism- and environment-specific (as descendant experiments show), but the questions about dynamics, repeatability and phenotype-genome coupling are general, and the simple environment allows precise fitness measurement. It lacks other species, diverse resources and spatial structure, so it is unrealistic relative to nature; others may rationally prioritize realism (mesocosms, in-host propagation of pathogens). The advice: consider generality, realism and precision, perhaps even before designing an experiment: "To thine own goals be true."

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A practical case study in experimental design: how choices of replication, environment, founder genotype and measurement shape what an experiment can conclude about evolution.
- Clarifies key population-genetics concepts: parallel vs collateral evolution, frequency-dependent selection, clonal interference, driver vs passenger (hitchhiker) mutations, acclimation vs adaptation.
- Demonstrates the value of a frozen "living fossil record" and neutral markers for rigorous competition-based fitness assays.

## Future Research Directions

- Microtiter-plate and robotic platforms for greater replication, with care over plastic, mixing and cross-contamination.
- Curiosity-driven "descendant" experiments with altered dilution, mutation supply, horizontal gene transfer, resources, temperature and ancestry, including in microbes outside the usual model organisms and in complex settings (mesocosms, in-host propagation) that trade generality and precision for realism.
- Using whole-genome sequencing to identify targets of selection in such experiments.
- Understanding the unexplained extinction of the Cit- lineage and the possible role of water quality.
- Continued stewardship of the LTEE in its second scientific generation (now at UT Austin) and analysis of the 75,000-generation samples.
