# Integrating model systems and genomic insights to decipher mechanisms of cancer metastasis

## Citation (NLM)
Leung MM, Swanton C, McGranahan N. Integrating model systems and genomic insights to decipher mechanisms of cancer metastasis. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(7):494-505. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00825-2

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00825-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00825-2)

---

## Summary

Metastasis, not the primary tumour, causes most cancer deaths, yet its mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Two research traditions have addressed it and until recently spoke past each other. **Model systems** — organoids, genetically engineered mouse models, human cell lines, xenografts — allow controlled perturbation and mechanistic dissection, and shaped the conceptual framework of the metastatic cascade. **Human tumour genomics**, now at single-cell and single-nucleotide resolution, describes what actually happens in patients but is largely observational. This review argues the two must be integrated and shows where each corrects the other.

**Metastatic potential.** A central question is whether metastasis requires specific "metastasis genes" or emerges from properties the tumour already has. Large sequencing efforts have failed to find dedicated metastasis driver mutations in most cancers; metastases typically share the driver landscape of their primary. This shifts explanatory weight toward non-genetic factors — epigenetic state, plasticity, and the microenvironment — and toward the possibility that metastatic capacity is present early rather than acquired late.

**Epithelial and mesenchymal transitions.** EMT and its reverse (MET) were long treated as a binary switch enabling invasion. Single-cell data have replaced this with a spectrum of hybrid states, and the review examines which of those states cells actually occupy in human tumours versus in models, where EMT is often induced far more completely than anything observed in patients.

**The tumour microenvironment (TME).** Immune cells, fibroblasts, vasculature, and extracellular matrix determine whether a disseminated cell survives and grows. This is where models and human data most often diverge: immunodeficient xenografts remove precisely the compartment that most strongly constrains metastasis in patients, which is a systematic bias rather than a random error.

**Dissemination.** Genomic phylogenetics of matched primary and metastatic samples reconstructs when and how cells left the primary — early versus late dissemination, monoclonal versus polyclonal seeding, metastasis-to-metastasis spread — questions that model systems cannot answer because they lack the patient's actual history, but that genomics answers only correlatively.

The review's practical message is a division of labour: use human genomics to establish what happens and when, use model systems to test whether a proposed mechanism is sufficient, and be explicit about which model deficiencies bias which conclusions.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Extends the cancer genetics lecture beyond driver mutations in the primary tumour to the process that actually kills patients
- Explains **clonal evolution and phylogenetic reconstruction** in tumours — an application of the same tree-building logic used in species phylogenetics
- A clear, honest treatment of **model organism limitations**: xenografts in immunodeficient mice remove the immune system, the very factor under study
- Introduces the **tumour microenvironment** and the idea that a cancer phenotype is a property of a tissue, not only of a cell's genotype
- The absence of dedicated metastasis driver mutations is a striking result worth discussing — it shows that a phenotype can be genetically encoded without having its own genes

## Future Research Directions

- Develop immunocompetent metastasis models that reproduce the human tumour microenvironment, so that immune constraints on colonization can be studied
- Determine whether metastatic capacity is acquired late or present early, using timing inference from matched primary–metastasis genomes
- Test in human tumours which points on the EMT spectrum cells actually occupy, and whether hybrid states are required for dissemination
- Identify non-genetic (epigenetic, plastic) determinants of metastatic competence, given that genetic drivers appear insufficient
- Establish standards for reporting which model limitations apply to which conclusion, so that mechanistic claims are interpretable across systems
