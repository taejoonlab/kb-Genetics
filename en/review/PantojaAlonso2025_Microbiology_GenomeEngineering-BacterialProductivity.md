# A Comprehensive Review of Genomic-Scale Genetic Engineering as a Strategy to Improve Bacterial Productivity

## Citation (NLM)
Pantoja-Alonso MA, Camas-Reyes JA, Cano-Segura R, Cardenas-Aquino MDR, Martinez-Antonio A. A comprehensive review of genomic-scale genetic engineering as a strategy to improve bacterial productivity. Microbiology (Reading). 2025;171(11):001628. doi:10.1099/mic.0.001628

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1099/mic.0.001628](https://doi.org/10.1099/mic.0.001628)

## Summary

This open-access review from Cinvestav Irapuato (Mexico) traces the history of genome engineering tools for bacteria and links each stage to its use in improving production strains (productivity defined as the total concentration of a compound produced in a culture within a given time). The authors organize the field chronologically (Fig. 1 timeline), argue that earlier reviews covered either technical foundations or specific applications, and position this one as connecting conceptual advances with practical strain-optimization examples. The "Genetic Era" of the 1960s produced recombinant DNA ("cut and splice", building on work from Luria, Arber, Smith, Nathans, Boyer, Mertz and Davis); Chang and Cohen built the first recombinant E. coli in 1973 by fusing pSC101 with the *Staphylococcus aureus* plasmid pI258 (Fig. 2), Bolivar and Boyer made pBR322 in 1977 (small, stable, many restriction sites), and human insulin chains were expressed in 1978-1979 and commercialized by Eli Lilly in 1982 as the first FDA-approved recombinant product, followed by somatostatin (1977), interleukin-2 (1984) and growth hormone (1986) in E. coli and a 250-fold alpha-amylase gain in *Bacillus subtilis*. Intracellular regulation and by-product toxicity still limited yields, so metabolic engineering (ME; Bailey 1991: manipulating enzymatic, regulatory and transport systems with rDNA technology) emerged, using host choice, pathway characterization, expression plasmids, inducible promoters, enzyme engineering, blocking competing pathways and overexpression; Table 1 lists chassis from E. coli and *B. subtilis* to non-model hosts.

Random genome engineering (Table 2) was used where sequences and functions were unknown: adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE, since 1951), UV (1976) and chemical mutagens such as nitrosoguanidine and EMS (1985), with examples including UV/nitrosoguanidine mutants of *Streptomyces* (tylosin, rifamycin, avermectin), EMS for alpha-amylase in *Bacillus* (130 to 5300 U/ml) and ALE of *Bacillus coagulans* for lactic acid; for example, ALE took an engineered E. coli p-hydroxybenzoic acid strain from 14.5 to 21.3 g/L. Limitations are poor transfer between species, long selection and variable efficiency, yet mutagenesis plus selection stays cost-effective for short-term strain development. Semi-random tools followed: site-specific integrases from temperate phages (1979; suicide plasmids with attP sites, limited to predefined attB sites; used for a BCG vaccine strain in 1991 and a more efficient phiCTX system in *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*) and transposons (1983; transposases recognizing terminal inverted repeats; insertion into promoters can raise expression, as with ampC in *Acinetobacter*; the mini-Tn5 vector pBAMD moved the PHB pathway into E. coli). Homologous-recombination approaches then needed recombinogenic hosts (recD mutants, 1989), suicide plasmids with R6K-gamma origins (1996) and I-SceI-based replacement (Posfai, late 1990s), applied to deletions of yadA, eaE, recA and mxiD in various bacteria but limited by long homology arms, slow procedures and low rates.

Rational genome engineering (RGE) followed Sanger sequencing (1977), PCR (1986), the first bacterial genome (*Haemophilus influenzae*, 1995), databases (RegulonDB, EcoCyc, KEGG), computational and systems biology and synthetic biology (BioBricks, 2003). Recombineering, notably Datsenko and Wanner's lambda Red (Exo, Beta, Gam) method with linear DNA carrying 30-50 bp homology arms and FLP-mediated marker excision (leaving an FRT scar), allowed deletions in any E. coli region, outperformed the earlier Rac RecE/RecT method and produced the Keio single-gene deletion collection and deletion strains with higher pyruvate, lycopene, lactic acid and succinate. Adaptations extended it to *Salmonella* (I-SceI for scarless edits, FRUIT using thyA), *P. aeruginosa* (longer arms, controlled lambda expression), *Clostridium acetobutylicum* (allele-coupled exchange), Gram-positives such as *Corynebacterium glutamicum* (RecA/SSAP-independent ssDNA recombination for GRAS bacteria) and others; Table 3 lists applications (for example C. glutamicum L-leucine 0.07 to 14 g/L; *Streptomyces avermitilis* avermectin B1a 6.447 to 9.613 g/L). Limitations noted by Elmore et al. and Yan et al.: one or two fragments, narrow host range, replicating plasmids, residual scars, and scarce information and tools for non-model strains; *Saccharopolyspora spinosa* (spinosad: UV and ALE to 0.547 g/L, then CRISPR to 0.693 g/L) illustrates difficulties of low transformation efficiency and GC-rich genomes, and new chassis (*Mycoplasma genitalium*, *Rhodococcus*, *Streptomyces clavuligerus*, *Vibrio natriegens*, *Shewanella*, *Burkholderia sacchari*) are being explored.

MAGE (Wang et al., 2009) uses ssDNA oligos incorporated via Red-beta for automated multiplex edits (20 genes modified in the DXP pathway to raise lycopene), giving the concept of "synthetic evolution" distinct from random adaptive evolution. Its limits are the need to pre-modify the host (for example deleting mismatch repair, which increases non-specific mutations), restriction mostly to E. coli-related bacteria (needs Red-beta homologs; P. aeruginosa homologs found bioinformatically) and oligo design that must consider G+C content and secondary structure. Variants: conjugative assembly genome engineering (2011), co-selection MAGE (2012), transient-mutator MAGE (2016), pORTMAGE (2016, non-model strains), implementations in *C. glutamicum* and *B. subtilis*, and SMGE in *B. subtilis* (2015) used for L-tyrosine and resveratrol production (2021).

CRISPR/Cas (2012) is presented as the culminating tool: Ishino (1987) repeats, Mojica (2002) hypothesis of adaptive immunity, Jansen (2003) Cas genes (adaptation and effector modules; classes 1 and 2, types I-VI, Fig. 5a), Brouns (2008) crRNA, Gasiunas and Sapranauskas (2012; 20-nt guides; S. thermophilus system functional in E. coli) and Jinek et al. (2012), who fused crRNA and tracrRNA into a single sgRNA directing Cas9. Editing efficiencies of 50-95% exceed recombineering (usually under 40%), depending on chassis, transformation and variant. Jiang et al. (2013) combined Cas9 with recombineering so that lethal double-strand breaks select edited cells (up to 65% success, adapted to *Streptococcus*, *Lactobacillus*, *Streptomyces*, *Clostridium*); dCas9 enables CRISPRa (2019; low efficiency, spacing-dependent) and CRISPRi (2020; reprogrammable, scalable, inducible, multiplexable, but blocks transcription of downstream genes in polycistronic operons). Table 4 lists productivity gains (for example E. coli L-threonine 127.3 g/L, isobutanol 48.37 g/L, 3-hydroxypropionic acid 36.7 g/L in *Klebsiella*, spinosad 0.633 g/L by CRISPRi). Practical challenges: Cas9 toxicity (needs tight inducible promoters), curable plasmids, auxiliary phage-derived recombination in bacteria lacking NHEJ/HDR, and codon optimization in *Streptomyces*. Off-target cutting (Cas9 tolerates up to three mismatches) is the main limitation, to be addressed by in silico and AI-based prediction, experimental detection, engineered Cas variants, nickases, alternative PAMs, sgRNA design and delivery.

Conclusion and perspectives. The authors name three remaining technical limits: efficiency and versatility in non-model bacteria with complex genomes, the lack of standardized genetic part libraries for diverse species, and long-term stability of engineered fluxes given epistatic interactions between introduced and endogenous genes. AI models, genome-scale metabolic models and flux balance analysis could pinpoint bottlenecks and reduce trial and error; genome reduction (for example 50% lower mutation rate after removing error-prone polymerases in E. coli, 17% shorter generation time in *Lactococcus lactis* N8 after 6.9% genome reduction) and minimal synthetic genomes with orthogonal regulatory networks can give more predictable chassis. They also stress biosafety (ecological disturbance, off-target effects), dual-use ethics and compliance with the Cartagena and Nagoya protocols.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Places modern genome editing in historical context, from random mutagenesis (mutate and select) to rational, site-specific editing: forward versus reverse genetics in practical terms.
- Illustrates molecular mechanisms exploited as tools: homologous recombination (lambda Red), site-specific recombination, transposition, and CRISPR/Cas adaptive immunity.
- Shows how genetic engineering of bacteria connects to applied outcomes (biochemicals, antibiotics, amino acids, recombinant proteins) and raises biosafety and ethical issues.

## Future Research Directions

- Reduce CRISPR/Cas off-target effects (AI-based prediction, engineered Cas variants, nickases, sgRNA design) and extend efficient editing to non-model, industrially relevant bacteria with GC-rich or poorly characterized genomes.
- Combine CRISPR with recombineering/MAGE and CRISPRi/CRISPRa for multiplexed pathway tuning, and build standardized genetic part libraries for diverse species.
- Integrate AI, genome-scale metabolic models, flux balance analysis and adaptive laboratory evolution to design high-productivity strains, with attention to long-term stability given epistasis between introduced and endogenous genes.
- Use genome reduction and minimal synthetic genomes with orthogonal regulation as predictable chassis, while following biosafety, dual-use and regulatory (Cartagena, Nagoya) guidelines.
