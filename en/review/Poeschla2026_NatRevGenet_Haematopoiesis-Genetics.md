# Genetic influences on haematopoiesis

## Citation (NLM)

## Summary

Blood has been the paradigm system for human genetics for a century. Its phenotypes are quantitative and routinely measured in every clinical laboratory, its cells are accessible without biopsy, its developmental hierarchy is well defined, and its diseases span the full range from Mendelian to polygenic to somatic. This review traces how each major mode of genetic analysis was first worked out in blood.

**Haemoglobin disorders.** Sickle cell disease was the first "molecular disease" — Pauling's demonstration of an altered protein followed by Ingram's identification of a single amino acid substitution, the first direct link from gene to protein to disease. Thalassaemias established the concepts of quantitative gene dosage, regulatory mutations, and the clinical importance of gene *expression* level rather than protein sequence. The **fetal-to-adult haemoglobin switch** and the discovery that *BCL11A* represses fetal haemoglobin — with the crucial variant lying in an erythroid-specific enhancer, not the coding sequence — became the model for going from a GWAS signal to a mechanism to a therapy, culminating in approved gene-editing treatments.

**Rare variants and monogenic blood disorders.** Bone marrow failure syndromes, inherited cytopenias, immunodeficiencies, and haemophilias illustrate how rare, high-penetrance variants identify genes essential to specific lineages.

**Population variation affecting blood phenotypes.** Blood cell counts and indices are among the most powerful quantitative traits in biobank genetics — measured in millions of people, highly heritable, and yielding thousands of associated loci. These have become a testing ground for **variant-to-function** mapping: massively parallel reporter assays, CRISPR screens in erythroid and myeloid cells, and single-cell genomics now connect common variants to specific enhancers, genes, and cell states.

**Somatic mutations and clonal haematopoiesis.** Haematopoietic stem cells accumulate mutations throughout life; occasionally one confers a proliferative advantage and its descendants expand. **Clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP)** — driven by mutations in *DNMT3A*, *TET2*, *ASXL1*, *JAK2* — becomes common with age and raises the risk not only of leukaemia but of cardiovascular disease and mortality. Blood is where somatic evolution in a normal tissue can actually be observed.

**Therapeutic applications.** The review closes with how this knowledge is being used: *BCL11A*-targeted editing for sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia, gene therapy for inherited immunodeficiencies, and the prospect of targeting clonal haematopoiesis.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- A single tissue that illustrates **every** category covered in a genetics course: Mendelian disease, gene regulation, quantitative traits, GWAS, somatic mutation, and gene therapy
- Sickle cell disease and the haemoglobin switch provide the historically foundational examples for the central dogma and gene regulation lectures
- The *BCL11A* story is the best available worked example of the complete arc from **GWAS association → causal enhancer variant → mechanism → approved therapy**
- **Clonal haematopoiesis** connects to the loss-of-Y review in this vault and makes somatic evolution in normal tissue concrete
- Blood cell traits are the practical demonstration of why biobank-scale quantitative phenotypes are so powerful

## Key References

2. **Ingram (1957)** — Gene mutations in human haemoglobin: the chemical difference between normal and sickle cell haemoglobin — First gene-to-single-amino-acid link
3. **Sankaran et al. (2008)** — Human fetal hemoglobin expression is regulated by the developmental stage-specific repressor BCL11A — Identification of the switch regulator
4. **Bauer et al. (2013)** — An erythroid enhancer of BCL11A subject to genetic variation determines fetal haemoglobin level — The causal non-coding variant

## Future Research Directions

- Complete variant-to-function mapping for the thousands of blood-cell-trait loci, most of which remain uncharacterized
- Determine what governs clonal expansion in CHIP — why some driver mutations expand and others do not, and what environmental or inflammatory factors accelerate it
- Establish whether CHIP can be therapeutically targeted or its cardiovascular consequences prevented
- Extend the *BCL11A* paradigm to other diseases: identify cell-type-specific enhancers whose editing produces therapeutic gene expression change
- Improve single-cell lineage tracing in human haematopoiesis using somatic mutations as natural barcodes, to map the real developmental hierarchy in vivo
