# Neurogenesis in Caenorhabditis elegans

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyae116](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyae116)

## Summary

This WormBook chapter reviews how a complete nervous system is built, neuron by neuron, in *Caenorhabditis elegans* — the only animal for which we know every neuron, every lineage, and (increasingly) every neuron's transcriptome. The hermaphrodite has exactly 302 neurons and the male 387. Electron-microscopy reconstruction of the entire nervous system (White et al. 1986; Cook et al. 2019) sorted the hermaphrodite's neurons into 118 anatomical classes, and single-cell RNA sequencing (Taylor et al. 2021) later converged on the same 118 classes at the molecular level while adding finer subclasses. That convergence between anatomy and transcriptome is itself an important result: neuron "type" is not an arbitrary label but a reproducible biological category.

The review is organized around two developmental steps. First, **specification**: a cell must be committed to a neuronal progenitor fate rather than a muscle or epidermal fate. Unlike vertebrates, *C. elegans* has no classical neural induction step in which signaling restricts a competent ectodermal field; instead, neuronal fate is largely assigned along the invariant embryonic lineage descending from the six founder blastomeres (AB, MS, E, C, D, P4), with proneural bHLH transcription factors and asymmetric division machinery acting at defined lineage positions. Second, **terminal differentiation**: the cell must switch on the specific battery of genes — receptors, neurotransmitter-synthesis enzymes, ion channels, adhesion molecules — that make it, say, an ASE chemosensory neuron rather than a touch receptor neuron.

The central conceptual result of the field, and the theme the authors emphasize, is the **terminal selector** model. A neuron's identity is encoded as *modules of gene batteries*, each battery under the control of a transcription factor (or factor combination) that binds a shared cis-regulatory motif across all genes in the battery and maintains their expression throughout the animal's life. Because identity is modular, and because parallel yet interconnected regulatory inputs converge on those modules, evolution and development can mix and match features: a neuron can acquire a new neurotransmitter phenotype without losing its morphology, and closely related subclasses can differ by a single regulatory module. The authors trace how cells integrate historical information along their lineage (chromatin state, transiently expressed lineage factors, Notch and Wnt signaling at specific divisions) with terminal selector activity to reach a stable final identity.

The chapter then treats **postembryonic diversification** — neurons that are added or remodeled after hatching, and identities that are modulated by time (developmental stage and aging), by genetic sex (sexually dimorphic neurons and sex-shared neurons whose properties differ between hermaphrodite and male), and by neuronal activity and experience. Finally, it argues that understanding these mechanisms in a lineage-resolved system provides a tractable entry point to the harder question of how neuronal diversity itself evolved: if identity is built from modules, then comparing module deployment across nematode species can reveal how new neuron types originate.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows what a **complete** answer to a developmental-genetics question looks like: a fixed cell number, a known lineage, a full connectome, and a single-cell transcriptome atlas — the payoff of Brenner's original argument for choosing *C. elegans*
- Introduces the **terminal selector / gene battery** concept, one of the clearest worked examples of how transcription factors organize cell-type-specific gene expression, and a natural bridge from the lecture on gene regulation to the lecture on model organisms
- Illustrates the difference between **specification** (choosing a fate) and **differentiation** (executing it), a distinction students routinely blur
- Demonstrates why an invariant lineage is a research tool, not just a curiosity: it lets you ask *when* in a cell's history a regulatory decision was made
- Relevant to the *C. elegans* model-organism lecture and to any discussion of how genotype maps to cell-level phenotype

## Key References

1. **Brenner (1974)** — *The genetics of Caenorhabditis elegans*, Genetics — Founding paper that established the worm as a genetic model precisely because a complete nervous system seemed tractable
2. **White et al. (1986)** — The structure of the nervous system of *C. elegans* ("The Mind of a Worm") — EM reconstruction defining the 118 anatomical neuron classes and the first complete connectome
3. **Cook et al. (2019)** — Whole-animal connectomes of both sexes — Extended the connectome to the male and to sex differences
4. **Taylor et al. (2021)** — CeNGEN single-cell atlas of the *C. elegans* nervous system — Molecular classification that independently recovered the 118 classes
5. **Hobert (and colleagues)** — Terminal selector framework — The regulatory-logic model on which most of this review's synthesis rests
6. **Ramón y Cajal (1889–1904)** — Histological classification of neurons — The origin of the question "how many kinds of neurons are there, and why?"

## Future Research Directions

- If neuronal identity is modular, how modular is it really? Systematically swap terminal selectors between classes and ask which features move together and which are separable
- Use cross-species single-cell atlases within *Caenorhabditis* and across Nematoda to test whether new neuron subclasses arise by duplicating and diverging existing regulatory modules
- Determine how chromatin state records lineage history — which marks are inherited through division, and how long a "memory" of an early decision persists
- Extend the activity- and sex-dependent diversification story: how much of adult neuronal identity is plastic, and does that plasticity use the same terminal selectors or a separate layer?
- Connect the transcriptome atlas to function: for each subclass-defining gene battery, what behavior breaks when it is removed?
