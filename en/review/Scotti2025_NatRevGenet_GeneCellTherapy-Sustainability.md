# Challenges and solutions to the sustainability of gene and cell therapies

## Citation (NLM)
Scotti C, Aiuti A, Naldini L. Challenges and solutions to the sustainability of gene and cell therapies. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(7):437-438. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00827-0

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00827-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00827-0)

---

## Summary

This short *Nature Reviews Genetics* Comment addresses a problem the scientific literature usually leaves to health economists: **gene and cell therapies (GCTs) work, and are nonetheless being withdrawn from the market**. The authors — including Alessandro Aiuti and Luigi Naldini of San Raffaele-Telethon, whose institute developed several of the first approved gene therapies — write from direct experience of this failure.

The scientific premise is no longer in doubt. There is substantial evidence that GCTs can radically improve the prognosis of people with previously untreatable genetic or acquired diseases: *ex vivo* haematopoietic stem cell gene therapy for severe combined immunodeficiencies and metabolic disorders, CAR-T cells for haematological malignancies, AAV-based *in vivo* therapies for inherited retinal and neuromuscular disease. Several are one-time, potentially curative treatments.

The problem is **economic sustainability**, and it bites hardest for **rare diseases** — precisely the diseases with the clearest monogenic targets and the strongest scientific case. The structural mismatch is straightforward. Development costs are those of any advanced medicine: manufacturing is complex and often patient-specific, quality control is demanding, regulatory requirements are heavy, and long-term follow-up is mandated for years after treatment. But the patient population may be a few hundred people worldwide, and a curative one-time treatment generates no recurring revenue. Health systems, meanwhile, are built to pay for chronic therapy in instalments, not for a cure delivered as a single large payment. The result has been commercially rational withdrawal of approved, effective therapies — including products for ultra-rare diseases that had been through the entire development pipeline.

The authors argue this calls for **innovative solutions** rather than acceptance. The directions they point to include non-profit and academic-led development and manufacturing (the model their own institute has used), decentralized or hospital-based production to reduce cost, regulatory adaptation proportionate to disease rarity, novel payment models such as outcome-based instalments and annuities, and public or philanthropic underwriting for conditions where no commercial market can exist.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Essential counterweight to the gene therapy lecture: after the mechanism and the clinical successes, students should know that scientific success does not guarantee patient access
- Introduces the economics of **rare disease** — the same characteristics that make a disease scientifically tractable (single gene, clear mechanism) make it commercially unviable
- Raises the structural mismatch between **one-time curative treatment** and health-system payment models, a genuinely unsolved problem
- Short and accessible, making it a good assigned reading to prompt discussion rather than a technical review
- Written by scientists who developed approved gene therapies, so the argument comes with standing rather than as outside commentary

## Future Research Directions

- Evaluate whether academic and non-profit development and manufacturing can deliver approved therapies at sustainable cost, using the existing European examples as test cases
- Develop and validate decentralized, point-of-care manufacturing to reduce per-patient cost
- Design and trial outcome-based and instalment payment models, and measure whether they actually change access
- Determine whether platform approaches — one vector, many indications — can amortize development costs across rare diseases
- Assess how regulatory requirements could be scaled to disease prevalence without compromising safety monitoring
