# Yeast as a Model System for Anticancer Drug Discovery

## Citation (NLM)
Simon JA, Bedalov A. Yeast as a model system for anticancer drug discovery. Nat Rev Cancer. 2004;4(6):481-492. doi:10.1038/nrc1372

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/nrc1372](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrc1372)

---

## Summary

This Opinion article from *Nature Reviews Cancer* argues that budding yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) deserves a larger role in anticancer drug discovery. The authors start from the fact that every cancer cell is genetically distinct from the normal cell it evolved from (missense mutations, amplifications, rearrangements, deletions and epigenetic changes), so the central challenge is to exploit those differences. Historically, low-specificity anticancer drugs came from cell-based screens using toxicity as the readout; cell-based screens have since been largely replaced by in vitro assays against predetermined targets, in part because of the belief that cell-based assays are non-specific. Yeast, as a unicellular eukaryote, straddles cell-based and organism-based screens, and it was the testbed for cDNA microarray analysis of drug action, deletion-mutant target identification, comprehensive synthetic-lethal screens and barcoded fitness profiling. It offers a 90-minute doubling time, simple cheap media, the first fully sequenced eukaryotic genome, efficient genetic manipulation and genome-wide tools. Cancer-relevant pathways are conserved, as Table 1 shows with functional homologues (ATM/ATR and RAD53, BLM/WRN and SGS1, BUB1/BUBR1 and BUB1, MLH1, BRCA1 and RAD50/RAD52, cyclin D1/E and CLN2, TOR and TOR1).

The common objection is that the yeast membrane and cell wall exclude small molecules. A large fraction of bioactive compounds do penetrate, and permeability can be raised by deleting ERG6 (ergosterol biosynthesis) and reducing efflux by deleting PDR1 and PDR3. In the NCI screen of more than 100,000 compounds, roughly 25% showed a greater than tenfold increase in growth inhibition in erg6, pdr1 and pdr3 strains relative to wild type (no quantitative analysis of each mutation was made). The article then organizes yeast screens into three types.

**Target-based screens** exploit the fact that many human proteins linked to accelerated growth in cancer arrest yeast growth when expressed; inhibiting the protein restores growth (Fig. 1). Of 38 human cDNAs of diverse classes expressed in yeast (Tugendreich et al. 2001), 12 (30%) interfered significantly with growth, including BUB1, p38, aurora-related kinase 1, PARP1 and PI3K. Growth was restored by catalytic-dead point mutations, by known inhibitors (SB 203580 for p38), or by co-expressing antagonists (PTEN for PI3K). Yeast lacks Parp activity, and the PARP1 growth defect is probably due to poly-ADP-ribosylation of essential proteins; it is rescued by co-expressing poly(ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase, and a screen found several novel submicromolar PARP1 inhibitors under evaluation. Positive-selection screens inherently exclude non-specific cytotoxic compounds, unlike negative-selection screens, which need care to prove the effect is on-target. A CDK4-overexpressing strain is rescued by INK4A or flavopiridol and can be used to find new CDK inhibitors; the other yeast CDKs (Cdc28, Pho85, Ssn8, Ctk1, Kin28) and 118 other Ser/Thr kinases act as broad specificity controls. For BCL2-family proteins, whose mechanism of cytochrome c release is unknown, yeast lacking caspases still shows BAX-induced cytochrome c release and growth arrest, rescued by BCL2, so a BAX/BCL2 strain can screen for compounds that modulate release and might promote apoptosis in BCL2-overexpressing tumours.

**Pathway-based screens** link the readout to inhibition of any component of a pathway, so the drugability of a specific target need not be known. The main example is epigenetic therapy. Epigenetic changes (CpG methylation, histone modifications) are hallmarks of cancer and cause silencing of tumour suppressors or ectopic activation of oncogenes; yeast has been instrumental in dissecting histone modification (human and yeast H3 are 90% identical; Sir2-like deacetylases and Dot1-like methyltransferases were named after yeast enzymes), and human HDAC inhibitors such as trichostatin A also act on yeast enzymes (Table 2). Sir2 is a good target because its human homologue SIRT1 deacetylates p53 (reducing its activity) and BCL6 (activating it), so Sir2 inhibitors might promote p53 activation and abrogate BCL6 oncogenic activity (Fig. 2). The screen used a *URA3* reporter near a telomere, silenced by Sir2/Sir3/Sir4, so that disrupting the pathway permits growth without uracil (Fig. 3); it is positive-selection and the reporter insertion does not weaken normal growth. Screening 6,000 NCI compounds identified splitomicin, which disrupts silencing at telomeres, mating-type loci and rDNA. Its transcriptional profile correlated highly with that of a *sir2* mutant (the approach first used for FK-506), and it inhibited Sir2 NAD+-dependent deacetylase activity in vitro; the profile also showed weaker off-target inhibition of Hst1 but not Hst2-4. Splitomicin-resistant SIR2 alleles localized the binding domain, and analogue synthesis and phenotypic screening gave derivatives selective for Sir2 or for Hst1. Preliminary cell-based and mouse-xenograft studies indicate NAD-dependent deacetylase inhibitors are promising antitumour agents (unpublished).

**Context-based screens** need neither target nor pathway; the readout is differential toxicity between cells representing tumour and normal cells, using chemical synthetic lethality: one non-lethal mutation provides the cancer-related context and a small molecule inactivates a second target. This addresses loss-of-function lesions that are hard to exploit therapeutically (mismatch repair loss in hereditary non-polyposis colon cancer, BRCA1 silencing, RB mutation). With the NCI Developmental Therapeutics Program, more than 100,000 compounds were tested against a yeast panel (erg6/pdr1/pdr3 background) in three stages: more than 70% inhibition of any of six strains (nine DNA-damage-response alterations); at least fivefold differential inhibition between most sensitive and most resistant strains; and a panel of 13 single-mutation strains. Nearly 100 compounds were bub3-specific. Loss of BUB3, a mitotic-spindle checkpoint component, causes aneuploidy, common in solid tumours; sensitivity of other checkpoint mutants (bub1, bub2, mad, bfa1, shown for compound NSC-142496 in Fig. 4) indicates the pathway rather than the single gene is targeted. These compounds lacked paclitaxel- or vinblastine-like activity, and their targets may be kinetochore, kinesin-like motor or microtubule-associated proteins known to be synthetically lethal with checkpoint loss. rad50-specific compounds included new topoisomerase I and II poisons, and smaller numbers were CLN2-overexpression-, rad53-1- and rad14-specific; results are on the NCI Yeast Anticancer Drug Screen website.

On next steps, hits need target identification (target-based screens give it directly; pathway and context screens do not, but biology and genomic methods or affinity chromatography can help, as with splitomicin and Sir2), correlation of target activity with the therapeutic phenotype, assessment of specificity and off-target effects, and safety and efficacy validation in cell and animal models. In the closing section the authors argue that pathway- and context-based screens, rarely run now, could be the most powerful for cancer, a collection of heterogeneous diseases lacking validated targets, because they can find both drugs and targets. Processes disrupted in cancer (cell-cycle progression, DNA replication and segregation, genome integrity, stress responses) are conserved, and many non-conserved human proteins perturb yeast cell-cycle progression and make suitable targets, although a unicellular eukaryote cannot model every aspect of a metazoan disease.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Shows how forward and reverse genetic logic (phenotype to target, target to phenotype) carries over into pharmacology, with yeast as the experimental platform.
- Illustrates conservation of pathways across eukaryotes and heterologous expression as a genetic assay.
- Gives clear examples of positive selection (URA3 reporter), reporter-gene design, synthetic lethality and suppression/rescue as tools for dissecting drug mechanism.
- Links gene-family evolution (Sir2/SIRT1, HDACs) to epigenetic cancer therapy.

## Future Research Directions

- Revive pathway- and context-based yeast screens, which the authors judge potentially the most powerful for finding both new drugs and new targets in heterogeneous cancers.
- Identify the molecular targets of bub3-specific and other context-specific compounds (kinetochore, motor and microtubule-associated proteins), then evaluate the homologous human proteins as anticancer targets.
- Develop more selective Sir2/SIRT1 inhibitors from splitomicin derivatives and test NAD-dependent deacetylase inhibitors in mammalian cells and xenografts.
- Pair yeast hits with genomic target-identification and specificity methods (expression profiling, deletion-mutant profiling, three-hybrid, proteomics), followed by cell and animal validation.
- Later realized in pooled barcoded chemical-genomic platforms (for example Zhou 2019).
