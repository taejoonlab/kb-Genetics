# DNA methylation in mammalian development and disease

## Citation (NLM)
Smith ZD, Hetzel S, Meissner A. DNA methylation in mammalian development and disease. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(1):7-30. doi:10.1038/s41576-024-00760-8

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-024-00760-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-024-00760-8)

## Summary

DNA methylation — the addition of a methyl group to cytosine, overwhelmingly at CpG dinucleotides in mammals — is the oldest known epigenetic mark and, until recently, the most descriptively studied and least mechanistically understood. The authors argue the field has matured past discovery and genomic cataloguing into a phase of asking *how* this chemically simple, evolutionarily ancient modification is incorporated into robust epigenetic codes that support development, ageing, and disease.

**The somatic landscape.** In mammals most of the genome is highly methylated, with methylation *depletion* marking the functionally interesting regions. **CpG islands** at promoters of housekeeping and developmental genes are typically unmethylated; distal **enhancers** show intermediate, dynamic methylation; gene bodies of actively transcribed genes are methylated; repeats and transposable elements are heavily methylated as a silencing mechanism. Methylation is thus better understood as a default state with informative exceptions than as a signal actively placed on specific targets.

**Control of the machinery.** The review examines how **DNMT** enzymes are regulated and recruited: DNMT1 with UHRF1 maintains methylation through replication by copying the parental strand pattern; DNMT3A and DNMT3B establish methylation *de novo*. Targeting is largely indirect, via chromatin — H3K4 methylation blocks DNMT3 recruitment, keeping active promoters unmethylated, while H3K36 methylation in gene bodies recruits it. Demethylation runs through the **TET** enzymes, which oxidize 5-methylcytosine toward removal. Methylation is therefore not a standalone code but is read from and written into the histone and transcription-factor landscape.

**Developmental dynamics.** Mammalian development involves two dramatic **global reprogramming** events — in the early embryo after fertilization and in primordial germ cells — during which methylation is largely erased and re-established. **Genomic imprinting** depends on marks that survive this erasure, and imprinting is the clearest case where methylation, not sequence, determines which parental allele is expressed. X-chromosome inactivation likewise depends on methylation for stable maintenance.

**Alternative, non-physiological landscapes.** The final major section covers what happens when the system fails. Cancer genomes characteristically show **global hypomethylation with focal hypermethylation of CpG island promoters**, silencing tumour suppressors. Ageing produces reproducible drift that underlies **epigenetic clocks**. Mutations in the machinery itself (*DNMT3A* in clonal haematopoiesis and leukaemia, *DNMT3B* in ICF syndrome, *TET2* in myeloid malignancy) cause disease directly. These abnormal landscapes are informative precisely because they show which features of the normal landscape are actively maintained.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- The core reference for the gene regulation and epigenetics lecture — comprehensive, current, and written by leaders of the field
- Explains **why methylation is heritable through cell division** (maintenance methyltransferase copying the parental strand), which is what makes it epigenetic in the strict sense
- Provides the mechanistic basis for **genomic imprinting** and **X inactivation**, both standard topics that are usually taught without the underlying chemistry
- Connects epigenetics to cancer, ageing, and Mendelian disease, supporting the human disease lectures
- The **CpG island** biology here is directly relevant to the dog de novo mutation paper in this vault, where CpG islands behave differently in the absence of PRDM9

## Future Research Directions

- Establish causality rather than correlation: which methylation events actually drive transcriptional change, and which are consequences of it?
- Determine what specifies *de novo* methylation targets in vivo with enough precision to predict methylation from sequence and chromatin state
- Use epigenome editing (dCas9–DNMT3A / dCas9–TET1) at scale to test the functional requirement of individual methylated regions
- Explain why ageing produces such reproducible methylation drift — is the clock a passive consequence of maintenance error, or an active program?
- Distinguish driver from passenger methylation changes in cancer, and evaluate whether reversing specific events has therapeutic effect
