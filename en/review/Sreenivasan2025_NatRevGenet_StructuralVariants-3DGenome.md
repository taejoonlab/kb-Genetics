# Structural variants in the 3D genome as drivers of disease

## Citation (NLM)
Sreenivasan VKA, Yumiceba V, Spielmann M. Structural variants in the 3D genome as drivers of disease. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(11):742-760. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00862-x

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00862-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00862-x)

---

## Summary

The genome is not a linear string inside the nucleus. It is folded into a hierarchy — chromosome territories, A/B compartments, and **topologically associating domains (TADs)**: megabase-scale regions within which sequences contact each other frequently and across whose boundaries contacts are suppressed. TAD boundaries are typically marked by CTCF binding sites and cohesin, and they act as insulators, restricting enhancers to their own domain. This architecture is what makes long-range gene regulation specific: an enhancer a megabase away can reach its correct target and not the wrong one.

**Structural variants (SVs) can break this architecture without touching a gene.** The review's core subject is the resulting **position effect** — a rearrangement leaves all coding sequences intact yet causes disease by changing which regulatory elements can reach which promoters. The authors catalogue the mechanisms: **enhancer adoption or hijacking**, where a deletion or inversion removes a boundary and lets an enhancer activate a gene it should not reach; **enhancer disconnection**, where a rearrangement separates a gene from its required enhancers; and **neo-TAD formation**, where a duplication creates a new self-contained domain containing an enhancer and a gene that were previously apart. The canonical demonstrations are limb malformations at the *EPHA4* locus and *IHH*, where deletions and duplications of TAD boundaries cause distinct malformations depending on which rewiring occurs.

The most important section for a careful reader is **context dependency**. Early work suggested that disrupting a TAD boundary reliably causes misexpression, but the picture has become more nuanced: many boundary deletions have no phenotype. Whether a boundary disruption matters depends on the specific enhancers and promoters present, their compatibility, the cell type and developmental stage in which they are active, and redundancy in the regulatory landscape. TAD boundaries are not switches; they bias contact probability, and the phenotypic consequence depends on what is on either side.

Finally, the review addresses the **clinical problem**. Most SVs found in diagnostic sequencing do not disrupt coding sequence, and there is no reliable way to predict which non-coding SVs are pathogenic. The authors review how 3D genome information — TAD maps, CTCF sites, enhancer catalogues, and predictive models — can be brought into variant interpretation, and are candid about how far that is from routine practice.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Extends the gene regulation lecture into three dimensions: enhancers, promoters, TADs, CTCF, and why nuclear architecture is functionally necessary rather than incidental
- Provides the clearest possible answer to "why does a mutation outside a gene cause disease?" — a question students always ask
- The **context dependency** section is a valuable correction to a simplified story that circulated widely; it shows how a field refines an initially overstated claim
- Directly complements the structural variation review in this vault: Collins & Talkowski catalogue SVs, this review explains one major mechanism in depth
- Clinically relevant to the interpretation of non-coding variants in undiagnosed disease

## Future Research Directions

- Develop predictive models that classify non-coding SVs as pathogenic or benign from breakpoint position, TAD architecture, and enhancer content
- Determine the rules of enhancer–promoter compatibility, which currently limit any prediction of which hijacking events will produce misexpression
- Systematically test boundary deletions across many loci to establish how often disruption has no consequence, and why
- Generate cell-type- and stage-specific 3D genome maps for the tissues relevant to developmental disorders, since existing maps are largely from cell lines
- Integrate 3D genome information into clinical variant interpretation guidelines with defined evidence criteria
