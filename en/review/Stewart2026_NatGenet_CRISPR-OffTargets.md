# Measurement and clinical interpretation of CRISPR off-targets

## Citation (NLM)
Stewart AA, Ahrens-Nicklas RC, Tsai SQ, Musunuru K, Giannikopoulos P, Clelland CD. Measurement and clinical interpretation of CRISPR off-targets. Nat Genet. 2026;58(1):20-27. doi:10.1038/s41588-025-02428-3

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02428-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02428-3)

---

## Summary

Since the 2012 work of Doudna, Charpentier and colleagues, CRISPR has moved from tool to therapy. *Ex vivo* editing reached the clinic with the first FDA approval of a CRISPR therapy for **sickle cell disease in 2023** and **β-thalassaemia in 2024**; *in vivo* genetic therapies are now in trials, including a landmark case in which a **customized CRISPR treatment was designed, tested, and administered to a neonate** with a rare genetic disease inside the window before fatal or permanent brain damage.

This Perspective makes an argument that is unusual to see stated plainly in the genomics literature: **the expectation that CRISPR therapies must have near-zero off-target edits is not a standard applied to any other class of medicine, and it is not achievable.** Clinical medicine, therapy development, and biology all demonstrate that "perfect" therapeutics do not exist. Every approved drug carries adverse effects that are accepted because they are outweighed by benefit relative to the severity of the untreated disease. Genome-related adverse events, including off-target edits, are an inherent risk of CRISPR therapies, and they should be evaluated the same way — as a risk to be characterized, bounded, and weighed, not eliminated.

The core contribution is a **practical framework for evaluating and assessing off-target safety**. Its organizing principle is that **not all genomic off-target events are equal**. An indel in intergenic sequence with no regulatory annotation is not equivalent to one in a tumour suppressor, an essential gene, a splice site, or a regulatory element controlling a dosage-sensitive gene. Location, functional consequence, the cell type edited, the fraction of cells affected, whether the edited cells are proliferative or post-mitotic, and whether an edited clone could expand all determine clinical significance. The framework accordingly asks developers and regulators to assess off-targets by **predicted clinical consequence** rather than by count.

The authors ground this in the tools available today — computational off-target prediction, unbiased genome-wide nomination assays, and targeted deep sequencing for quantification — and in tools still to be developed, and they explicitly draw lessons from **other therapeutic modalities and current clinical practice** for how genome-related adverse event profiling should be conducted. The stated goal is to streamline the transition of CRISPR therapeutics from bench to bedside without lowering the standard of safety.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Provides the clinical and regulatory counterpart to the mechanistic CRISPR material: how a genome-editing therapy is actually judged safe enough to use
- Makes the important general point that **risk assessment is comparative** — safety is judged against the untreated disease, not against perfection
- Explains why **off-target location and functional context** matter more than off-target number, which is a genuinely non-obvious point
- Directly relevant to the gene therapy lecture and to any classroom discussion of genome editing ethics
- Complements the somatic genome editing monitoring review in this vault, which covers the measurement side of the same problem

## Future Research Directions

- Develop validated criteria that link a specific off-target edit to a quantified clinical risk, rather than reporting off-target counts
- Improve computational prediction of off-target sites so that experimental nomination assays can be prioritized rather than exhaustive
- Establish how off-target assessment should differ between *ex vivo* editing (where edited cells can be characterized before infusion) and *in vivo* editing (where they cannot)
- Determine the long-term consequences of low-frequency off-target edits through extended clinical follow-up of treated patients
- Build regulatory precedent for risk–benefit thresholds proportionate to disease severity, so that ultra-rare fatal diseases are not held to the same off-target standard as mild ones
