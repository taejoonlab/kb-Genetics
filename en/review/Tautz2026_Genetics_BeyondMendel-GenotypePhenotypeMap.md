# Beyond Mendel: a call to revisit the genotype–phenotype map through new experimental paradigms

## Citation (NLM)
Tautz D, Pallares LF, Andersson L, Barghi N, Barton N, Bay R, et al. Beyond Mendel: a call to revisit the genotype-phenotype map through new experimental paradigms. Genetics. 2026;232(4):iyag024. doi:10.1093/genetics/iyag024

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyag024](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyag024)

---

## Summary

This is a position paper signed by 23 evolutionary and quantitative geneticists arguing that genetics — as a research enterprise, as a funding priority, and above all as a subject we teach — is still organized around a framework that we know to be inadequate. The framework is **Mendelism**: the assumption that genotypes map to phenotypes through simple one-gene–one-trait relationships, studied in standardized genetic backgrounds.

The historical argument is the heart of the piece. Two founders of genetics, Gregor Mendel and Francis Galton, were born in the same year, 1822. Galton was by far the better known at the time; his *Natural Inheritance* (1889) addressed the inheritance of **continuous** variation such as human height, collected systematic family data, and produced the statistical machinery — regression, correlation — that quantitative genetics still uses. Karl Pearson elaborated this "**biometric school**" mathematically from 1900 onward. But the rediscoverers of Mendel, notably Hugo de Vries and William Bateson, emphasized the **discrete, particulate** nature of inheritance, and this view largely displaced the biometric tradition. R. A. Fisher's 1918 reconciliation showed formally that many discrete Mendelian loci acting together *produce* continuous variation, and thus that the two schools were never actually in conflict. Yet, the authors argue, the reconciliation was absorbed only in theory. In practice, Mendelism — single genes, large effects, isogenic backgrounds, knockouts — became the operating paradigm of genetics research, molecular biology, and science education, while the biometric insight was quarantined into a specialist subfield.

The empirical case that this is a problem comes from genomics. Genome-wide association studies, quantitative-genetic dissection in model organisms, and large biobanks converge on the same picture: most phenotypes arise from **complex networks of many interdependent genes**, with individually small effects, pervasive epistasis, extensive pleiotropy, and strong dependence on genetic background and environment. Effects measured in one background frequently fail to replicate in another — not because the experiments were bad, but because the one-gene–one-trait abstraction does not hold. Crucially, the authors note this affects public understanding and policy too: "the gene for X" framing shapes how people think about heritability, disease risk, and human difference.

The proposal is methodological rather than merely critical. Moving beyond the one-sided paradigm requires **integrative, evolutionarily grounded experimental approaches** designed from the start to capture multigenic, context-dependent inheritance: outbred and naturally variable populations rather than isogenic lines; multiple environments rather than one standardized condition; and, above all, **large-scale automated phenotyping**, since the rate-limiting step is no longer genotyping but measuring many traits on many individuals under many conditions. The authors call for dedicated research programs — this is explicitly a funding argument as well as a scientific one.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly relevant to the earliest lectures of any genetics course: it argues that the way we *introduce* the subject (Mendel first, complexity as a footnote) has lasting distorting effects
- Restores **Galton and the biometric school** to the founding narrative, and explains Fisher's 1918 synthesis as the resolution of a genuine historical controversy rather than a technical footnote
- Gives students a defensible reason why "extensions to Mendel" — epistasis, pleiotropy, incomplete penetrance, background effects, gene–environment interaction — are not exceptions but the normal case
- Connects the Mendel lectures to the complex-traits/QTL and GWAS lectures as a single argument rather than separate units
- A rare instance of a large group of leading researchers writing explicitly about how genetics should be taught, useful for framing course-level discussion

## Future Research Directions

- Build and standardize **high-throughput automated phenotyping** platforms — the paper identifies this as the principal bottleneck
- Shift experimental design from isogenic reference strains to **outbred and wild-derived panels** measured across multiple environments, so that background and G×E effects are estimated rather than eliminated
- Develop statistical and experimental frameworks that treat epistasis and pleiotropy as parameters to be measured, not nuisances to be controlled away
- Test directly how much of the replication failure in single-gene studies is attributable to genetic background, using the same allele placed in many backgrounds
- Revise introductory genetics curricula to present Mendelian and biometric inheritance as two views of one system from the outset
