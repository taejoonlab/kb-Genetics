# Heritable polygenic editing: the next frontier in genomic medicine?

## Citation (NLM)
Visscher PM, Gyngell C, Yengo L, Savulescu J. Heritable polygenic editing: the next frontier in genomic medicine? Nature. 2025;637(8046):637-645. doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08300-4

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08300-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08300-4)

---

## Summary

Germline genome editing has been debated extensively on ethical grounds, but almost always with **monogenic** disease in mind — correcting a single causal variant in an embryo. This *Nature* Analysis, by two quantitative geneticists and two bioethicists, asks a question the literature had not addressed: what would **polygenic** germline editing actually achieve? The authors' judgement is that polygenic editing in human embryos and germ cells is likely to become technically feasible **within the next three decades**, and that no one had yet tried to predict the consequences of altering many disease-associated variants at once.

The quantitative result is deliberately provocative. Using the empirically measured effect sizes of common variants from large GWAS, the authors show that **editing a relatively small number of genomic variants could theoretically produce very large reductions in disease susceptibility** — substantial shifts in individual risk for **coronary artery disease, Alzheimer's disease, major depressive disorder, type 2 diabetes and schizophrenia**, and large changes in quantitative risk factors such as **LDL cholesterol and blood pressure**. The arithmetic follows from polygenicity itself: if liability is the sum of thousands of small effects, editing even the top few dozen large-effect common variants moves an individual far along the liability distribution.

The authors are explicit that this is a **theoretical** calculation, and much of the value of the paper lies in the assumptions it exposes. The projection assumes GWAS effect estimates are causal and transferable, that effects remain **additive** when many variants are edited together (untested at this scale, and vulnerable to epistasis), that effects are stable across environments and ancestries, and that editing has no unintended consequences. It also runs directly into **pleiotropy**: a variant that lowers one disease risk may raise another, so multiplex editing optimizes one trait while perturbing unmeasured others.

The ethical analysis sits alongside the genetics rather than after it — the authors' point is that the ethical debate has been conducted without a quantitative picture of what is at stake, and that a serious debate needs one.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Forces students to take **polygenicity seriously as a quantitative fact**: if you accept the GWAS picture, this calculation follows from it
- An exceptionally good vehicle for discussing the assumptions behind polygenic scores — **additivity, causality, pleiotropy, portability, gene–environment interaction** — because each becomes consequential
- Distinguishes **germline (heritable)** from **somatic** editing and shows why the polygenic case raises different issues from the monogenic one
- Integrates **bioethics with quantitative genetics** rather than treating them as separate discussions
- Connects the CRISPR, GWAS, polygenic score, and complex-trait material into a single argument

## Future Research Directions

- Test empirically whether polygenic effects remain additive when many variants are edited simultaneously, in cellular or model-organism systems
- Quantify the pleiotropic cost of multiplex editing: for a given set of edits, model the predicted change across all measured traits, not just the target
- Determine whether GWAS-derived effect sizes are causal at the individual variant level, since fine-mapping shows most associations are not the causal variant
- Model equity consequences, given that polygenic scores are least accurate in the populations least represented in GWAS
- Develop governance frameworks that address polygenic germline editing specifically, since existing frameworks were written with monogenic correction in mind
