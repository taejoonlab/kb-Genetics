# Guidance for estimating penetrance of monogenic disease-causing variants in population cohorts

## Citation (NLM)
Wright CF, Sharp LN, Jackson L, Murray A, Ware JS, MacArthur DG, Rehm HL, Patel KA, Weedon MN. Guidance for estimating penetrance of monogenic disease-causing variants in population cohorts. Nat Genet. 2024;56(9):1772-1779. doi:10.1038/s41588-024-01842-3

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01842-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01842-3)

## Summary

**Penetrance** is the probability that an individual carrying a pathogenic variant develops the associated disease. It is the number a clinician actually needs when counselling someone found to carry such a variant — and, this Perspective argues, it is a number the field has systematically got wrong.

Genome-wide sequencing has linked thousands of monogenic diseases to variants in more than 4,000 genes. Within these, a subset are cases where a single rare genotype in one gene causes disease. But that apparent simplicity conceals genetic and allelic heterogeneity, **incomplete penetrance**, **variable expressivity**, pleiotropy, multi-system phenotypes, and gene–environment interaction. In some cases a variant is necessary and sufficient (complete penetrance); in others, additional genetic or environmental factors are required, so some carriers never develop disease or develop a milder form. Penetrance is therefore always quantified with respect to a specific disease **and a specific age**.

The central methodological problem is **ascertainment bias**. Historically, penetrance was estimated from large pedigrees and from affected individuals and their at-risk relatives who were **clinically referred** for genetic testing — a **phenotype-first** approach. Families come to attention *because* they contain affected people. Conditioning on that fact means the carriers you count are enriched for the very outcome you are measuring, and the resulting estimates are **substantially inflated**. Many textbook penetrance figures derive from exactly this design.

The availability of whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing in very large population-based cohorts — UK Biobank, All of Us, and similar — makes a **genotype-first** approach possible: identify everyone carrying the variant regardless of clinical status, then determine how many are affected. Penetrance estimated this way is often dramatically lower than the clinical literature reports. The authors are careful that this design has its own bias in the opposite direction: population cohorts are subject to **recruitment ("healthy volunteer") bias** and often exclude the most severely affected, so genotype-first estimates can **under**estimate penetrance. Their conclusion is that genotype-first estimates are nonetheless the appropriate ones for **secondary or incidental findings** — a variant found in someone with no relevant symptoms or family history is exactly the situation the population-based estimate describes.

The bulk of the paper is practical guidance for conducting such studies: how to define and annotate variants and groups of similarly annotated variants robustly, how to define phenotypes from clinical codes and biomarkers rather than self-report, how to handle age-dependence, how to report uncertainty, and how to recognize residual biases.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Turns **incomplete penetrance** from a definition into a measurement problem, which is far more instructive than the textbook treatment
- **Ascertainment bias** is the single most transferable concept here — it explains why a great many published effect sizes are inflated across all of genetics and epidemiology
- Contrasts **phenotype-first and genotype-first** study designs explicitly, and shows that the two answer subtly different questions
- Directly relevant to the human disease and genomic medicine lectures, and to the ethics of returning incidental findings
- Complements the Kooiker dog polymyositis study in this vault, which reports quantitative penetrance for a risk allele

## Future Research Directions

- Apply genotype-first penetrance estimation systematically across the ACMG secondary-findings gene list and publish revised estimates
- Quantify and correct for healthy-volunteer bias in population cohorts, for example by linking to national health registries with complete ascertainment
- Determine what explains incomplete penetrance in specific cases — modifier variants, polygenic background, or environment — rather than treating it as unexplained
- Develop age-specific penetrance curves rather than single lifetime figures, since counselling depends on age
- Extend penetrance estimation to ancestrally diverse cohorts, since almost all current estimates derive from European-ancestry populations
