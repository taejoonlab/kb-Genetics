# Functional synonymous mutations and their evolutionary consequences

## Citation (NLM)
Zhang J, Qian W. Functional synonymous mutations and their evolutionary consequences. Nat Rev Genet. 2025;26(11):789-804. doi:10.1038/s41576-025-00850-1

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00850-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-025-00850-1)

---

## Summary

A **synonymous mutation** changes a codon without changing the encoded amino acid. Because the protein is unaltered, such mutations were long assumed to be functionally inert, and this assumption is load-bearing: synonymous sites have been used as the canonical **neutral reference** in molecular evolution — the denominator in dN/dS tests of selection, the calibration for molecular clocks, and part of the empirical foundation of the **neutral theory** itself. This Perspective by Jianzhi Zhang and Wenfeng Qian argues that recent evidence shows synonymous mutations can influence nearly every step in the expression of genetic information, and are often **strongly non-neutral**.

**Impact on gene expression.** The review works through the mechanisms systematically. Synonymous changes alter **mRNA secondary structure**, affecting stability and the accessibility of the ribosome binding region; they change **codon usage** and therefore **translation elongation speed**, which in turn affects **co-translational protein folding** — the same protein sequence can fold differently if the ribosome moves through it at a different rate; they can create or destroy **splice sites and exonic splicing enhancers**, changing the transcript itself; they alter **mRNA stability** and degradation rate; and they can disrupt **regulatory motifs and miRNA binding sites** that overlap coding sequence.

**Causes of codon usage bias.** Preferred codons are used more often than expected across genomes, and the review evaluates the competing explanations — selection for translational efficiency and accuracy, mutational bias, and GC-biased gene conversion — noting these are not mutually exclusive and their relative weight varies with effective population size.

**Fitness effects.** The strongest recent evidence comes from systematic mutagenesis experiments in which large numbers of synonymous mutations were introduced into genes and fitness measured directly. A substantial fraction had measurable fitness effects, in some studies comparable in magnitude to nonsynonymous mutations — a result that generated significant controversy about experimental design, which the authors address.

**Evolutionary consequences and disease.** If synonymous sites are not neutral, then dN/dS ratios are biased, molecular clock calibrations are affected, and inferences of positive selection may be distorted. On the medical side, synonymous variants have been implicated in human disease through splicing disruption and altered expression, yet clinical variant interpretation pipelines routinely filter them out as benign — a systematic blind spot.

## Significance in Introduction Context

- Directly challenges a simplification taught in every genetics course ("silent mutations do nothing"), which makes it exceptionally good for prompting discussion
- Explains **codon usage bias** and why the genetic code's redundancy is not evolutionarily free
- Introduces **co-translational folding** — that translation *rate* is part of protein production, not just translation *fidelity*
- Has direct consequences for how students should read **dN/dS** and neutral-theory-based tests of selection, connecting the central dogma lecture to the population genetics lecture
- The clinical point — synonymous variants filtered as benign — is a concrete, actionable limitation of current genomic medicine

## Key References

1. **Kimura (1968, 1983)** — The neutral theory of molecular evolution — The framework that treats synonymous sites as neutral
2. **Ikemura (1985)** — Codon usage and tRNA content in unicellular and multicellular organisms — Foundational codon usage bias work
3. **Sharp and Li (1987)** — The codon adaptation index — Standard quantification of codon bias
4. **Kimchi-Sarfaty et al. (2007)** — A "silent" polymorphism in MDR1 alters substrate specificity — The landmark demonstration that a synonymous change alters protein function via folding

6. **Shen et al. (2022)** — Synonymous mutations in representative yeast genes are mostly strongly non-neutral — The controversial fitness-measurement study central to the current debate
7. **Sauna and Kimchi-Sarfaty (2011)** — Understanding the contribution of synonymous mutations to human disease — The clinical genetics case

## Future Research Directions

- Resolve the controversy over synonymous mutation fitness effects with experiments designed to exclude the confounds raised against earlier studies, across multiple organisms
- Quantify how much bias non-neutral synonymous sites introduce into dN/dS and molecular clock estimates, and develop corrected estimators
- Build predictive models of which synonymous changes will be functional, based on mRNA structure, splicing, and codon context
- Integrate synonymous variants into clinical interpretation pipelines rather than filtering them, and estimate the diagnostic yield gained
- Determine how much co-translational folding effects contribute to the total functional impact of synonymous variation, relative to expression-level effects
