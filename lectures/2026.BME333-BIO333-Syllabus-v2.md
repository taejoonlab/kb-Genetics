# BME333 / BIO333 — Genetics (UNIST, 2026 Fall) · v2

## Course Objective

Students will learn how genetic information is transmitted, expressed, varied, and analyzed across scales — from single genes to whole genomes and populations — and will be able to apply genetic reasoning and experimental logic (forward and reverse genetics, model organisms, and genomic data) to real-world biological and biomedical problems.

## Course Description

An introduction to the principles of genetics, framed by evolution and illustrated with model organisms and human-genetics applications. The course moves from transmission genetics (Mendelian inheritance and its extensions; the chromosomal basis of heredity and sex determination) through molecular genetics (DNA structure, replication, the central dogma, recombination, mutation, chromosome rearrangement and ploidy, gene regulation and epigenetics), into real-world genetics (population and quantitative genetics, genome annotation and resources). It then covers experimental strategy (forward and reverse genetics, disease modeling), surveys six genetic model organisms (*E. coli*, *C. elegans*, *Drosophila*, mouse, zebrafish, dog), and closes with human genetics (cancer, developmental and metabolic disorders, GWAS, and gene therapy).

**Reference textbooks:** [Genetics: From Genes to Genomes (8e)](ref.Genetics-FromGenesToGenomes.md) · [Evolution: Making Sense of Life (4e)](ref.Evolution-MakeSenseOfLife.md)

## Lecture Details

Objective and description for each lecture; click the title to open the lecture note (learning objectives, ~60-min outline, textbook chapters, and linked vault readings).

### 01 · [Evolutionary Ideas](../en/lectures/lec01_Evolutionary-Ideas.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec01_Evolutionary-Ideas.md)
*Week 01 Mon (08-31)*

- **Objective:** Understand how evolutionary thinking and the unsolved problem of heredity set the stage for genetics, culminating in the Modern Synthesis.
- **Description:** Pre-Darwinian ideas of heredity (blending, pangenesis, use/disuse); Darwin's descent with modification by natural selection and its missing mechanism of inheritance; modern definitions of adaptation, fitness, and selection; reconciliation of Darwinian selection with Mendelian and population genetics.

### 02 · [Tree of Life, Orthology & Paralogy](../en/lectures/lec02_Tree-of-Life-Orthology-Paralogy.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec02_Tree-of-Life-Orthology-Paralogy.md)
*Week 01 Wed (09-02)*

- **Objective:** Read phylogenies and use homology concepts to reconstruct evolutionary history and infer gene function.
- **Description:** Reading and rooting trees and the tree-of-life concept (with horizontal gene transfer and LUCA caveats); homology vs. orthology vs. paralogy; tree building and bootstrap support; dN/dS and branch-site tests of selection; the molecular clock and de novo gene birth.

### 03 · [Mendel's Principles of Heredity](../en/lectures/lec03_Mendel-Principles.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec03_Mendel-Principles.md)
*Week 02 Mon (09-07)*

- **Objective:** Master the laws of segregation and independent assortment and the particulate theory of inheritance.
- **Description:** Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses (3:1, 9:3:3:1); Punnett squares and probability rules; why Mendel's experimental design was decisive; the historiography (the data-too-good debate, rediscovery); the modern molecular identity of Mendel's pea genes.

### 04 · [Extensions to Mendel's Laws](../en/lectures/lec04_Extensions-to-Mendel.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec04_Extensions-to-Mendel.md)
*Week 02 Wed (09-09)*

- **Objective:** Explain departures from simple Mendelian ratios through allelic relationships and gene interactions.
- **Description:** Molecular basis of dominance and recessivity; incomplete/co-dominance, multiple and lethal alleles; pleiotropy, penetrance, expressivity, epistasis; gene–gene and gene–environment interactions as a bridge to oligogenic inheritance.

### 05 · [Chromosomes & Sex Determination](../en/lectures/lec05_Chromosomes-Sex-Determination.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec05_Chromosomes-Sex-Determination.md)
*Week 03 Mon (09-14)*

- **Objective:** Establish the chromosome theory of heredity and survey how sex is genetically determined and how it evolves.
- **Description:** Sutton–Boveri chromosome theory and Bridges' nondisjunction proof; meiosis linked to Mendel's laws; human karyotype and aneuploidy; sex-determination systems (XY, ZW, haplodiploidy, environmental); sex-chromosome origin, degeneration, and dosage compensation.

### 06 · [Central Dogma, DNA Structure & Replication](../en/lectures/lec06_Central-Dogma-DNA-Structure-Replication.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec06_Central-Dogma-DNA-Structure-Replication.md)
*Week 03 Wed (09-16)*

- **Objective:** Connect the identity and structure of the genetic material to how it is copied and expressed.
- **Description:** Evidence that DNA is the hereditary material (Avery–MacLeod–McCarty; Hershey–Chase); the double helix and base pairing; semiconservative replication and its machinery; the central dogma (DNA→RNA→protein) and the one gene–one enzyme concept.

### 07 · [Recombination & Linkage](../en/lectures/lec07_Recombination-Linkage.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec07_Recombination-Linkage.md)
*Week 04 Mon (09-21)*

- **Objective:** Use recombination frequency to map genes and understand the molecular basis of crossing over.
- **Description:** Linkage vs. independent assortment; two- and three-point mapping and interference; the double-strand-break-repair mechanism of recombination and hotspots; linkage disequilibrium and association mapping; LOD scores in human pedigrees.

### 08 · [Mutation & Copy Number Variation](../en/lectures/lec08_Mutation-CNV.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec08_Mutation-CNV.md)
*Week 04 Wed (09-23)*

- **Objective:** Classify mutations and understand their origin, induction, and detection.
- **Description:** Molecular types and functional effects of mutation; the Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test (mutations pre-exist selection); mutagenesis as an experimental tool; the Ames test; copy-number variation and transposable elements as sources of variation and disease.

### 09 · [Rearrangement, Ploidy & Organellar Inheritance](../en/lectures/lec09_Rearrangement-Ploidy-Organellar.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec09_Rearrangement-Ploidy-Organellar.md)
*Week 05 Wed (09-30)*

- **Objective:** Predict the consequences of chromosomal rearrangements and altered ploidy, and contrast organellar with nuclear inheritance.
- **Description:** Deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations and their meiotic effects; aneuploidy from nondisjunction; auto- vs. allopolyploidy and the costs and benefits of whole-genome duplication; mitochondrial/chloroplast inheritance (maternal inheritance, heteroplasmy).

### 10 · [Gene Regulation & Epigenetics](../en/lectures/lec10_Gene-Regulation-Epigenetics.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec10_Gene-Regulation-Epigenetics.md)
*Week 06 Wed (10-07)*

- **Objective:** Contrast prokaryotic and eukaryotic gene regulation and understand epigenetic and 3D-genome control.
- **Description:** Operon logic vs. combinatorial enhancer-driven regulation; transcription factors, enhancers, and super-enhancers; 3D genome organization (TADs, chromatin loops, Hi-C); chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, and transposable-element control in cell identity and disease.

### 11 · [Real World Genetics — Variation in Population](../en/lectures/lec11_Population-Variation.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec11_Population-Variation.md)
*Week 07 Mon (10-12)*

- **Objective:** Apply Hardy–Weinberg and the forces of evolution to measure and interpret population variation.
- **Description:** The Hardy–Weinberg principle and its assumptions; how mutation, migration, drift, and selection perturb allele frequencies; effective population size; heterozygosity, runs of homozygosity, and genomic islands of divergence in real datasets (e.g., the HGDP).

### 12 · [Real World Genetics — Complex Traits & QTL](../en/lectures/lec12_Complex-Traits-QTL.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec12_Complex-Traits-QTL.md)
*Week 07 Wed (10-14)*

- **Objective:** Explain continuous variation with the polygenic model and map quantitative-trait loci.
- **Description:** The multiple-factor hypothesis reconciling Mendel with continuous variation; partitioning phenotypic variance and heritability; QTL mapping via marker–trait linkage; reading LOD-score QTL scans and the limits of QTL analysis; the bridge to human complex traits.

### 13 · [Real World Genetics — Genome Annotation & Resources](../en/lectures/lec13_Genome-Annotation-Resources.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec13_Genome-Annotation-Resources.md)
*Week 09 Mon (10-26)*

- **Objective:** Understand how genomes are sequenced, assembled, annotated, and searched.
- **Description:** Genome vs. assembly vs. annotation; short-read sequencing and read mapping; ab initio/homology/evidence-based gene annotation; sequence-similarity search (BLAST, dynamic programming, BLOSUM62); public resources (NCBI, Ensembl, UCSC) and the limits of current annotation.
- **Method box (10 min):** *From reads to a variant table.* FASTQ - BAM - VCF; what the DP / GQ / AF columns mean; why a filter threshold is a judgement, not a fact. Then, in three minutes: why long reads (PacBio, ONT), telomere-to-telomere assemblies and pangenome references are replacing a single linear reference. One figure, one worked VCF line - no new theory.

### 15 · [Reverse Genetics](../en/lectures/lec15_Reverse-Genetics.md) · [ko](../ko/lectures/lec15_Reverse-Genetics.md)
*Week 10 Mon (11-02)*

- **Method box (10 min):** *The genome-editing toolbox on one page.* Knockout vs knock-in, dCas9 (CRISPRi / CRISPRa), base editors, prime editors - what each one can and cannot change. Closing point: off-target effects are **measured**, not assumed to be zero.

## Course Format

**Weeks 1–7 (before the midterm)** are unchanged from v1: two instructor lectures per week, building the
conceptual foundation.

**Weeks 9–15 (after the midterm)** run on a two-day rhythm:

| Day | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Monday** | Instructor | The lecture as written, plus a 10-minute **Method box** at the end — one modern technique the students will meet on Wednesday. One page, one figure, no equations. |
| **Wednesday** | Students | Three teams present one recent paper each (15 min + 5 min Q&A). The instructor moderates and closes the session with a 15-minute synthesis linking the papers back to the lecture. |

The rationale: **no new lectures are added and no topic is dropped.** Modern methods enter the course
through the papers the students present, and each Monday's Method box supplies exactly the vocabulary
needed to read that week's papers — nothing more. The Method boxes accumulate into a small toolbox
(seven pages by the end of term) that students carry into their second presentation.

### The seven Method boxes

| Week | Lecture | Method box |

## Paper Presentations

### Teams and slots

- **10 teams of 3 students** (class of ~30). Teams are fixed for the semester.
- Each team presents **twice**, so every student presents twice.
- **7 sessions × 3 teams** (the last session has 2) = **20 presentations**, one paper each.
- A team's two slots are placed in different halves of the term (one in Weeks 9–12, one in Weeks 13–15),
  so the second presentation benefits from the Method boxes accumulated in between.
- Teams and paper assignments are announced in **Week 7**, before the midterm.

### Session timing (75 min)

| | |
|---|---|
| 0–20 | Team A: 15 min presentation + 5 min Q&A |
| 20–40 | Team B |
| 40–60 | Team C |
| 60–75 | Instructor synthesis: how the three papers connect to each other and to the lecture |

### Presentation template (10 slides)

Teams must use this structure. It is deliberately rigid — the point is to practise reading a paper,
not to design a talk.

1. **One sentence.** What this paper found, in one sentence a classmate can repeat.
2. **Before this paper.** What the field did not know.
3. **The question.** Stated as a question, not a topic.
4. **The experiment, in one diagram.** Draw it yourselves; do not paste the paper's schematic.
5. **The method page.** *What tool did the authors use, and why was it necessary?* Connect explicitly to
   a Method box from a Monday lecture. If the paper uses a method not covered, explain it here at the
   same level — one page, one figure.
6–8. **Three figures from the paper**, reproduced as-is. For each: what is on the axes, what the legend
   means, and what the authors conclude from it.
9. **What the authors did *not* show.** One limitation, one alternative explanation.
10. **Back to the course** + **two discussion questions** for the class.

Slide 5 is the one that matters most and is graded most heavily.

### Audience duties

Every student who is not presenting must submit **one question** on each of that session's three papers
before class (abstract-level reading is enough). Questions are the basis of the participation grade and
seed the Q&A.

## Assessment

The course grade is based **entirely on the paper presentations**. There is no written exam;
the university examination periods in Weeks 8 and 16 are not used for a course exam.

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| First presentation (Weeks 9–12) | 40% |
| Second presentation (Weeks 13–15) | 45% |
| Peer evaluation within the team, averaged over both presentations | 15% |

The second presentation is weighted more heavily: by then the students have the Method boxes,
have watched other teams, and have received a marked rubric on their first attempt. Improvement
between the two is expected and is what the weighting rewards.

Pre-session questions (see *Audience duties*) are **required**; they are not scored, but a student
who misses more than two sessions' questions loses 5 points from each presentation grade.

### Presentation rubric (100 points)

| Criterion | Points |
|---|---|
| Accuracy — the paper's claim and evidence are represented correctly | 30 |
| **Method page (slide 5)** — the technique is explained clearly and its necessity is justified | 25 |
| Figure reading (slides 6–8) — axes, legends and inference handled correctly | 20 |
| Critique (slide 9) — a real limitation, not a generic caveat | 10 |
| Delivery, timing, and Q&A | 15 |

The rubric is published with the team assignments in Week 7 and is used identically for both
presentations, so a team can see exactly where it gained or lost points the first time.

Use of AI tools to prepare the presentation is permitted and expected. Teams must include a short
appendix slide listing what they asked, what the model got wrong, and how they checked it.
